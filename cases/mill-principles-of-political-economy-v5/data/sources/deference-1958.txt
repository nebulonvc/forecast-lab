# Brains, Breeding, and Knowingness: The Politics of Meritocracy in Mid-Twentieth-Century Britain 

Alex Hill

 Alex Hill 

University College London UK

alex.hill.20@ucl.ac.uk.

Published: 16 May 2024

## Abstract

This article re-studies fifty-seven interviews with working-class voters conducted between 1958 and 1960 for Robert McKenzie and Allan Silver’s Angels in Marble: Working Class Conservatives in Urban England (1968). It uses these interview transcripts as a window into aspects of the mid-twentieth-century vernacular, specifically vernacular understandings of the social order. It argues that a powerful meritocratic ideology runs through the interviewees’ responses, articulated through the concept of ‘brains’. This discourse revered academic and professionalized forms of knowledge, arguing that they should form the currency of social mobility and the criterion of good political leadership. Hierarchies premised on class, race, and gender could be reinforced if they were perceived to reward those with ‘brains’ and criticized if they did not. However, this vernacular ideology was circumscribed by two other vernacular concepts: ‘breeding’ and ‘knowingness’. ‘Breeding’ celebrated the knowledge that came from elite experiences associated with family and upbringing. ‘Knowingness’ celebrated the knowledge that came from the experience of working-class life. These two concepts set a limit on the appeal of a vernacular meritocracy that based itself around ‘brains’.

In 1958, Robert McKenzie and Allan Silver set out to prove the ‘deference thesis’. 1 This thesis attributed ‘cross-class voting’—working-class voters turning out for the Tory Party—to a typically English deference to the aristocracy. 2 The thesis had existed in one form or another for a long time. 3 Famously, in The English Constitution (1867), Walter Bagehot had argued that ‘the mass of the English lower classes defer to the English higher class’. 4 McKenzie and Silver, two sociologists, suspected that this culture of deference still thrived in the mid-twentieth century and was keeping the Conservatives in power, so they proceeded with four phases of research. The first phase, in 1958, took the form of interviews with 604 working-class voters in seven constituencies in urban England. 5 Working-class voters were defined as ‘those living in a household where the chief wage earner was employed in a manual occupation’. 6 The second phase, running from 1958 to 1960, involved re-interviewing fifty-seven of these voters in intensive sessions lasting up to three hours. Open-ended questions were asked, and their answers tape-recorded and transcribed. McKenzie and Silver did not attempt to find fifty-seven interviewees who were representative of the working-class population in terms of age, gender, and race. Instead, they selected a range of voters with diverse political views. The third phase was a survey of voters in thirty-six marginal constituencies, and the fourth phase was a series of semi-structured interviews gauging opinions on the Profumo affair. 7 From this mass of data, McKenzie and Silver divined three types of working-class Conservative: ‘deferentials’, ‘seculars’, and ‘mixed’. 8 They concluded that deference to the aristocracy was indeed ‘a distinctive feature of working-class Conservatism’. 9 But by 1968 when their study, Angels in Marble, was finally published, the ‘deference thesis’ was no longer fashionable. 10 Labour’s election victories in 1964 and 1966 implied that the average English voter was not as enchanted by the aristocracy as previously thought. 11 Consequently, McKenzie and Silver’s findings were mostly overlooked, and their study receded into obscurity.

This article returns to the fifty-seven interview transcripts collected in Angels in Marble’ s second phase. 12 As such, it follows in the footsteps of Mike Savage, Jon Lawrence, and Florence Sutcliffe-Braithwaite by using the ‘raw materials of post-war sociology’ to explore popular politics. 13 It asks whether McKenzie and Silver’s material can be interpreted differently if approached with different concerns. Rather than sorting interviewees into ‘deferential’ and ‘secular’ groups, this article explores how interviewees discussed politics in their own words. What it finds is not simply deference to the aristocracy but reverence for ‘brains’. Interviewees, regardless of how they voted, were convinced that there were innate intellectual differences between people: those who had ‘brains’ and those who did not. They argued that these differences should determine the distribution of social rewards: those with ‘brains’ should be upwardly mobile and in positions of power. As one clerk put it, ‘there’s a lot of men today who’ve got the brains to make a good job of something, they just haven’t had that chance to have backing’. 14 Interviewees did not all agree on who exactly these people with ‘brains’ were; instead, different speakers used the concept for different ends. Some, like the clerk, used it to criticize the injustice of their lives. Others used it to confirm their satisfaction with the mid-century status quo. Regardless, many interviewees invoked ‘brains’ as part of a vernacular ideology of meritocracy which held that inequalities could be justified, and hierarchies should be deferred to, as long as they were based on intellectual capacity. 15 By mapping the meanings of ‘brains’, and measuring this discourse’s ‘throw’, this article explores how meritocracy exercised such power in the mid-twentieth-century vernacular. 16 

Historians of modern Britain have already studied meritocracy, but there has been much more focus on its intellectual development and political fate than its popular appeal. In The Two Cultures Controversy (2011), Guy Ortolano uses the public debate between C.P. Snow and F.R. Leavis to characterize the three decades after the Second World War as Britain’s ‘meritocratic moment’. 17 Snow and Leavis could not agree on much, but they both believed ‘in a society in which individuals could achieve positions commensurate with their talents’. 18 Certainly, there were dissenters from this consensus—Michael Young, for instance, who first popularized the term ‘meritocracy’ in his dystopian classic, The Rise of the Meritocracy (1958). 19 Lise Butler’s study of Young argues that his opposition to meritocracy was rooted not only in his ethical socialism but also his thinking on gender and family. 20 Still, Young’s critique was unusual: Dean Blackburn shows that meritocratic thinking prevailed among most elites. 21 On the whole, intellectual historians have not dwelt on the ideology’s broader popularity, but Ben Jackson does suggest that meritocracy ‘drew on claims about personal responsibility and rewarding excellence that held a wide appeal’. 22 When social historians have considered meritocracy, however, they have tended to argue the opposite—that ‘support for meritocracy was actually very fragile and its supposed triumph short-lived’. 23 In his history of mass education, Peter Mandler shows that many families resented Britain’s elitist meritocratic settlement, manifested in the 1944 Education Act, the era of grammar schools and secondary moderns, and systems of academic selection like the 11-plus. 24 After 1944, Britain’s ‘actually-existing-meritocracy’ continued to exclude working-class children from opportunity. 25 Upper- and middle-class parents still disproportionately promoted their children’s status, and Britain’s elite remained stubbornly privately educated. 26 Mandler, however, conflates hostility to this ‘actually-existing-meritocracy’ with broader ideological opposition to meritocracy. His analysis of survey data certainly demonstrates that parents were frustrated with the 11-plus and the limited number of grammar school places in England and Wales. 27 In Scotland, academic selection was also widely perceived to be flawed and unfair. 28 Nonetheless, close reading of McKenzie and Silver’s interview transcripts reveals that people still adopted a meritocratic logic when they spoke of social justice. Often, they criticized Britain’s ‘actually-existing-meritocracy’, but in a way that implied that a functioning meritocracy, one that rewarded ‘brains’, would be a good thing.

Interviewees’ deference to ‘brains’, however, existed in tension with other vernacular concepts. As Claire Langhamer has shown, ‘ordinary’ people in the mid-twentieth century often resisted the authority of the ‘brainy’ expert by championing the ‘authority of experience’. 29 We can see this resistance at work in the interviews. Some interviewees insisted that they wanted to be governed by those who demonstrated what Peter Bailey identified in working-class culture as ‘knowingness’. 30 That is to say, they wanted to be governed not by the brainiest but by someone who had first-hand knowledge of the struggles of ‘ordinary’ or working-class life. Another challenge to ‘braininess’ came when interviewees invoked ‘breeding’; here, interviewees demonstrated an anti-meritocratic preference for those whose authority came from their elite upbringings. So, while ‘brains’ were commonly revered, interviewees could draw on other concepts like ‘breeding’ and ‘knowingness’ that were not so meritocratic. Importantly, this article does not attempt to classify interviewees into different ideological groups—the ‘meritocrats’ and the ‘anti-meritocrats’—and count the prevalence of different ‘types’. Such an exercise would not sufficiently capture the complexity and contradictions of the interviewees’ ideas. As will be demonstrated, the same interviewee often drew on several of these discourses at different points in their testimony. Given the slipperiness and instability of interviewees’ attitudes, this article explores how past subjectivities might make meaning in ways that defy straightforward categorization. It maps the vernacular concepts that interviewees could draw on—specifically, ‘brains’, ‘breeding’ and ‘knowingness’; and, following James Hinton, it examines how individual interviewees could ‘bend, select, recombine, amend, or transform’ these concepts to furnish their own subjective take on the social order. 31 

The repurposing of sociological data in this way has been contentious. In a recent roundtable for Twentieth Century British History, the sociologist John Goldthorpe challenged historians’ reuse of his material. 32 Goldthorpe’s main complaint is methodological: he argues that historians have not been sufficiently rigorous in their approach to qualitative data. They have quoted ‘snatches of speech’ from archived interview transcripts without proving the typicality of those ‘snatches’. 33 For Goldthorpe, more explicit coding and counting of the data, perhaps with the help of software, would pave the way for disciplinary rapprochement. 34 But such a method presupposes that individuals have stable attitudes or images of society which can be accessed and assessed. Such a presupposition does not do justice to this material’s complexity, the slippery creativity with which McKenzie and Silver’s interviewees made sense of their social world. Therefore, I have approached the material through close reading, archival referencing, and attention to what Roslyn Dubler calls the ‘twin beacons of context and corroboration’. 35 My purpose, here, is not to count different social attitudes, but instead to richly describe and characterize past subjectivities, to show how they could make sense of their social worlds, not how many held a particular attitude. Where Goldthorpe’s method aims to distil the underlying attitudes of respondents and classify them using categories developed by the sociologist, my approach is to anatomize the vernacular discourses that individuals drew on and helped to forge: I therefore stay close to the language used by interviewees themselves, who talked about ‘brains’, ‘breeding’, and ‘knowing’, as well as cognate ideas and images. What this uncovers is a powerful current of meritocracy running through the vernacular, organized around the concept of ‘brains’, but supplemented and sometimes undermined by two rival concepts, ‘breeding’ and ‘knowingness’.

## ‘Brains’ and Meritocracy

During each interview, McKenzie and Silver presented their interviewees with the following hypothetical:

> Now, suppose there was a choice for Prime Minister between two men. Here is a card that tells you about each man. One is the son of the banker, A, who also owns a lot of land. He went to Oxford University. After the war, he went into politics and became leader of his party. The other man is a son of a factory worker, B. He went to Oxford on a grant. After the war, he went into politics and became the leader of his party. Now, which man do you think would make the better Prime Minister?

The question was crude, but the sociologists could use it to categorize their interviewees. Those who preferred the leader of ‘socially superior origins’, the son of the banker, were ‘deferentials’; those that insisted the two men were equally desirable, or preferred the son of the factory worker, were ‘seculars’. 36 

By that logic, Ernest Brace was a ‘deferential’. He certainly fitted the profile. The retired plasterer was a lifelong Conservative voter whose reverence for the party was matched by a deep suspicion of Labour. He picked the son of the banker as his hypothetical Prime Minister:

> Well, of course, I’m a working man, but I should certainly think the man, the son of a banker, should have more in his head to learn and study than a working man … So, therefore, I should choose the first, A, because I should think if his father was a banker, he must have brains and education … 37 

We should not read too much into Brace’s preference for the banker’s son. Many interviewees, despite McKenzie and Silver’s best intentions, were clearly influenced by party-political loyalties when they answered this question. One Tory voter, having picked the son of the banker, explained that ‘one sounds like a Conservative and the other like Labour’. When the interviewer clarified that the men were not in different parties, she re-evaluated her decision, stating she would accept either man as long as ‘they have the capability and brains’. 38 Another interviewee dismissed the factory worker’s son: ‘he sounds like a socialist to me anyway!’ 39 Clearly, interviewees’ answers were affected by partisanship. Ernest Brace had spent his life voting for Conservative candidates drawn from elite backgrounds—why stop now?

While Brace’s choice is unsurprising, his explanation is interesting. He was preoccupied by the intellect of the son of the banker, the man who had ‘more in his head’ and ‘brains and education’. We can see the same pattern in James Lansdell’s answer. Like Brace, Lansdell was a passionate Conservative; unlike Brace, he picked the son of the factory worker as his hypothetical Prime Minister:

> Well, on brains, I should say B. On account of the fact that he must have had the brains to have got to the university, even with a grant, he must have had the brains to go through ordinary school and have got to Oxford University, so the father being a factory worker or a banker doesn’t come into it. 40 

Lansdell and Brace’s answers were different, but they both drew on the concept of ‘brains’ as a justification. Both engaged with the politics of vernacular meritocracy; however, they stood at different positions on the meritocratic spectrum. Brace’s was a more elitist meritocracy, one that assumed that the class structure, by and large, already reflected the distribution of ‘brains’. Lansdell’s answer, on the other hand, gestured to a more democratic meritocracy that believed that ‘brains’ were distributed across the class structure. As interviewees engaged with vernacular meritocracy, they swung back and forth on this spectrum between elitist and democratic meritocracy, all the while celebrating ‘brains’.

Now, it might be suspected that Brace and Lansdell’s references to ‘brains’ were conditioned by the interview format and do not necessarily imply the existence of a wider vernacular ideology. After all, these interviews were ‘unusual cross-class encounters’ between an academic researcher and a working-class subject. 41 In such a context it is conceivable that Brace and Lansdell were primed to talk in this way: sat across from a ‘brainy’ researcher, they inevitably attested to the importance of ‘brains’. But analysis of other material reveals that the concept of ‘brains’ reverberated beyond the social science interview. Letters to newspapers throw up numerous examples of writers calling for help from those with ‘brains’. One unemployed man wrote to the Hull Daily Mail to complain about unemployment in his city: ‘Surely we have people in Hull with brains and initiative to tackle this problem.’ 42 This assumption that unemployment was a problem to be tackled by those with ‘brains’ was, as Blackburn shows, indicative of a broader conviction in mid-century politics: that ‘individuals who possessed an empirically informed understanding of social problems were best placed to inform political conduct’. 43 Those with an ‘empirically informed understanding’, those with ‘brains’, could also be called upon to tackle more humdrum problems than unemployment. One concerned husband complained to the Nottingham Evening Post that his wife had slipped on a ‘highly-polished hospital floor’: ‘Surely, the brains behind industry could evolve something less dangerous to walk on?’ 44 Another letter writer, from Elgin, Moray, demanded of the Picture Post that ‘the brains that beat the magnetic mine, the submarine, and the V-bomb be directed to the problem of the roads’. 45 This logic, that all problems could be solved through the proper application of ‘brains’, was reinforced by the popular BBC radio programme, The Brains Trust, in which experts discussed a variety of questions for an audience of millions. 46 During the Second World War, questions flooded into the Listeners Research Department on everything from ‘Why is Russian propaganda better than ours?’ to ‘Why do men have nipples?’ 47 Across the country, imitation ‘brains trusts’ were convened for local entertainment. 48 Clearly, Lansdell and Brace’s invocation of ‘brains’ echoed beyond the confines of the social science interview.

Still, it could be argued that there was nothing meritocratic about Brace’s explanation. It could be that his notion of ‘brains’ celebrated a certain upper-class savoir-faire, rather than intellectual ability, and that is why he favoured the son of the banker. Certainly, one interviewee seems to have elided ‘brains’ with the trappings of an elite education. Shirley Pasfield, an office cleaner, picked the son of the banker, explaining that ‘these people who have had the money and the brains and always have had it—“blue blood” as they say—they’re polite and nice as can be’. 49 Here, Pasfield conflated ‘brains’ with ‘blue blood’, implying that the criterion of good political leadership was an intellect combined with aristocratic pedigree. However, other material suggests that ‘brains’ was much more commonly used to connote innate intellect and was not considered solely the preserve of the aristocracy. Faith in its innateness could, particularly when it came to one’s own children, take on an oracular quality. In Education and the Working Class (1962), Brian Jackson and Dennis Marsden’s study of working-class, grammar-school children, one mother in Huddersfield explained that when her son was born

> he had the funniest shaped head that you can imagine. It stuck out here, like that! And I said “Oh, Dr Sykes, look at that his head. I’ve never seen one like that.” And Dr Sykes said, “I have. It’s a long time since I’ve seen one like that. Don’t you worry Mrs. Beckworth, those are brains. Those are brains pushing out, and as long as you take care of him until that head’s firm, you mark my words, something will come of this.” 50 

Jackson and Marsden may have failed to detect a note of irony in this story’s telling; nonetheless, it does show how ‘brains’ was used in the vernacular to designate innate intellectual ability. In Psychological Subjects (2006), Mathew Thomson questions the pervasiveness of psychometrics and intelligence in ‘the social consciousness’ of the period. 51 After all, psychologist Cyril Burt claimed that the term ‘intelligence’ did not circulate in everyday usage and was rarely overheard. 52 But perhaps Burt was listening out for the wrong term. A vernacular understanding of the intellect, ‘brains’, clearly circulated widely. According to Richard Hoggart in The Uses of Literacy (1957), it was one of the four admirable qualities in working-class life, along with luck, beauty, and ‘steady endeavour’. 53 

Each of these qualities could be gendered in distinct ways, and ‘brains’ was no exception. It was notably but not exclusively masculine in its application. Consider, for instance, how one interviewee narrated opportunity in post-war Britain: ‘Every man, as I say, given education and he’s got the brains, he’s got to make it, he’s bound to make it.’ 54 As Liz Heron points out, the ‘protagonists’ of post-war social mobility stories were generally men, ‘their conflicts and dilemmas assumed as masculine by their very nature’. 55 In fact, most discussions of childhood in the mid-century took the white male child as their ‘invisible norm’. 56 So, in working-class families, sons were routinely and proudly described as ‘the brains of the family’. 57 (This was despite the fact that girls consistently outperformed boys in primary school and on the 11-plus). 58 Across all of the Angels in Marble interviews, not once was the concept of ‘brains’ applied to girls or women.

Other material from the period suggests that the concept could be feminized, but then it operated differently. Girls and women could be described as having or missing ‘brains’ but this was usually in the context of an evaluation of their attractiveness—did they have beauty or ‘brains’? In Margaret Kennedy’s The Feast (1950), for example, Bruce tries to talk himself out of his infatuation with Nancibel, dismissing her as, ‘A servant, a village girl, not outstandingly pretty. She had no brains either, and but a poor education.’ 59 Where she did appear, the ‘brainy’ woman was generally a figure of fun. In P.G. Wodehouse’s The Inimitable Jeeves (1923), for example, Bertie Wooster dismisses Honoria Glossop as, ‘One of those dashed large, brainy, strenuous, dynamic girls you see so many of these days.’ 60 For women, there was generally thought something of a trade-off between beauty and ‘brains’. Irene Payne, who came from a working-class family and went to a grammar school in the 1960s, remembered worrying that ‘being regarded as intelligent would make me less attractive to boys’ so hid how many O-Levels she was taking from her friends. 61 This is attested to in various women’s memoirs of the period: the widespread assumption that while it was good for a boy to have ‘brains’, girls might have been better off with beauty. In Bad Blood (2000), her memoir of growing up in a village on the Welsh border, Lorna Sage reflected on her circumstances, pregnant at 16 due to a lack of understanding of birth control:

> you were supposed to choose between boys and books, because for girls sex was entirely preoccupying, your sex was more of you than a boy’s appendage, you were your sex, so you had to do without if you were to have enough energy, self-possession and brains left over to do anything else. 62 

Ultimately, this vernacular meritocracy, premised on ‘brains’, enshrined a particular gender order, one in which working-class boys were thought to be the vanguard of upward social mobility. Certainly, vernacular meritocracy could be adapted to contest this gendered order. For example, one woman complained in a letter to the Daily Mail that ‘even in these enlightened days, women need twice the brains and determination to succeed as do men in the same circumstances’. 63 As Caitríona Beaumont has argued the mid-century was not a ‘silent period’ between ‘first wave’ and ‘second wave’ feminism; rather, it was a period in which the women’s movement campaigned to realize women’s status as equal citizens. 64 ‘Brains’ had a part to play in this campaign; however, the gendered narrative of working-class boys with ‘brains’ exercised an unrivalled gravity.

Across the Angels in Marble archive, only one interviewee contested the idea that there were innate intellectual differences between people: ‘I don’t think it’s in the person themselves; you’ve got the same as everyone else has. This education helps to train the mind this way, so that you can do these things.’ 65 Much more common was the position of Roisin Elder, an Irish housewife living in Halifax, who thought that one’s class position was determined by some combination of ‘brains’ and education. When asked if this was fair, Elder responded: ‘I think so. I wouldn’t pay for education though: let them get it with their brains—then it’s fair … ’. 66 Elder’s distinction between ‘brains’ and education allowed her to make her case against private education. She argued that hierarchies based on the intellect were fair but paying for opportunities to develop that intellect was not. So, while not strictly egalitarian, this distinction between ‘brains’ and education was crucial in calls for a more democratic meritocracy: ‘brains’ were distributed across the class structure, but certain inequalities were denying working-class boys with ‘brains’ the opportunity to develop.

Interviewees could then draw on vernacular meritocracy to appeal to the state. While differences in ‘brains’ could not be helped, it was necessary to equalize opportunity among all with ‘brains’. As Jill Hoyt, a secretary from Coventry put it, ‘If the boy, hypothetical or otherwise, has the brains, then he should be given every opportunity’. 67 The use of the conditional here is telling: the state should extend further provision to boys if they can demonstrate they have got ‘brains’. Certainly, parents were frustrated by Britain’s ‘actually-existing-meritocracy’. 68 Under the selective education system, only a minority of students in England and Wales were allocated to the grammar schools, and only if they passed the 11-plus exam. Many parents doubted that this system of academic selection was fair. 69 But comments like Hoyt’s show that people’s belief in equality of opportunity was slippery: it could mean equal educational provision for all, but it could just as easily mean that opportunities should be reserved for those who had proved they had ‘brains’. 70 Several interviewees spoke in the conditional tense about the opportunities that boys should have. Shirley Pasfield, for instance, said that if she had a son of 15 she would ‘rather he stayed on and had a good education until he was 18 … if he was brainy’. 71 A housewife in Blackley hoped her son would work in ‘something forthcoming, like electronics, if he is clever enough’. 72 And both women advocated the further provision of university grants if a working-class child had ‘brains’. The housewife remarked that ‘the grants are limited now, and I think if you have the brains you should be able to go to College’. 73 Pasfield agreed: ‘I mean, if they’ve got the brains and pass to go to those places, I think the government should pay them a grant’. 74 Their argument that the state should extend provision on the condition of ‘brains’ was reflected in contemporary parliamentary debates on education. As Laura King has shown, these debates framed some children as ‘future leaders’ and others as ‘future workers’. 75 The state’s role was to ensure that it was the boys with ‘brains’ who became the ‘future leaders’. Put another way, the state was expected to facilitate a transition from a class hierarchy to a hierarchy of ‘brains’.

Vernacular meritocracy could also act as a challenge to racialized hierarchies, as can be seen in McKenzie and Silver’s interview with Annie Smith. Her racial identity is ambiguous in the archive, but her interviewer records her as ‘dark and exotic looking’, her ‘mother was Portugese, father English, various other mixtures in the background’. 76 Born and raised in Singapore, Smith had married a ‘typical Cockney’ and was now living in London. She was asked whether ‘people like her’ felt they had enough say in politics. She said she did not: ‘I mean, you never get “half-caste”, as people call the likes of us with different parentage, in here. It’s all Englishmen, isn’t it, running everything.’ The concept of ‘brains’ aided Smith as she criticized racist thinking:

> If he’s black, what does it matter for goodness’ sake, if you give them a proper education. I shall always say, if you take a white baby and a black baby and you give them the proper education, they’ve got the brains, they will learn. But they think because they have black skins, they’re idiots… 77 

The racist logic that Smith was challenging, which argued white supremacy was a consequence of innate intellectual superiority, descended from the scientific racism of the nineteenth century and was spread by eugenicists operating in Britain’s colonies and port cities throughout the inter-war period. 78 Certainly, this logic was far from predominant among psychologists by the mid-twentieth century, but it still proliferated in the education system. 79 Smith’s speech shows how racialized communities could resist it; references to ‘brains’ could provide the basis for anti-racist discourses. We can glimpse this dynamic in the Mass Observation Archive where one respondent, a Lieutenant in the Royal Marines explained his ‘sympathy’ for Black Americans: ‘He has the brains, as is shewn when he gets equality of education with his white brothers.’ 80 ‘Brains’, then, could be part of a larger ‘history of claim-making’, in which marginalized people laid claim to rights and recognition in Britain. 81 

European refugees also drew on vernacular meritocracy to bolster their claim to citizenship. One German refugee, a manager, explained to the Picture Post that his applications for roughly fifty administrative jobs in industry and commerce were all ignored—he suspected because of his ‘foreign name’. 82 This was a common problem for refugees with a professional background. Even if they were offered an interview, they found their qualifications went unrecognized in post-war Britain, requiring them to retrain. 83 The German refugee described this state of affairs as ‘not very encouraging to the plans to attract foreign workers and brains to this country’. 84 Another letter writer, Mrs W. Szkoda, used the concept similarly, asking, ‘What do these accommodation advertisements mean by: “English only”? Personality and brains are not considered’. 85 The fact that the concept was drawn on in such circumstances, to make claims for rights and recognition, implies that it had considerable rhetorical power.

## ‘Breeding’ and ‘Knowingness’

While ‘brains’ was a powerful concept in interviewees’ vernacular, it did not go unchallenged. Where the ‘brains’ concept valourized innate intellectual ability and academic, professionalized knowledge, other mid-century discourses prioritized the knowledge that came from personal experience. 86 Indeed, Langhamer has pointed to evidence to suggest that the ‘perceived significance of experience as a way of knowing the world’ grew during the Second World War. 87 Such a conception of knowledge could be used to attack the politics of meritocracy from multiple angles. For instance, some interviewees preferred to celebrate a political leader’s ‘breeding’ rather than their ‘brains’. Usually, this meant ignoring meritocratic sentiments in order to extol the virtues of an aristocratic background. For example, one man, a woodworker for a large engineering firm, picked the banker’s son because he came from ‘a class of people who … have had a good education, good breeding and background and … who have been diplomats for centuries’. 88 References to ‘good breeding’ clearly echoed the pervasive (if contested) aristocratic deference of nineteenth-century Britain. 89 And, as Geraint Thomas has shown, paternalist appeals on behalf of aristocratic families could still be heard in the electoral politics of the 1930s. 90 In the mid-twentieth-century, voters could still speak of their desire to be governed by those with ‘breeding’.

Others eschewed ‘brains’ in order to celebrate the benefits of having a working-class background. Roisin Elder, the Irish housewife who had criticized the system of private education, said,

> The [son of the banker] has been born with a silver spoon in his mouth. The [son of the factory worker] hasn’t. He understands working conditions; that means he understands us, the working class. He’d know about jobs, unemployment, making ends meet. He’d be better. 91 

Here, Elder focused less on the son of the factory worker’s intellectual virtues, preferring to focus on the knowledge he would have acquired from experience. Working-class cultures had long celebrated the ‘knowingness’ that came from living a working-class life. 92 Appeals to the authority of experience were common, for example, among working-class writers of the nineteenth century. 93 From the late-nineteenth century onwards, this discourse had acquired a more formal, political edge that questioned the value of aristocrats and their ‘breeding’ in favour of working-class representatives in Parliament. 94 This often subtle (and often classed) politics continued to thrive in the mid-twentieth century, when the advice of the ‘brainy’, middle-class expert was still routinely kept at ‘arm’s length’ by working-class people. 95 So, both these concepts—‘breeding’ and ‘knowingness’—could be drawn on by interviewees to circumscribe vernacular meritocracy. They sat awkwardly alongside references to ‘brains’ as interviewees struggled to make sense of their society.

‘Breeding’ proved to be a particularly awkward concept for interviewees. Celebrating a person’s aristocratic background was considered taboo, as can be seen in Olive Jarvis’s case. About halfway through her interview, she began to laugh. At first, these were brief titters, but by the end of the interview the transcriber was unable to keep up, and jotted down ‘can’t hear the rest, drowned in riotous laughter’. It was the question about the two hypothetical Prime Ministers that had set Jarvis off. She had chosen the son of the banker, explaining that he had ‘been brought up in politics’, comparing him to Churchill, and concluding that ‘he was that type of person who came from a good family … ’. The interviewer began to press her on her wording:

I: And you think the family …

O: I think it’s important.

I: And can you tell me why you think so?

O: Well … (laughter) …

I: In what way do you think it helps?

O: Well, it’s breeding, isn’t it, really? 96 

Jarvis’s laughter seems to have been triggered by her discomfort with the question. The answer, ‘breeding’, appeared to be so axiomatic but also somehow unsayable.

Several other respondents who picked the son of the banker seemed uncomfortable with their choice. One decorator, on being presented with the choice, immediately answered, ‘That’s an awkward one!’. 97 A Paddington housewife seemed similarly wary as she stated that the son of the banker would have ‘been brought up a bit better and more decent than the [son of the factory worker] … It’s a bit awkward to say … In my opinion, they’re in a better line than this factory worker’s son’. 98 Both of these interviewees recognized that they were discussing something delicate. After all, the mystique of paternalism and innate aristocratic virtue was firmly in crisis by the end of the 1950s. 99 Importantly, those who drew on this taboo notion of ‘breeding’ were not peculiar outliers in the Angels in Marble sample. Several interviewees, including those who elsewhere spoke in meritocratic terms, drew on the concept of ‘breeding’—Annie Smith, for instance, who had used ‘brains’ in a statement of anti-racism. When she was asked what determined the class structure, she replied: ‘Education—not alone, what shall we say, crudely, breeding as well … ’. 100 Much like Jarvis, Smith recognized appeals to ‘breeding’, which rubbed up against egalitarian norms, were crude but relied on the concept nonetheless.

This taboo aside, the notion of ‘breeding’ was a clear challenge to the vernacular ideology of meritocracy which called for upward mobility for the ‘brainy’ and educated. The problem, according to this logic, with those who were upwardly mobile was a tendency towards ‘big-headedness’. Ernest Brace, who believed that son of the banker had better ‘brains’, drew on ‘big-headedness’ when he explained his suspicion of the son of the factory worker:

> Because it seems to me, factory workers—they’re alright, I’m not saying anything against them, I worked in a factory myself—but a lot of them get what we call, “big-headed” … Well, out comes one of these jumped-up puppies, as we call ‘em, you know? “I’m the ticket steward ‘ere’, where’s your ticket?” 101 

For Brace, the upward mobility of the son of the factory worker actually counted against him. Brace insisted that he did not ‘like to run me own class down’, but ‘the lower-class people, if they do get up a bit, they always seem to be selfish to me’. 102 Laura Tidsdall, in A Progressive Education? (2020), finds a similar dynamic wherein clever working-class children were commonly stereotyped by their teachers as ‘cocky’ or ‘smug’. 103 The upwardly mobile were penalized for supposed ‘big-headedness’, an allegation which could take on a party-political edge. One writer to the Nottingham Evening Post described Labour as ‘Socialist “go-getters”, whose one ambition is to reach the top of the ladder, no matter whose fingers they tread on in the process’. 104 It was a common critique of the Labour Party, one given added succour by the memory of Ramsay MacDonald and his susceptibility to the ‘aristocratic embrace’; once Labour activists arrived in Parliament, they cared only for themselves. 105 These suspicions of the upwardly mobile sat incongruously alongside vernacular meritocracy.

Eric Donnan is an interesting case study for demonstrating the contradictory pulls of vernacular meritocracy and notions of aristocratic ‘breeding’. His interviewer clearly enjoyed meeting him, describing the 66-year-old mechanic as a ‘collector’s piece’, an ‘old-time, diehard socialist’. 106 When asked what decides a person’s status, Donnan replied,

> Well, education is one thing. Brains, there’s another thing that’s just come across my mind. Going back to the First World War, I think it’s true to say that you don’t really know a man unless you soldier with him. 107 

So far, so good: some combination of ‘brains’ and ‘education’ determine a person’s status. However, Donnan’s mentioning of the First World War triggered in him a memory of a Major Peacock who he served with: ‘Now, that’s what I call a man, and he was a real gentleman … what I call the “real aristocracy”’. 108 Peacock’s authenticity was, according to Donnan, proved by his ease and respect for others: ‘If he came down on his horse down the road and saw you standing there, course naturally you had to do the salute, he’d stop, might ask you a few questions’. 109 So, despite his commitment to vernacular meritocracy, and despite any socialist misgivings, Donnan held onto the notion that there was a ‘real aristocracy’, deserving of their social esteem. It is striking how Donnan gendered the concept, describing Peacock as ‘what I call a man’. The concept could be particularly useful to men who wanted to simultaneously identify and disavow hierarchy through shared notions of masculinity. 110 Donnan was not the only man to draw on this concept in the social science interview. In his study of late-1940s Bethnal Green, psychologist James H. Robb came across a man who distinguished between ‘the real aristocracy, who won their titles in the field’ and ‘the pseudo-aristocracy who bought their titles and would sell their country’. 111 In the man’s remarks it is heavily implied that this ‘pseudo-aristocracy’ was Jewish. In another study of Bethnal Green, conducted a decade later, a man complained to Raphael Samuel about working-class people guilty of ‘snobbishness’, contrasting them with ‘the genuine aristocrat, the wealthy people who accept everybody at their true worth’. 112 Invoking a ‘real’ or ‘genuine’ aristocracy, in contrast to a ‘pseudo-aristocracy’, allowed some men to skirt around the taboos of paternalism: there were aristocrats worthy of their position thanks to authentic respect for everyone. Evidently, interviewees could draw from their repertoires of older discourses developed in the nineteenth century that allowed them to creatively celebrate the aristocracy’s good ‘breeding’ and distrust the corruptible ‘bigheadedness’ of the working-class political activist. 113 

On the other hand, the critique of meritocracy offered by ‘knowingness’ tended to celebrate the working-class politician. While the concept of ‘breeding’ encouraged scepticism of upward mobility, ‘knowingness’ tended to encourage the idea of upward mobility. But rather than doing this to get the best ‘brains’ in charge, ‘knowingness’ called for leaders who could draw on their experience of ‘ordinary’ or working-class life. One housewife from Coventry offered a classic formulation of this discourse: ‘I would rather be governed by someone like myself because people who come from the same class of home are more understanding’. Her interviewer pressed her, here—what would they be more understanding of? ‘Of our way of life’, she replied. 114 This discourse was a comfortable fit for many who chose the son of the factory worker as their Prime Minister. Another housewife agreed: ‘We should have somebody at the top with a knowledge of working people’. 115 Meritocratic sentiments did not figure in these interviewees’ understandings of the ideal social order. Instead, they argued that those in the governing class should be there by virtue of their class experiences.

For several interviewees, it mattered that the son of the factory worker had ascended to the governing class the ‘hard way’. This can be seen in the way they projected their own struggles onto the hypothetical son of a factory worker. One cleaner, herself coming from a large working-class Welsh family, began to speculate on the differences between the son of the banker and the son of the factory worker:

> Well, I don’t know much difference between the two. The factory worker must have been brought up to rough it sometimes. Hasn’t always had a lot of money, and he’d know how people like us really feel. Perhaps, he’d been brought up in a big family, and come up the hard way … The other one would be a bit spoilt, always had money and wouldn’t know the value of anything. 116 

As Rebecca Goldsmith suggests, these perceptions of shared experience—growing up in a big family, for example, or hailing from the same part of the country—‘enhanced political trust, owing to the belief that politicians who understood working-class experiences would prioritize workers’ interests once in power’. 117 That leaders ‘came up the hard way’ was also important to Eric Donnan despite his comments about a ‘real aristocracy’. He picked the son of the factory worker, explaining that he would have been ‘brought up the hard way, had experience of the working class. … He would certainly make the better man’. 118 Donnan projected elements of his life onto the factory worker’s son, too, imagining that he would have gone ‘to school with other children less fortunate than himself. … When I was a boy I think half the children were running about with no shoes or socks’. 119 

This concept of ‘knowingness’ was particularly popular among Labour voters like Donnan. In their work on the Mass Observation archive, Nick Clarke et al. have suggested that it was only in the late-twentieth century that politicians came to be criticized for being ‘out of touch’ with ‘the real world’. 120 However, Lawrence’s work on the 1963 Luton by-election would suggest otherwise: the Labour Party argued at length that the Tories were ‘out of touch’ and ‘did not know enough of the way ordinary people lived’. 121 Clearly, this desire to have the difficulty of one’s life known by the governing class was an important part of mid-century political culture, and something the Labour Party could capitalize on. Indeed, it was the rise of the Labour Party itself that helped mobilize this politics. ‘Labour representation’ was a defining issue of early twentieth-century politics. 122 Labour figures like Ramsay Macdonald and Philip Snowden were particularly celebrated for their humble origins. 123 And in the second half of the twentieth century, much of the appeal of the Labour Party continued to rest on its perceived commonality with the working-class. 124 As one refuse collector put it, ‘They’re not up there, looking down on us. They’re like us: real people’. 125 

Of course, Labour always remained exposed to the charge that it did not truly ‘know’ the lives of ‘ordinary’ people. Partly, this was because of the affluent backgrounds of many prominent Labour figures. For instance, in 1950 one reader, identifying himself as an ‘ordinary working man’ complained to the Nottingham Evening Post of the ‘greed and hypocrisy’ of Attlee’s Labour Government, asking, ‘What can our £14,000 a year “Red Flag” singing Prime Minister, with his three houses, know of the misery of those thousands without homes of their own?’ 126 Over time fewer and fewer of Labour’s parliamentary candidates had working-class backgrounds. 127 On top of this, an evolving politics of race and immigration gave added weight to the argument that the Labour Party could not, or would not, understand the lives of its voters. We can see this dynamic at work in the infamous election in Smethwick in 1964: Labour’s Gordon Walker was unseated by a racist campaign led by Peter Griffiths which accused Walker of being ‘out of touch’ with the constituency’s white electorate. 128 ‘How easy to support uncontrolled immigration’, wrote Griffiths in the Smethwick Telephone, ‘when one lives in a garden suburb’. 129 (Gordon Walker was living in Hampstead Garden Suburb at the time.) Accusations of aloofness could extend to trade union leaders too. As John Benson shows, they could become victims of their own success: the more they achieved at a national level, the further they became isolated from rank-and-file members. 130 ‘Brainy’ they may have been, but their success exposed them to the charge that they did not really ‘know’ what working-class life was like.

There was a gap, then, between ‘brains’ and ‘knowingness’, different concepts that implied different expectations of politics and subtly distinct visions of the governing class. An angry exchange in the letters pages of The Cornishman in 1947 draws our attention to how wide this gap could be. S.J.H. Comer, a self-confessed ‘regular London reader’, wrote in to criticize the track record of Attlee’s Labour Government, citing statistics on housebuilding and average consumption, before concluding rhetorically: ‘Can your esteemed correspondent truthfully justify the promises made by the Labour party—are WE better off in 1947 than we were in 1919?’ 131 A week later, Comer was criticized for being ‘better acquainted with figures and statistics than with the actual conditions of the so-called “working classes”’. The author of this response, who identified himself as ‘only an uneducated ex-miner’, insisted:

> I don’t know much about figures, but I do know that the period 1924-29, under the Tory Government, was a pretty lean one as far as this particular area was concerned…. When we complained and quit we had the dole and the means test. Do you know anything about that, Mr Comer? 132 

This exchange dramatizes the different conceptions of politics that were implied by ‘brains’ and ‘knowingness’: Comer operated within a ‘brainy’ political style, while the ex-miner deployed ‘knowingness’ to great effect. At other times, however, ‘brains’ and ‘knowingness’ could be fused together. A good example of this occurred during the Second World War when the Kensington Workers’ Forum held a ‘Brains Trust’ of their own with a difference: ‘the brains were those of young working people not of academic grey beards’. The West London Observer reported that the meeting ‘benefitted from the presence of a woman who came from a Welsh mining village and who was able to show how … the miners were being given a raw deal’. 133 Here, ‘brains’ and ‘knowingness’ seem to have been yoked together: the ‘expertise’ being provided by the panel was, at least in part, a consequence of their experience. This combination was central to Harold Wilson’s political appeal too: he promised to harness the ‘best brains’ for Britain, yes; but he could also draw on a more demotic repertoire to portray himself as the ‘scholarship boy, on the way up’ but still in touch with his voters. 134 In time, voters would come to think of this as artifice, but its political appeal at first was evident.

## Conclusion

How did vernacular meritocracy come to exercise such ‘throw’ in mid-century Britain? Those who describe this period as Britain’s ‘meritocratic moment’ have tended to explain meritocracy’s hegemony with reference to Harold Perkin’s The Rise of Professional Society (1989). 135 According to Perkin, the idea of meritocracy was fostered by an emerging professional middle-class from the 1880s onwards; to bolster their status they established a ‘professional ideal’ that appealed for ‘trained expertise and selection by merit’. 136 By the mid-twentieth century, this appeal had paid off, and the professional and managerial middle-classes were at the helm of Britain’s welfare and warfare states, its extensive imperial bureaucracy, the large corporations of organized capitalism, and numerous NGOs. 137 According to Savage, the middle-classes continued to sustain their status in this period through the development of new scientific and technical identities. 138 However, Hinton has challenged Savage’s interpretation: ‘culture’, it seems, still mattered to the middle-class. 139 Anyway, as this article has shown, appeals to meritocratic logic were by no means exclusive to a status-hungry middle-class: working-class people could use the vernacular of ‘brains’ to celebrate or criticize the social order.

Vernacular meritocracy exercised such power because while Britain in the mid-century was not empirically meritocratic, it was starting to feel like it might be or ought to be. Certainly, this period was not a ‘golden age’ of social mobility. 140 The relative chances of mobility for the working-class and middle-class remained roughly the same as before. 141 Nonetheless, there was marked upward social mobility in this period at the expense of downward mobility thanks to shifts in the occupational structure. 142 This absolute upward mobility was much more resonant for working-class people than any sense of their relative mobility. 143 They could see it in their own working lives, or the working lives of their children and grandchildren. They could see it in their children and grandchildren’s growing educational attainment. 144 They could also see it in the public sphere as more and more ‘self-made men’ entered the peerage. 145 None of this diminished the powerful role that inherited capital—economic, social, and cultural—played in protecting those at the top from downward mobility, but it did draw attention away from it.

Why did ‘brains’ become the particular idiom with which vernacular meritocracy articulated itself? Partly, this was because of changes in the process of mobility. As Mandler points out, the faith in education to bring about upward social mobility was the result of the coincidence of labour-market changes with the onset of mass education. 146 That coincidence ensured that education became crucial to people’s understandings of social mobility. Labour-market changes also coincided with new regimes of social mobility. 147 Where mobility once seemed to operate based on a currency of ‘respectability’—wherein ‘respectable’ parents could make sure to ‘speak for’ their ‘respectable’ children—the gates of upward mobility were increasingly guarded by examinations, many of them group intelligence tests. 148 As Gillian Sutherland has shown in her history of mental testing, ‘a passion for formal examinations had been steadily gaining ground in England since the end of the eighteenth century’. 149 By the turn of the twentieth century, these examinations increasingly purported to measure something distinguishable from educational attainment, variously recognized as ‘genius’, ‘talent’, or ‘intelligence’. 150 Meanwhile, white-collar workers, particularly the great mass of clerks, referred to themselves as ‘brain workers’. 151 Labour Party propaganda picked up on this, presenting its programme as one that could unite ‘hand workers’ and ‘brain workers’. 152 All of this encouraged the idea that ‘brains’ were—or, at least, should be—the criterion for upward mobility.

Vernacular meritocracy’s mid-century power and contradictions were concentrated in the 11-plus. Eve Worth, in her oral history of women of the ‘welfare state generation’, has highlighted the cultural weight of the 11-plus, suggesting it functioned as a Barthian myth ‘which signifies and condenses a set of meanings beyond the exam itself’. 153 Granted, not all local authorities used intelligence tests for their 11-plus; in fact, they do not seem to have been used at all in Wales. 154 Still, where they were applied the exams’ impacts were ‘visceral’, dividing families and sibling relationships into older age. 155 The 11-plus epitomized the mixed messages of the mid-century welfare state. This was, after all, a state that told its citizens that they were each worth something, that they each mattered, and deserved a chance. 156 But it was also a state that was keen to sort and differentiate its citizens, to reformulate and reaffirm hierarchies among them. 157 ‘Brains’ proved to be a convenient vernacular concept in this context: it squared a deferential commitment to hierarchy with the ongoing spread of egalitarian norms. Put bluntly, hierarchies could be justified as long as they were premised fairly on ‘brains’.

Vernacular meritocracy has continued to develop since the mid-century. Social scientific work suggests that distributive justice is more and more commonly understood in meritocratic terms. 158 However, the vocabulary of vernacular meritocracy has transformed: mobility is less often explained in terms of ‘brains’; instead, the contemporary vernacular speaks in terms of ‘talent’, ‘effort’, ‘ambition’, and ‘hard work’. 159 Perhaps these are more market-friendly concepts whose power reflects a more market-friendly political culture. After all, those deemed to have ‘brains’ were often the left-liberal ‘armchair theorists’ of Oxbridge or public sector professionals—both enemies of Thatcherism. 160 But, more profoundly, this change in vocabulary points to a vernacular meritocracy that is markedly less deferential than it once was. 161 That mid-century desire to be governed by the ‘brainiest’ has atrophied in a political culture that is increasingly suspicious of experts’ claims to privileged, impartial, impersonal knowledge. 162 More often voters want politicians who are ‘normal’, in touch with ‘reality’ as experienced by ‘ordinary people’. 163 As the appeal of ‘brains’ has diminished, the appeal of ‘knowingness’ has grown. This is not to say that, as Michael Gove would have it, people ‘have had enough of experts’. 164 As Jennifer Crane’s history of child protection shows, expertise has increasingly grounded itself in appeals to individual experience and emotion. 165 ‘Knowingness’ has cemented itself as form of expertise. This is partly due to the cultural impact of the new social movements since the 1960s whose ‘identity politics’ has stressed the importance of shared experience as a basis for political action. 166 But it is also the result of an even longer-term triumph for working-class cultures which have insisted on the unique value of experience. 167 For now, ‘experience’ has edged out ‘brains’ as the seemingly unassailable way of knowing the world. 168
