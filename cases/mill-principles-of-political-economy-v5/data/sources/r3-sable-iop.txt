### Territorial and geographical aspects of sable extraction, harvesting and sale of its skins - from Ancient Russia to the Russian Federation

L V Shadyul 1,2,3, Y E Vashukevich 1 and E V Vashukevich 1

1Institute of Natural Resources Management - Faculty of Hunting, Irkutsk State Agrarian University named after A A Yezhevsky, Irkutsk, Russia 2 Russian Sable LLC, Irkutsk, Russia

3E-mail: liubasha82@icloud.com

Abstract. The article is devoted to territorial and geographical aspects of the fur trade formation and development in different periods of Russian history. The data of various departmental and literary sources on sable extraction volumes, starting from the 6th century AD, are analysed. Until now. The authors relying on the collected data have tried to clarify the dynamics of extraction the most valuable type of commercial fur. The information received from the largest fur fairs in Tsarist Russia, and later from international fur auctions, has been studied. It has been established that the data of various official reports and other literary sources differ to a large extent, which does not allow establishing the exact volumes of sable extraction with a high degree of reliability. It was confirmed that by the 30s of the 20th century, the sable was practically destroyed in most of the range, which required the adoption of measures to restore the species over several years. The article discusses development of the system of procurement and sale of sable skins, examples of selling prices in different historical periods are given. The dynamics of the preparation of sable skins in the Soviet and market periods is shown. The reasons for price fluctuations in the world fur market are revealed.

1. Sable extraction, preparation and sale of its skins in the pre-revolutionary period 
One of the earliest crafts in Russia is fur trade. In prehistoric times, human life was directly dependent 
on the prey of wild animals. Sculptural images and drawings from the Paleolithic times on the walls of 
grottoes, caves, and rocks testify to hunting as an ancient human occupation [1]. 
The use of hunting products as a commodity for exchange began much later. The earliest mentions 
of the skins of fur-bearing animals, used as a “currency”, are found in the time of the Scythians and 
Sarmants – tribes that inhabited the southern regions of Europe, starting from the 1st century BC [1]. 
According to B M Zhitkov, who studied the history of sable trade at the beginning of the 20th 
century, during the 18th dynasty valuable goods were imported from the East to Ancient Egypt, 
including sable skins [2]. 
In the chronicles of the 6th century of Kievan Rus, there is information about the fur trade. Various 
facts indicate many fur-bearing animals, which were hunted near Kiev. During the time of Prince 
Vladimir, a special hunting squad was created, which paid great attention to the hunting industry. The 

importance of hunting in Kievan Rus is evidenced by the facts of history that calculations were made 
with the skins of fur-bearing animals [1]. 
Bohai State in the 7th-10th centuries conducted trade with China in valuable furs, in particular 
sable. In the mentions of the Arab traveler Ibn Batuta, it is said that the Volga Bulgars exchanged their 
goods in Siberia for expensive furs. Novgorodians already in the 10th-11th centuries exported sables 
with the native population beyond the Urals, then selling them to England. The traveler of the 13th 
century Marco Polo mentions sables among the treasures of Kublai who conquered Manchuria and 
Korea. It is well known that the Greek colonies on the Black Sea traded in furs, which the northern 
merchants brought from the depths of the mainland. The explorer of the Eurasian continent Miller, 
describing the conquest of Siberia by the Russians, pointed out that in the 12th-14th centuries. The 
Ostyaks on the Irtysh River obeyed the Tatars and paid them tribute with sables, which the conquerors 
took to Bukhara. In the 15th century in Dzungaria in Altai, the local khan also collected tribute with 
sables [3]. 
Until the 15th century, fur continued to be an important object of trade and played a significant role 
in the economy of the Russian state. They paid tribute with commercial furs, gave out salaries, 
presented foreign sovereigns, their own and foreign subjects. 
In the 17th-18th centuries furs were of great value and in demand, which contributed to the 
territory expansion of the Russian state. Thanks to the sale of furs abroad, the royal treasury was 
replenished annually by 80-110 thousand rubles. 
From the 15th to the beginning of the 17th centuries the tribute (“yasak”). collection was 
introduced – a natural tax from the peoples of Siberia and the North, mainly with furs. Until the 
beginning of the 18th century the same tribute was also collected from the peoples of the Volga 
region, and at the end of the 17th – beginning of the 18th century, the inhabitants of Kamchatka were 
also covered with tribute. Natural tax was collected with sable, fox, and other furs. The commander of 
an army gave the order to sort it according to quality: “best to best, and middle to middle, and thin to 
worst”. All skins supplied to Moscow once a year had to be of very good quality [3]. 
In 1716, in Kamchatka, in addition to tribute “yasak”, a large number of furs was collected as 
“sovereign's commemoration”, “voluntary offerings” [4]. 
When the embassy was sent from Muscovy to Vienna, the Russian ambassador was given 40.360 
sables in stock. This indicates that Russia was then rich in sables and other valuable furs. Siberia 
turned out to be even richer in various fur-bearing animals, the conquest of which began at the end of 
the 16th century [5]. 
In the 16th century “a small then conquered part of Siberia gave annually up to 200 thousand sable 
furs” [5]. 
According to Grigory Kotoshikhin, in the middle of the 17th century, the “sable treasury” 
accounted for almost a whole third of the state budget [5]. 
The importance of the fur trade in the development of Siberia in the 17th century is evidenced by 
the very symbolism of its coat of arms from the letter of grant dated 1690 – two sables pierced by two 
crossing arrows and holding the “crown of Siberian kingdom” in their teeth. 
Due to growing value of furs, the trade for fur animals, especially sable, has reached a large size. 
“The disappearance of the sable was caused solely only by unreasonable hunting and fishing activities, 
which was caused from the second half of the 18th century by the Russian-American company, which 
received monopoly rights to buy furs in the Okhotsk-Kamchatka Territory” [6]. 
The increase in demand for furs led to its rapid “industrialization” throughout the territory of 
Russia. If the maximum average annual sable production fell on the 40s of the 17th century and 
equaled 145 thousand pieces, then in the 90s of the same century it fell to 42.3 thousand pieces. In just 
70 years (1621-1690), 7,248,000 sables were harvested in Siberia [7]. 
Over the next century, the sable situation continued to deteriorate. The “loss” of sable already in 
the 19th century can be judged by the data of its extraction in certain regions of Siberia. So, in the 
1860s in Gizhiginsky District of the Far East (west of Kamchatka), about 1000 sables were extracted 
per year, and in 1891 – only 500 pieces. Another region of the Far Eastern Territory – Amgunsky 

(along the Amguni River, tributary of the Amur) together with Sakhalin gave 1115 sables in 1912, and 
in 1924-1925 – only 73. 
In Yakutia it turned out an average of 4450 skins per year for 1895-1904, for 1905-1909 it was 
3000 pieces each year, and in 1913 – 1100 pieces. In 1928, the annual sable extraction in Yakutia was 
determined in only dozens of skins [8]. 
2. Sable extraction, preparation and sale of its skins in the first half of the twentieth century At the very beginning of the 20th century in Russia, according to various evidence, from 8 to 70 thousand skins were harvested per year, and the species range was scattered separate zones that remained in places that were inaccessible to hunters [9]. Due to the rapid decline in sable population throughout the Russian Empire, in 1912 the tsarist government introduced the first three-year prohibition of sable extraction and recognized the need to organize special sable natural reserves. Despite the discrepancy in the data on the number of sables harvested annually at the beginning of the 20th century, in the sources studied by us at that time, the general pattern of a sharp decrease is obvious. At that time, fairs played an important role in the trade in sable skins, among which the Irbit, Nizhny Novgorod, Yakutsk, Kyakhtinskaya, Verkhneangarskaya and Irgetskaya fairs should be singled out. Information about the fur trade before the First World War, for the most part, is given in the materials of the Central Statistical Committee (CSK), as well as in the reports of the two largest Russian fairs – Irbit and Nizhny Novgorod, where fur products were brought from all over Russia, and partly at the fairs in Obgorodsk and in Yakutsk [10]. According to the CSK 3.500 sables were harvested in 1911, in 1912 – 1.222, in 1913 – 1096, and in 1914 – 62. Due to the “difficulty of collecting”, these data on the number of animals hunted are very inaccurate. More reliable sources of information are the reports of Nizhny Novgorod and Irbit fairs. But their reports also need to be treated with caution. First, fair goods could be transported from one fair to another and, therefore, be registered several times. Secondly, the fairs got to the goods, obtained not only in the current year, but also remained unsold from previous years. So, according to V Ya Generozov at the Irbist Fair from 1906 to 1909 59.800 sables were sold, i.e. about 15.000 skins annually. During 1910-1913, a total of 52.700 skins were exhibited, or 13.175 per year. Thus, the number of harvested sables in the Obdorsk Territory was 13-15 thousand per year [11]. In parallel, in the 2nd half of the 19th - early 20th centuries in Siberia, the number of wholesale fur traders increased, with a turnover of hundreds of thousands of rubles. In 1913, the capital invested in the Siberian fur trade was estimated at 13 million rubles. The First World War also negatively affected the volume of the fur trade; the export of Russian furs decreased from 17.052 thousand rubles in 1913 to 4.072 thousand rubles in 1915 [12]. The situation with the fur trade in Yakutia, which is the main industry of the local population, also worsened every year. As a result of the merciless extermination of sable in 1913, only 2.200 Yakut sables took part in the fur trade in Russia, and in the 1930s, no more than a few dozen of them were harvested. According to V Lvov, in Yakutia at the end of the 19th century, on average, 4.5 thousand sables were harvested per year, and in 1928 the sable extraction was equal to dozens of specimens [5]. In Kamchatka, the situation with the sable fishery developed in the same way. In 1882, to protect and restore the population of this animal, special rules were issued prohibiting hunting for it, and protected areas were established. But the measures taken were not successful, and the protection of the sable on the territory was not implemented [10]. At the beginning of the twentieth century, the number of many valuable species of fur-bearing animals decreased in Kamchatka, but it was the sable that suffered the most. By 1907, the number of Kamchatka sables decreased from 9800 to 1336-2265 per year. According to the statistics report 
(1930) in the 1890s in the Gizhiginsky District, about 500 sables were harvested, and in the 1920s – 

less than a dozen per year. The famous “Ukin” sables were hunted in 1909-1910 up to 1000 
specimens, in 1921-1922 – 130, and in 1922-1923 – only 50. 
Kamchatka was the main supplier of sable for the USSR (more than 1/3 of all products). Therefore, 
the first steps of Kamchatka authorities after Sovietization were aimed at the protection and 
development of the hunting industry. Thanks to the measures taken, the depletion of the fur wealth 
was stopped. The main event for the animal protection was the work resumption of reserves and 
sanctuaries. They began to use the so-called “launches” – prohibition of hunting for a certain period 
(1-3 years). In Kamchatka, after the revolution, there were two launches on the sable, because of 
which the number of the animal increased. So, before the launch of 1924-25, 3023 sables were 
harvested, and after that, in 1927-28 – 4 344 sables. 
According to the research of A P Kurilovich in the Evenki Area, the workpiece volume was equal 
to 278 pieces in 1926, and in 1932 – 32 pieces. That is, the number of sables harvested on the territory 
of Evenkia catastrophic decreased by the 1930s [13]. 
Unfortunately, little information has been collected on sable hunting in the Baikal region at the 
beginning of the century. According to prof. E. B Patri [14], the number of sables caught on the 
territory of Tofalaria in 1912 was equal to 293 pieces, and in 1914-1915 it doubled. 
In 1927-1929 in the north of the Irkutsk Region, hunting was the dominant factor in the economic 
life of the Tungus, which was the only source of income. S Patkanov mentioned this in his research 
[15]. From the expedition of I P Kopylov on the territory of the Kirensky District (today, most of the 
Katangsky District of the Irkutsk Region), with an area of about 10 million hectares, in the season 
1928-1929, only 8 sables were received in the blanks [16]. 
The volumes of sable catch on the territory of Transbaikalia are mentioned in the data of the 
Verkhneangarsk and Irgetsk fairs. Here at the end of the 19th century, from 100 to 200 sable skins 
were sold annually. 
The data on the sable sale at the Kyakhtinsk fair for the period under review could not be found. As 
well as materials for sable procurement at the beginning of the 20th century in the Khabarovsk 
Territory and Primorye, which is presumably associated with the dispatch of furs directly to China. 
At the same time with the decrease in sable production, their prices increased. At the beginning of 
the 20th century, the price of a Barguzin sable was 300-400 rubles, and an ordinary sable – 25-100 
rubles. 
In 1919, a sable skin was already valued from 250 to 2500 rubles depending on the quality and 
territory of its extraction [17]. 
The highest price for the Barguzin sable was registered in the USA in 1925 and in London in 1928. 
In both cases, the sable was sold for 12 thousand rubles, i.e. was valued above 8 kg of gold [5]. 
3. Sable extraction, preparation and sale of its skins in the Soviet years 
The demand for commercial furs, in particular sable, began to grow sharply in the 20s of the last 
century. So, in 1921-1922 at an auction in Leipzig, foreign manufacturers highly appreciated Soviet 
fur. 
In 1926, for the first time, the question arose of creating a domestic fur auction. But in the 
implementation of this idea, some difficulties arose: the furs export was in the hands of several 
organizations that did not synchronize their activities; there was a lack of information about foreign 
markets and buyers; there were no qualified personnel. 
Since 1925, the development of the first standard for furs began, which was supplemented and 
refined in 1926. 
Meanwhile, the price of sable skins continued to rise, and the hunting became completely 
uncontrollable. This again led to the undermining of the resources of the valuable animal by the 1930s. 
The increase in competition between various joint-stock companies engaged in the procurement of 
furs led to all kinds of abuse in the acceptance of raw fur and lending to hunters. 
Procurers called for more furs to be extracted, regardless of quality. Therefore, neither the timing of 
the hunt, nor the reasonable amount of catch were observed. 

In 1930, the state established the All-Union Fur Syndicate, the main task of which was the 
centralized export of furs to the market. 
On October 24, 1931, the syndicate was reorganized into the All-Union Fur Association 
“Sojuzpushnina”, which monopolized the sale of Soviet furs on the world market. Thus, the idea of 
creating an auction was brought to life. The first auction was held in Leningrad in March 1931. 78 
representatives of 67 foreign companies from 12 countries came to the auction. At his auction, 95% of 
furs put up for sale were sold at prices that were on average 6-8% higher than the price level of 
London auction held in February of the same year. According to the price list in 1931, the cost of a 
sable hide from the Barguzin ridge, depending on the color, ranged from 60 rubles for the lightest one 
up to 500 rubles per head, which was equal to the cost of 60-450 squirrel skins. The selling price of 
high-quality skins reached up to 1000-1200 rubles. The skins of individual specimens were fabulously 
expensive [18]. 
The state monopoly on the fur’s procurement and the elimination of its private turnover ensured the 
prohibition observance of sable hunting, which contributed to the restoration of its population [19]. 
On January 1, 1935, the government of the USSR introduced a complete prohibition of production 
and sale of sable skins. Its effectiveness was ensured by the state's monopoly on furs purchase. In the 
1940s, bodies of the State Hunting Inspection were created and systematic re-climatization work 
began. 
At this time, the natural period of restoration of the sable range and its abundance began, which 
was supported by active work on acclimatization, hunting control and biotechnical measures. 
All the measures taken have yielded positive results. The number of sables has increased during the 
5-year prohibiting period, and the boundaries of the range have expanded. 
The Soviet Union became the world leader in the wild fur market. In the 1940s fur products already 
occupied the second or third place in the structure of Soviet exports. 
With an increase in the sable population, its extraction also increased. Hunters-procurers exploited 
new territories in the areas of sable re-acclimatization, mastered deep lands, and hunting intensity 
gradually increased. 
Since 1958, in comparison with the previous period, in Siberian regions, the Urals and the Far East, 
sable extraction has increased by 1.5-2 times. In the best hunting areas, the output was 20-40 or even 
more animals from every 100 km 2 of the hunting territory. 
In the late 1950s, early 1960s sable population density in the main extraction regions of the USSR 
has doubled, and their extraction in the same territory increased even by 3-4 times. It should be noted 
that the unofficial trade in fur has always existed and the data on the official sable hunting did not 
reflect the volume of the actual extraction on national basis. 
In Irkutsk Region, sable extraction, after the opening, increased faster than in other regions of the 
Soviet Union. From 1958/59 to 1961/62, an average of 8 animals were hunted from 100 km 2 inhabited 
by sable. This is 1.5 times more than in Krasnoyarsk Territory, Evenkia, Kamchatka and other regions. 
The output of sable in Irkutsk Region was second only to its output on the lands of Altai and Sayan. 
On the territory of Altai and Sayan, the lands were the most productive and hunting efficiency was 
especially high (15-18 sables per 100 km 2). 
In each of the regions, sable population varied asynchronously. For example, in the main hunting 
regions of Central Siberia, the preparation of sable skins decreased from 76 thousand in 1961/62 to 40 
thousand in 1972/73, and, accordingly, the population – from 210 to 165 thousand individuals. In 
Cisbaikalia and Transbaikalia, harvesting in the same period decreased from 71 to 28 thousand skins, 
and the population – from 205 to 98 thousand [20]. 
The sable population was variable and depended not only on the hunting intensity, but also on 
various natural factors. But thanks to the correct extraction planning, considering illegal hunting, 
population fluctuations were not great and did not threaten them. 
In 1976, the hunting industry of the USSR was carried out by 89 state industrial enterprises of 
Hunting Department, 114 cooperative industrial enterprises of Rospotrebsoyuz, 148 state farms and 30 
collective farms in the Far North [21]. The governmental procurement offices and society of hunters 

also played a significant role in the exploitation of sable stocks. In the five-year period from 1971 to 
1976 an average of 135 thousand sable skins were harvested per season. 
By the mid-1980s, considerable experience had been accumulated in organizing a commercial 
hunting economy, which made it possible, on the one hand, to keep the sable population at a high 
level, and, on the other hand, to maintain a high level of harvesting of these specimens. However, in 
the changed organizational and economic conditions, the previously used system of control measures 
has become ineffective. Nevertheless, the sable population, despite the intensive hunting, remained at 
a high level. 
To increase purchases of furs and furs raw materials, state procurement prices were significantly 
increased, and great attention was paid to the volume and quality of prepared furs. In the season 1985- 
1986 the peak of sable skins preparation was noted, amounting, according to the official data, to 256.6 
thousand pieces. In the mid-1990s, there was a slight recession, followed by an increase in demand 
and prices in the global fur market. 
4. Modern stage 
With the transition of the Russian state at the beginning of the 1990s to a market economy, great 
changes took place in the fur industry. A significant number of hunting enterprises collapsed and 
retrained into small private outlets, mainly fur-breeding ones. The state monopoly was violated, the 
official statistics of procurement did not correspond to reality. This lack of control had a negative 
impact on the ability to maintain procurement statistics. Even though in 1998-1999 fur procurements 
began to grow gradually, by 2003 they amounted to no more than 65% compared to the mid-1980s. 
The country lost its monopoly on sable, sable farms appeared in Finland, Denmark, and China. 
According to A G Klyushev, in the season 2000-01 at the auctions of the auction company 
“Sojuzpushnina” 335.4 thousand sable skins were exhibited [22]. 
In 2005, a record for the post-perestroika period was reached, the hunting rate, the production of 
sable in Russia was at least 400 thousand pieces. But already in 2006, according to the experts, more 
than 400 thousand skins of commercial sable were exhibited at all world auctions.

5. Conclusion 
Sable extraction has played a significant role in the economic basis of the state for a long time. The 
earliest mentions of fur-bearing animals used as “currency” are found in the 1st century BC. 
The decrease in the sable population, which began at the end of the 17th century, reached a critical 
level by the first half of the 20th century. By the 30s of the last century, the sable was practically 
exterminated due to uncontrolled hunting, because of which emergency government measures had to 
be taken to protect the sable, control and prohibit its hunting. 
In the period from 1900 to 1940 the data on the sable hunting and sale are rather contradictory. Due 
to the lack and discrepancy of the data on the real amount of harvested sables from various authors, it 
is difficult to derive the dynamics of the hunting volumes.

Since 1941, there has been a gradual increase in sable extraction because of an increase in its population in some parts of the range. A slight decrease in indicators in the 1960-70s due to powerful migration almost throughout the sable range. In the mid-1990s, there was a slight decline in prices for sable skins and, consequently, in sales. But, by the beginning of the 21st century, an unconditional growth in the quantity and prices of the goods sold can be traced. This was followed by a period of stabilization with a moderate decrease in the volume of workpieces. It should be borne in mind that the official data reflect only a part of the goods sold. Since the beginning of the 2000s, sable has strengthened its position in the fur market and took first place in export sales. According to the estimates of fur experts, 600-700 thousand sable skins are supplied to the world fur market annually. Despite such a commercial load on the animal’s population, its abundance is quite stable, but requires systemic monitoring.
