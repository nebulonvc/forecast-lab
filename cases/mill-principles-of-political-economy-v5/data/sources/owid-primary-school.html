A century of progress in access to primary education

Esteban Ortiz-Ospina

The world has made major progress in expanding access to education. A century ago, most children did not have the option to go to school at all. Today, access to education is widely seen as a basic right that governments are expected to provide. And most governments succeed — according to statistics compiled by UNESCO, about 9 in 10 children of primary school age are enrolled.

The chart shows how this expansion unfolded for boys and girls separately. Throughout most of the 20th century, enrollment rose steadily, but boys remained more likely to be in school than girls. It was only towards the end of the century that this gap began to close. Today, the gap is small: around 91% of boys and 89% of girls are enrolled in primary school.

While the gap is small globally, it remains large and persistent in some countries. In Chad, in Central Africa, about 80% of boys are enrolled in primary school, compared with 67% of girls. This difference has shown little change in recent years, as the data linked below shows.

    Globally, women are now more likely to enroll in higher education than men

    Esteban Ortiz-Ospina

    In the 1970s, outside of rich countries, very few people went on to higher education. Globally, it was more likely for men, but still uncommon.

    The chart shows how this changed in the decades that followed: enrollment increased for both genders, but faster for women. The gap reversed at the turn of the century, and has widened since.

    Today, two generations later, the global rates are 47% for women and 41% for men.

    Both the expansion of higher education and the reversal of the gender gap have happened across most of the world. With the exception of Sub-Saharan Africa, the rate for women is now at least as high as the rate for men in all regions.

  • November 17

    Global electricity generation has tripled since 1985 — most of this growth came from Asia

    Hannah Ritchie

    In 1985, the world generated around 10,000 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity. In 2024, it produced just over 30,000 TWh — three times as much.

    Three-quarters of this increase came from Asia. The chart shows how generation has grown, both globally and in each region.

    Yes, population growth explains some of this. But two other drivers were even stronger: more people gained access to electricity, and they consumed more of it. In the early 1990s, fewer than half of South Asians had access to basic electricity. Today, access is almost universal.

    At the same time, electricity generation per person increased by almost 450% — far faster than in any other region. Outside of Asia, it has barely changed. This is why, globally, per-person consumption has only doubled.

    This growth has given households more and better energy services, and it has also powered a huge expansion of industry.

  • November 14

    Differences in GDP per capita across sub-Saharan Africa are large

    Esteban Ortiz-Ospina

    Sub-Saharan Africa is the world’s poorest region. Its GDP per person is around $5,000 a year — less than a quarter of the global average of around $22,000.

    But looking at the region as a whole can make it easy to forget just how different the economies within it are. That’s what this chart shows, by ranking GDP per capita across sub-Saharan African countries.

    These differences are large. At the top of the chart, Gabon has a GDP per capita of about $19,000, roughly 18 times that of Burundi at the bottom. The gap between the richest and poorest countries in sub-Saharan Africa is even larger than the gap between the region and the world as a whole.

  • November 12

    How much CO2 can the world emit while limiting global temperature rise?

    Hannah Ritchie and Pablo Rosado

    In 2015, countries worldwide signed the Paris Agreement, aiming to keep the global temperature rise “well below 2°C” and to limit it to 1.5°C.

    To meet these targets, there are limits to the amount of carbon dioxide (CO2) that can be emitted. These are called carbon budgets. Every year we emit more CO2, these budgets shrink.

    In the chart, you can see estimates for how much CO2 the world can emit while staying below different levels of warming. This is based on a 50% likelihood of staying below it; if we wanted to guarantee we wouldn’t exceed these temperatures, our budget would be much smaller.

    For perspective, we’ve compared each budget with the projected amount of CO2 that the world is expected to emit in 2026. This tells us how many years we have left if emissions stay at their current levels.

    At current emission rates, the 1.5°C budget would run out around 2030. It seems implausible that global emissions will fall quickly enough to avoid this.

    The 2°C budget would last until mid-century. By taking action on climate change, we buy ourselves more time and can avoid this level of warming.

    This is based on the latest estimates from the Global Carbon Project. See how emissions are changing in your country

  • November 10

    Global CO2 emissions from fossil fuels are set to fall in 2026

    Hannah Ritchie and Pablo Rosado

    Global carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from fossil fuels are projected to fall by around 0.4% in 2026. This is based on the latest preliminary estimates from the Global Carbon Project, published today.

    This is a small reduction within an overall upward trend. Emissions have grown more slowly over the past decade than in previous decades, but they have not yet peaked or begun a consistent decline.

    This fall mostly came from a reduction in oil consumption and a small decline in gas. Coal use increased slightly.

    Among the major emitters, emissions are projected to fall in China, the United States, the European Union, and Japan. But they’re expected to increase by around 4% in India.

    Why did emissions fall? The authors cite three key factors.

    The deployment of clean energy (particularly solar and wind) displaced some fossil fuel use that would have otherwise been required to meet growing energy demand. Electric vehicles also reduced oil demand. Finally, the war in Iran and its impact on oil prices led to reduced demand, which has inadvertently reduced emissions.

    Emissions from land-use change are more uncertain but are projected to increase this year due to peat fires and forest degradation in Southeast Asia.

  • November 7

    Almost all the growth in global coal use since the late 1980s has come from China

    Max Roser

    Outside China, the world’s use of coal has barely changed since the late 1980s. It was just over 20,000 terawatt-hours back then, and almost four decades later, it is still the same.

    But the change has been very different in China. The country has increased the use of coal enormously, and today, China is burning more coal than the rest of the world combined.

    The future of global coal use, and the emissions that come with it, depends heavily on what happens in China.

  • November 5

    Population aging is pushing up Chile’s crude cancer death rate, even as age-adjusted rates are falling

    Esteban Ortiz-Ospina

    In Chile, the annual number of cancer deaths as a share of the total population has been rising for decades. From this, it would be easy to assume that cancer is getting worse. But the chances of dying from cancer at most ages are lower than they used to be. The missing piece is that the country’s population is changing quickly, and older people now make up a much larger share.

    That’s what the chart shows. The crude cancer death rate, in red, counts registered cancer deaths per 100,000 people of all ages. This metric has been going up, reflecting the fact that cancer risks increase steeply with age, and the population has grown much older.

    The blue line, by contrast, shows the age-adjusted rate. This metric removes the effect of aging by holding the age structure constant.

    This line has been falling for decades, which means that Chileans at almost every age are now less likely to die from cancer than people of the same age were in the past.

    The pattern we see in Chile is happening in many other countries, too, and is visible in the global estimates.
