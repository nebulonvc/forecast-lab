The brief 
The poten*al for co-opera*sa*on to act as a vehicle for economic development in Wales has long 
been vaunted, with proponents drawing, for instance, on the example of Mondragon in the Basque 
Country (see, e.g., Osmond 2012). But despite decades of studies, recommenda*ons, and proposals, 
co-opera*sa*on at scale has arguably never been delivered. According to Co-opera*ves UK data 
(2023), co-opera*ves and mutuals currently account for around 0.6% of total Welsh GDP. 
Including in the context of Brexit, the journey to net zero, the current cost-of-living and cost-of-doing 
business crises, and a changing post-Covid geopoli*cs, Welsh industry and the wider Welsh economy 
is facing new and renewed challenges, to which co-opera*sa*on may offer some solu*ons. 
Co-opera*sa*on and related business models (e.g. community ownership, employee buy-outs) may 
also provide part of the solu*on to a longstanding issue in the Welsh economy – that of ownership 
churn and the ‘missing middle’ (FSB 2017).

Why are Co-ops important? 
Whilst co-ops are commercial businesses, they are structured in such a way as to have a mission that 
takes precedence over the search for profit. To be recognised as such they must adhere to the seven 
co-op principles as defined by the Interna*onal Co-opera*ve Alliance (ICA) 1:- 
1. Voluntary and open membership 
2. Democra*c Member Control 
3. Member Economic Par*cipa*on 
4. Autonomy and Independence 
5. Educa*on, Training, and Informa*on 
6. Co-opera*on among Co-opera*ves 
7. Concern for Community 
These are an updated version of the original principles laid down by the Rochdale Pioneers in 1844 2 
and have not changed substan*ally. 
Unlike ordinary joint stock (share) companies, co-ops have their capital anchored. They are un-traded 
en**es with par-value shares, i.e. their shares remain at £1 however well the business is doing. Any 
retained surpluses remain within the co-op for future growth and investment, and they are thus 
isolated from the con*nuous varia*on of the stock market. 
The stock market has changed over the last half century and is now much more unstable as a result. 
In the 1980s the London Stock Exchange 3 changed its rules on share trading. Under the old rules 
shares could only be bought and sold through a Stockbroker, who in turn instructed a Floor Trader to 
buy or sell on your behalf, and any shares bought could not be re-sold un*l the cer*ficate was in the 
Broker’s hands. Thus, all investors had to keep any shares they bought for a minimum of 3 days. Now 
shares can be traded directly on the Internet, and so can be bought and sold in seconds. So called 
day-traders even trade shares automa*cally using algorithms. This level of automa*on has also led to 
people trading in “Futures” and “Op*ons”. These are not real things, and trading in them is really a 
form of gambling, gambling with people’s livelihoods.

#### Conclusions

It is important to remember that correla*on is not the same as causa*on, so we need to look 
carefully at the results of co-op development elsewhere and in the past in Wales and the UK to try to 
tease out what works and what doesn’t. 
LegislaGon 
Looking first at India, it is obvious that a vast amount of legisla*on is not, of itself, the answer, 
although looking at the Marcora law in Italy it is clear it can be incredibly useful. The law can be a 
hindrance, or it can open up possibili*es, but it can’t do the job by itself. What is needed is a 
combina*on of factors covering culture, knowledge, appropriate legal structures, and suitable 
finance. 
Localism 
The history of co-op development in the UK shows that a plethora of local CDAs produced results. 
The same also applies to economic development in general, although a co-ordina*ng role at na*onal 
level is useful as well. The local knowledge, easy availability, links to Local Authori*es and other 
economic development partners can ensure that sensible advice is available to people thinking of 
star*ng a co-op. When all these factors were in place in the 1980s, and there was a concentra*on on 
the crea*on of worker co-ops, their number rose to around 1,500 across the UK compared to today’s 
figure of only 400. 
Anecdotal experience indicates that many poten*al co-operators are s*ll put off by business advisors 
who do not understand the co-opera*ve model, and if they are the first point of contact, then the 
co-op op*on is not considered. Amongst the professions, lawyers, accountants, and mainstream 
business advisers, co-ops are regarded as an op*on only for a minority of people and are anyway not 
‘proper businesses’ and designed to fail. These same people will of course shop at John Lewis or 
Waitrose but not put two and two together.
