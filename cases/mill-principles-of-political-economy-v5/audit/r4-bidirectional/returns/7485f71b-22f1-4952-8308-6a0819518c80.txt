#### Introduction Class, Politics, and the Decline of Deference in England, 1968–2000

Class and the English have a peculiar and distinctive relationship: the first industrial nation, it was in England where languages of class grew ‘earliest and most exten sively’, replacing the language of ‘ranks’, ‘orders’, and ‘degrees’ over the course of the nineteenth century and becoming central to politics and social distinction.¹ In the late twentieth century, however, multiple arguments surfaced—from sociolo gists, political scientists, politicians, and others—claiming that the significance of class had declined.² This book tests those claims from one particular angle: it examines popular and political discourses of class in the period from 1968 to 2000. It concludes that class remained a defining theme in English culture and politics in these years. However, if we examine what ‘ordinary’ people meant when they talked about class, we find that this changed significantly in this period. Moreover, vernacular languages of class did not change in response to changing political languages of class under Thatcher and New Labour: the vernacular had its own underlying trajectories of change. Crucial to the redefinition of languages of class was the decline of deference. As society grew less deferential, and in some ways more individualistic, fewer and fewer people were willing to accept or defer to class hierarchies. What had once seemed fixed and enduring became historical and open to question and critique. Importantly, though, these shifts in vernacular languages of class had political implications: they chimed with the new ways in which Thatcher, and later Blair, attempted to appeal to the changing electorate. and identities in this period. One common narrative emphasizes the impact of 
Thatcher, arguing that Thatcherism wrote ‘class’ out of politics—and culture more

This is the case even in the important recent work by sociologists Geoffrey Evans and James Tilley, The New Politics of Class, which suggests that class divisions and class identities and attitudes have seen no great rupture in the past half-century (though the relative size of different classes has changed). Evans and Tilley argue that what has changed is the Labour Party: in becoming a ‘middle-class’ party it has left the working classes without meaningful political representation, leading to a rise in non-voting, and SNP and UKIP voting among the working classes. Their historical narrative is based, however, on largely quantitative evidence, which cannot give a full picture of the meanings people ascribed to class categories. Though their analysis of political change on the left is important, we need to understand popular identities and discourses of class in more qualitative ways if we are to understand how they have changed. A third narrative often assigned to late twentieth-century England is the ‘rise of individualism’.¹⁹ This is often lamented, though it has sometimes been cele brated.²⁰ But whether it is praised or condemned, the individualistic society is generally seen as a society where mass formations have broken down; an atomized or fragmented society; a society where classes and class identities are less important. Here, social and cultural change is again not seen as dependent on politics; rather, the rise of individualism is driven by economic change: the long post-war period of affluence and relative economic security is seen to have led to the rise of individu alism and ‘post-materialistic’ politics of gender, race, environmentalism, and other such issues.²¹ Another variant of this narrative stresses the fragmentation and decreasing cohesiveness of classes, suggesting that increasingly widespread afflu ence, education, and social mobility have, in the post-war period, given rise to far larger numbers of people with mixed class characteristics and confused class identities.²² This has led to the ‘individualization’ of political interests, just at the same moment that politics began to institutionalize individualism and lose its focus on the collective.²³ These narratives are all probably well-known to those who study late twentieth century England; each echoes arguments made at the time about class and indi vidualism. In 2015, Mike Savage and a team of sociologists published The Great British 
Class Survey, a Bourdieusian study of the class groupings and identities in Britain in 
the 2010s.²⁶ One of the study’s key findings was that Britain was fragmented into 
seven class groupings. At the top and bottom were small, distinctive, and polarized 
groups: the wealth elite and the precariat. In the middle were five groups, but these, 
importantly, had more blurred and messy boundaries. The older, sharper dividing 
lines between upper, middle, and working classes were gone. In addition, Savage 
et al. found that class itself, as a concept, had ‘gone underground’.²⁷ People still 
noted and acted on class distinctions made on subtle grounds like dress or accent. 
But they were, by and large, extremely averse to admitting this, and condemned 
class language and class snobberies. In 1968, I show, class was not ‘underground’, 
though many working-class men and some younger middle-class people rejected 
class snobbery. By 2000, class was very definitely ‘underground’. This study 
provides a historical explanation for how this state of affairs came about. Its argu

People were familiar with the language of class and placed themselves biographically using class terms. But there were several important shifts in how people talked about class. Social changes—the expansion of white-collar and service sector work, the rise of consumerism, homeownership and education— meant that many found it difficult to sharply delineate a ‘middle’ and ‘working’ class even in the 1970s; this was even more true in 2000. Many felt the real distinctions were between a large group of ‘ordinary, working’ people and two marginal groups: the workless at the bottom and privileged elites at the top. In addition, women’s roles changed dramatically in the post-war period, with new education and job opportunities, and particularly from the late 1960s, with the challenge of Women’s Liberation, and the widespread availability of new forms of contraception.²⁸ This had complex implications for the class structure and unset tled traditional images of class. Mass migration from the new commonwealth in the post-war period also significantly changed the ethnic composition of society,²⁹ and this seemed to many to further complicate and cut across class lines. ‘The working class’ had long been a highly (if implicitly) racialized and gendered category, and changes in these areas thus had unsettling implications for class. By 2000 ‘working class’ and ‘middle class’ had become reified cultural stereo types which relatively few people felt that they straightforwardly fitted. In the popular imagination, the Second World War was generally seen as a dramatic break: before, a class-divided world of ‘Upstairs, Downstairs’; afterwards, a more recognizably modern society where the boundary between middle and upper-class blurred and the aristocracy was dramatically reduced in status. This mythologized view had some truth in it (live-in domestic service did practically disappear after the war; the landed aristocracy was brought low by the decline in the returns on land), though it was doubtless encouraged by the heritage boom, TV shows like Upstairs, Downstairs, and the explosion of social history from the 1960s on.³⁰ True or false, it was a pervasive view of history, with important implications for the present. Many, however, also emphasized pride in their roots, so that being ‘working class’ was often celebrated as a cultural heritage or regional identity, even as people felt that they had moved away from ‘working-class’ communities and lifestyles. Instead of feeling that the categories ‘working class’ and ‘middle class’ defined their lives now, most people saw themselves as part of a large group of ‘ordinary’ people in the ‘middle’ of society. A second key change this book identifies is a shift in the nature of white-collar 
workers’ identities and values. At the start of the period, many who saw themselves

those ‘beneath’ them. By 2000, aspirations towards a socially egalitarian outlook 
(always common among working-class men) were widely shared, if not always 
entirely realized, among women, the middle classes, and even upper-class people. 
This was how class ‘went underground’. Authenticity, individuality, and ordinari

professions of a socially egalitarian outlook often failed to translate into reality).

middle-class jobs and lifestyles, forging a far more disparate ‘middle class’. But the 
increasing focus on authenticity, individuality, and ordinariness was also the result 
of a cultural shift: the decline of deference. Changes in class talk thus offer a useful 
lens through which to examine this important change. Looking at how people 
talked about class enables us to track the extent and the consequences of the decline 
of deference. It is a shift that has been much commented on, but which has received 
little sustained historical attention.³⁴

It is this decreasing linkage between ‘class identities’ and politics that is the more significant shift to understand. From some perspectives—though only some—England is still, today, a country where a large number of people feel some affinity to the label ‘working class’. This can be the case without much connection being made between working-class identity and political action, though. In fact, rather than focusing on trying to understand the shifting balance of ‘class identities’ (a difficult task given the multiple and context specific identities we are all capable of having), it is more useful to read the late twentieth century through the lens of the decline of deference. A brief statement of what I mean by ‘deference’ is needed here. In this study, I use the concept to refer to the tendency to defer to authority, convention, and tradition in an array of areas— political, social, cultural, and personal. It is important to note that deference to authority may be a performance made out of necessity,³⁵ rather than a deeply held conviction of the rightness of deference to authority. But even if it is an unstable performance, it is significant. This study tracks the causes, timing, and conse quences of the decline of deference by paying attention to the attitudes people voiced when asked about ‘class’. The decline of deference made people more individualistic in some ways, though this did not equate to a selfish, greedy, materialistic, anti-collectivist orientation. People were more keen to assert their right to choose as individuals, but also keen to emphasize authenticity and ordin ariness as opposed to privilege and snobbery. Unpicking the causation of such a complex cultural shift will always be a contested subject. In fact, explaining its causes fully lies outside the scope of this study, which uses languages of class to track the decline of deference and its implications for identities and politics. But I would suggest that if we are searching for the causes of the decline of deference, we need to examine economic, social, and cultural shifts. None of these can be separated out as ultimately causative. The roots of the decline of deference and rise of individualism go back many centuries, and scholars have rightly pointed to the significance of the Reformation, the Enlightenment, and the urbanization, industrialization, and trade union organiza tion of the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries as important factors lying behind this shift in subjectivities.³⁶ But deference declined more quickly than ever after the Second World War, and by the late 1970s the evidence was so pervasive that Ralph Miliband described Britain as being in a ‘state of de-subordination’.³⁷

The decline of deference did not mean the death of discourses of class, but it did profoundly reshape people’s attitudes to their own identities, to tradition, to elites, and to the social changes they had lived through. In 1969, a shipbuilder might comment on voting Labour, ‘[y]ou’ve got to being a working class person’.⁵⁰ By the end of the period people linked class to politics less instinctively, and emphasized making up their own minds more. There was more scepticism towards elites. ‘Working class’, redefined as a cultural heritage, had less obvious political valence, posing problems for Labour. This book goes further, then, than simply tracking continuity and change in vernacular languages of class in the late twentieth century; it examines how these mapped onto politics. It charts how Thatcher and New Labour understood changing political and social identities, and how they attempted to yoke these to their political projects. Thatcher stumbled—more by chance than design—on rhetoric which had many echoes in what ordinary people were saying about class: she railed against old fashioned elites’ power and against petty bureaucracy, and stressed individual freedom. Thatcher had to try to get away from class language in the late 1970s because she was so dangerously stamped with a narrow, upper-middle-class, pearls and-twinsets image. Her particular ideas about society came in large part from her Methodist upbringing and the anti-class railings of neoliberal think-tanks. She happened to hit on a message which chimed in some ways with growing sentiments in the electorate. But that synergy had limits. By contrast, New Labour developed a language of social description emphasizing the growth of a ‘new middle class’ united by hard work and aspiration, which chimed with the growing sentiments of much of English society. The Labour modernizers achieved this in part because they used innovative techniques like focus groups to listen to what people were saying about society and politics. To acknowledge this is not to offer a sweeping endorsement of the New Labour years. New Labour successfully worked with the grain of social and cultural shifts to bring about Labour victory and reshape the economy and society. In the end, the architects of New Labour did not carry through on big ambitions to reshape political common sense (or, to use the Gramscian term, hegemony) through policy and discourse. They perceived them selves as—and thus were—trapped by social, economic, and cultural structures. They failed to fully take into account the cultural shifts which meant that their working-class base was no longer secure, and this was one reason for Labour’s poor showing in the elections of the early 2000s.⁵¹ Nevertheless, Labour’s modernizers correctly perceived that Thatcher had not wrought an irreversible cultural revolution, and that some of the social and cultural trends narrowly associated with her—not

In order to understand vernacular discourses of class, historians must turn to sources where individuals give an account of themselves. In this book, that means reusing the raw materials of post-war sociology, social observation, Mass Observa tion (MO), and oral history. Reuse of sociological and oral history material has been of increasing interest to historians in recent years.⁵² These sources enable me to use people’s own words to examine what they thought and felt about class, society, and themselves.
