### Key Takeaways

- India tops the list with over 18 million citizens living abroad.

- Some small countries, like Guyana and Samoa, have over half their populations living outside their borders.
- Conflict, jobs, and opportunity are major drivers of emigration.

Migration is often viewed through the lens of immigration (people arriving in a country). But what about those leaving?

This infographic by Iswardi Ishak, using data from the UN Department of Economic and Social Affairs, maps the countries with the highest number of citizens living abroad in 2024.

### What is Emigration, and Why Does It Matter?

Simply put, emigration is the opposite of immigration. It refers to the act of leaving one’s country to live elsewhere. While commonly discussed in regions receiving migrants, emigration has major impacts on origin countries too, from remittances to brain drain.

The reasons for leaving vary. Common “push factors” include poverty, violence, corruption, and climate change. Conversely, “pull factors” are driven by better education, jobs, healthcare, and safety abroad.

A closer look at the data shows that just five countries account for roughly one in five global emigrants.

Unsurprisingly, populous countries dominate the top of the list. India leads with over 18 million citizens abroad, followed by China (11.7 million), Mexico (11.6 million) and Ukraine (9.8 million). Meanwhile, countries like South Sudan and Syria have high emigration numbers relative to their total populations, driven by conflict and instability.

Of developed nations, the UK stands out with 4.8 million emigrants, largely driven by retirees, expats, and long-standing diasporas.

### The “Brain Drain” and High Emigration Rates

When viewed as a share of total population, smaller countries top the list. In Guyana, Samoa, and Bosnia & Herzegovina, over 50% of citizens live abroad. This reflects a classic “brain drain” effect, where educated or skilled workers leave for better prospects, often slowing economic growth at home.

### Explaining the “Migration Hump”

A key theory in migration studies is the “Migration Hump”. It suggests that emigration rises as countries move from low- to middle-income status, when people gain the means and motivation to move. As prosperity continues and countries become high-income, emigration rates drop.

This phenomenon helps explain why middle-income nations like Egypt, the Philippines, and Pakistan feature prominently in the dataset.

## Africa Climbs the Rankings

African countries are projected to make some of the largest gains in the global population rankings by 2050.

Nigeria is expected to add 154 million people between 2026 and 2050, rising from sixth to third place globally. DR Congo is projected to add another 120 million people, nearly doubling its population and overtaking Brazil.

Together, Nigeria, DR Congo, and Ethiopia are projected to add roughly 346 million people, nearly equivalent to the entire projected U.S. population in 2050. This reflects a broader demographic shift, with sub-Saharan Africa’s population expected to increase by 79% between 2024 and 2054, reaching 2.2 billion.

## Two Very Different Demographic Futures

By 2050, China’s median age is projected to reach 53.1, more than twice Nigeria’s at 24.9. This nearly 28-year gap highlights the contrasting demographic futures of two of the world’s most populous countries.

For China, an aging workforce and growing retiree population could intensify pressure on pensions and economic growth. Nigeria faces a different challenge: creating enough jobs for its young and growing population. Its demographic advantage will depend on its ability to translate workforce growth into employment and productivity gains.

From fixing a car engine to removing a clothing stain, which everyday tasks do Americans believe they can handle?

This visualization uses data from a Pew Research Center survey conducted from April 28 to May 4, 2025. It compares the share of U.S. men and women who say they could definitely or probably perform 12 everyday activities, measuring self-reported ability rather than tested skills.

The differences are especially pronounced in technical and mechanical tasks. Men report substantially greater confidence in fixing a car engine, navigating with a compass, and weatherproofing windows and doors.

By contrast, the tasks where women report greater confidence show much narrower gaps. Removing a clothing stain has the largest female advantage, at just four percentage points.

## Why Do Men Report More Confidence in Technical Tasks?

One possible explanation is that confidence and actual ability do not always align.

A 2019 study published in the American Economic Review found that gender stereotypes can influence how people assess their own abilities. In laboratory experiments, both men and women tended to overestimate their performance, particularly on difficult tasks, but the extent of overestimation varied by subject.

These findings offer context for Pew’s results, although they do not establish why the gaps in this survey exist. Differences in experience, familiarity with particular tasks, and willingness to express confidence could also play a role.

## Education Reveals a Different Pattern

Higher education is associated with greater confidence in some everyday skills, but not all of them.

For example, 97% of postgraduates say they can interpret charts and tables in news articles, compared with 76% of Americans with a high school education or less. A similar pattern appears in calculating tips mentally.

Car repairs tell the opposite story. Among Americans with a high school education or less, 36% say they could fix an engine problem, compared with just 17% of postgraduates.

The contrast highlights how practical experience and formal education may be associated with confidence in different kinds of skills.

## How Confidence Changes With Age

Older Americans also report greater confidence in several everyday tasks.

Among adults under 30, 72% say they could grow a vegetable garden, compared with 89% of those ages 50 to 64. Younger adults are also less confident in explaining yeast’s role in breadmaking.

Mental arithmetic shows a similar pattern: 77% of adults under 30 say they could calculate a tip without a calculator, compared with 90% of those ages 65 and older.

## What the Survey Actually Measures

These findings reflect what respondents believe they can do, not their demonstrated ability. Differences in experience, familiarity, and confidence may all influence how people answer.

The full Pew Research Center survey included 5,085 U.S. adults. The 12 tasks were divided between two randomized survey forms, so each question was answered by approximately half the respondents.

Small differences of one or two percentage points should not be interpreted as meaningful gender differences without additional statistical analysis.
