### ANTITRUST & THE IMMORAL ENERGY ECONOMY

by: Kristen van de Biezenbos*

Abstract

The age of climate change is upon us, but regulations and programs intended to decarbonize the electricity sector have been effectively stymied by utilities defending their monopoly power. The lack of robust antitrust enforcement against the utilities to date is a result of the belief that regulation is sufficient to police monopoly anticompetitive activity, that the natural monopoly structure of utilities makes them a poor fit for antitrust principles, and that the utilities are immune from Sherman Act claims under the state action exemption. But these rationales reflect a misunderstanding of the purpose of public utility law and its preference for monopolies, and the exemption is narrower than many realize. To provide much-needed clarity, this Article advances a framework to guide the application of state and federal antitrust law to electric utilities. In doing so, it is the first to use the moral economy lens to counter the economic narratives that have contributed to persistent beliefs that protection of the monopolies is necessary for a functioning electricity industry. Understanding the economic organization of the electricity sector through moral economy principles explicates the normative social outcomes that monopolies in the public utilities sphere were intended to achieve. These norms challenge the long-promoted view of electricity utility monopolies as an economic inevitability and provide guidance to the Department of Justice, state attorneys general, and injured par ties who wish to bring antitrust claims against monopoly utilities.

The Filed Rate Doctrine In addition to the state action exemption, utilities may raise the filed rate doctrine as a defense to antitrust claims. 197 Under this doctrine, rates filed with the appropriate PUC cannot be challenged, whether or not those rates have been approved by that commission as just and reasonable. 198 The purpose of the doctrine is to protect ratepayers from utilities attempting to charge differing, discriminatory prices to certain groups of customers. 199 Consequently, a majority of courts have found that the filed rate doctrine can only be raised in actions brought by utility customers and not in suits brought by competitors. 200 As such, it is not generally a bar to antitrust claims unless those claims are being raised by utility customers as part of an attack on rates. It bears mentioning, however, because monopoly utilities still argue the doctrine, even in cases brought by competing firms. The Common Law Roots of United States Public Utility Law Imagine a rushing river, on either side of which are bustling towns and busy markets. The river itself is a source of fish, navigation, and drinking water. Since the river provides its own bounties and free traverse of its waters is important, there is only one crossing for miles. Using the Moral Economy as a Framework to Guide Antitrust Actions There is little debate that decades of federal and state regulation meant to encourage competition in the electricity system have largely failed, although it may be more attributable to enforcement failures by regulators as opposed to an inherent weakness in a regulatory approach. 274 Whatever the reason, the pace of decarbonization has already been significantly slowed by utility intransigence and anticompetitive behavior. And, where competitive markets for disruptive technologies already exist, the need to implement these technologies rapidly to achieve net zero makes reliance on markets that are dominated by monopoly companies untenable. 275 Without some means of stopping them, there is no reason to believe that utilities will adapt to better accommodate these disruptive technologies when doing so would hurt their cost-recovery business model. Antitrust presents a potentially powerful second choice where regulation is inapplicable or insufficient, but the power of the natural monopoly narrative, the pervasiveness of regulation in the space, and the existence of the state action exemption have left antitrust an unreliable avenue for parties injured by the anticompetitive behavior of utilities. Enter the moral economy. Perhaps more so than in industries, the social expectations and values that shaped electricity regulation are intrinsic to its regulatory landscape. Indeed, while the natural monopoly narrative was highly persuasive in the crafting of state and federal electricity regulation, so was the law of public callings and the Supreme Court’s powerful reasoning in Munn. The FPA and nearly all state regulations include requirements that rates be just and reasonable, that utilities cannot discriminate between ratepayers of the same class, and that there is a duty on the part of utilities to serve their customers. 276 These are not requirements driven by economic notions of efficiency or allocation of resources. They are concepts that are so fundamental to the idea of a public utility that not even Insull and the profit-seeking members of the NELA could erase them. 277

While the seemingly endless ability of monopolies to avoid the requirements of laws aimed at protecting independent companies in markets touching on electricity service has sometimes seemed intractable, antitrust offers a potential solution.
