Jeffrey B. Dawson, Matthew Higgins, and Chanwoo Kim Growth in the U.S. working-age population has fallen to near zero with the recent sharp drop in net immigration. Given the ongoing decline in the native-born working-age population, the available labor supply could soon begin to shrink. The demographic path the U.S. may soon navigate has already been trod by several other advanced economies that have experienced more rapid population aging and historically lower immigration. This post lays out the basic facts about advanced economy (AE) experiences since 2014 and raises the prospect that the demographic headwinds long visible abroad may now be coming into clear view at home. The table below shows percentage changes in three variables from 2014 to 2025: working-age (ages 15-64) populations, prime-age (ages 25-54) populations, and the active population, or labor force. The countries shown are the sixteen largest AEs, ordered by economic size. We also show median values for a larger set of thirty-six AEs and for developments in China, at the bottom of the table. Level-Setting AE Population and Labor Force Growth AE Population and Labor Force Growth Since 2014 (percent) The first column shows wide variation in the growth of working-age populations. Growth has been negative for seven of the countries shown, with particularly marked declines in Japan, Italy, Poland, and Taiwan. Growth has also been negative in the median AE. Yet several countries have seen gains of more than 5 percent. Growth in the U.S. was solidly positive at 3 percent, but not near the top. Variation in the growth of prime-age populations, shown in the second column, has been still more striking, ranging from dramatic declines (Japan, Germany, Italy, and Spain) to marked increases (Canada, Australia, and Sweden). Again, the U.S. stands toward the higher end of the distribution. Larger declines in the prime-age population than in the working-age population (note the gap in Spain) imply that a large share of the population has moved into the 55-64 age group and will drop out of the traditional working-age population over the coming decade. As we’ll see shortly, the variation in countries’ demographic trajectories is mostly an immigration story. The third column of the table shows labor force growth since 2014. The contrast with demographic developments is stark. Where working-age populations have grown, active populations have grown more strongly; where working-age populations have declined, active populations have defied demographic gravity. (Indeed, in our broader sample of thirty-six AEs, only Greece and Latvia saw small labor force declines.) In several cases, the contrast can only be termed remarkable. Japan, for example, saw 6 percent labor force growth on a 7 percent drop in its working-age population. Germany saw almost 6 percent growth on a 2 percent decline in its working-age population—and a drop of more than 8 percent in the high-attachment prime-age population. The gap between growth in active and working-age populations implies that labor force participation has been increasing.
