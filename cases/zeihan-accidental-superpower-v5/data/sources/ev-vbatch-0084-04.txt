The country has a relatively young and growing population of about 7.4 million people, with a universal literacy rate. The Kyrgyz Republic is also recognized as a human capital champion country , based on its notable scores in the World Bank’s new Human Capital Index Plus (HCI+) — among the highest for countries at comparable income levels.
The Kyrgyz Republic’s economy is small and open. Services account for the largest share of GDP, while agriculture, remittances, and gold production remain important. Growth averaged 10.2% a year from 2022 to 2025, significantly reducing poverty as measured by both national and international poverty lines.
Despite this strong performance, the economy remains vulnerable to external shocks and commodity price changes. Greater diversification and resilience are therefore important.
The government's National Development Plan, launched in 2025, aims to achieve upper-middle-income status by 2030. It prioritizes connectivity, agriculture, renewable energy, and green tourism. The plan seeks to strengthen regional integration and infrastructure. It also aims to make better use of the country’s natural resources and location to support more inclusive and sustainable growth.

Consumer spending, investment, higher real wages, and rapid credit expansion supported this growth. Construction grew by 60.6% in real terms, industry by 11.1%, and services by 6.1%. Growth continues to exceed its estimated potential, contributing to inflationary pressures.
Inflation rose in May 2026 following fuel shortages caused by supply disruptions from Russia. Average inflation increased from 8.2% in December 2025 to 10.7% in July 2026.
The budget surplus widened to 6.6% of GDP in the first half of 2026, up from 6.1% a year earlier. Revenue increased to 52.9% of GDP from 44.7%, supported by strong tax collection, higher non-tax revenue, and a transfer of profits from the Central Bank. Spending also rose to 46.3% of GDP from 38.6% a year earlier, mainly due to higher spending on goods, services, and capital expenditures. Public debt increased to 40.5% of GDP at the end of June 2026, from 39.5% at the end of 2025.
Growth is projected to slow to 9.6% in 2026 as consumer spending and investment normalize from a high base. Higher fuel prices are expected to slow economic activity by raising transport and production costs and reducing household purchasing power. Average inflation is projected to reach 12% in 2026 due to strong domestic demand and higher fuel prices. The budget is expected to remain in surplus at 1.7% of GDP in 2026, supported by strong revenues. Expenditure is expected to rise following recent public sector wage increases. This outlook remains subject to external developments. Slower growth in Russia or China could reduce trade and remittances. A tightening of sanctions on Russia, including secondary sanctions, may also have implications for trade and financial activity. Potential disruptions to Russian fuel supplies and the conflict in the Middle East could increase energy and fertilizer prices.
Strong economic growth has continued to reduce poverty and improve living standards in the Kyrgyz Republic. The poverty rate is estimated to have fallen from 12.5% in 2024 to 11.0% in 2025 (based on the international poverty line of $4.20 a day in 2021 purchasing power parity terms). Rising labor income accounted for about three-quarters of this decline, while remittances, pensions, and social assistance contributed to the rest. Although progress slowed in 2025 after larger reductions in earlier years, the positive trend continued despite higher prices limiting gains in purchasing power for lower-income households.
The country’s development challenge remains to translate growth into more inclusive, private sector–led job creation, including for women and youth. Labor force participation is 44% for women compared with 71% for men, and women’s average earnings are about 69% of men’s.
The World Bank Group supports this agenda through a combination of policy engagement and reforms to strengthen the business environment and institutions, investments that raise productivity and connectivity (including in energy, water, agriculture, and digital), and efforts to mobilize private investment. Working as One World Bank Group, IDA is supporting priority reforms and public investments, while IFC and MIGA help mobilize private capital through financing, advisory support, and risk mitigation — together supporting sustainable growth and expanding economic opportunities.
