ShareAs the war in Ukraine continues in its fifth year, CFR’s initiative on securing Ukraine’s future is tracking the conflict with timely analysis and policy recommendations.
After Russia’s invasion in February 2022, Ukraine became by far the top recipient of U.S. foreign aid, marking the first time a European country held the top spot since the Marshall Plan directed vast sums to rebuild the continent after World War II. Yet U.S. commitment to Ukraine has shifted sharply under the second Trump administration, and there has been no significant aid legislation since 2024.
As of June 30, 2026, the U.S. Congress has made available $196 billion in spending related to Operation Atlantic Resolve and the war in Ukraine, according to the U.S. Special Inspector General for Operation Atlantic Resolve. Of the $196 billion, $164 billion comes from five pieces of legislation, the last of which was passed in April 2024. The remainder comes from annual agency budgets and other appropriations ($28 billion), and from NATO contributions received to pay for the provision of U.S. arms (allies or partners provided $5 billion in funds, which could be counted as aid from those donors).

Trump has allowed the sale of U.S. weapons to Ukraine through a plan known as the Prioritized Ukraine Requirements List (PURL), in which NATO allies pay for U.S. weapons and transfer them to Ukraine. The program is providing advanced U.S. weapons such as Patriot missiles and is supplying the majority of the missiles used in Ukraine’s air defense systems. However, by mid-October 2026, Ukrainian stockpiles of interceptors had almost been exhausted, and, as of October 21, the Trump administration had not signed off on the foreign transfer of additional missiles.
In October, Ukraine also reported experiencing other disruptions in assistance, including the loss of early-warning intelligence about incoming Russian ballistic missile attacks, as well as slowdowns in its Starshield and Starlink internet services operated by Elon Musk’s SpaceX. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy accused the United States of intentionally causing the disruptions.
The Trump administration has denied involvement. In a statement on October 19, a senior Pentagon official said that “there has been no change to Department of War policy regarding intelligence sharing or any capabilities we are enabling Europeans to provide to Ukraine.” Secretary of State Marco Rubio the following day said nothing had been “deliberately denied to Ukraine.” But on October 20, the Wall Street Journal reported that Musk ordered the slowdown and that he and Trump discussed it, with one source saying they spoke beforehand; CBS News reported that Musk consulted several administration officials in advance to gauge Trump’s thinking.
Much of the U.S. aid has gone toward providing weapons systems, training, and intelligence that Ukrainian commanders need to defend against Russia, which has one of the world’s most powerful militaries. Many Western analysts say the military aid provided by the United States and other allies has played a pivotal role in Ukraine’s defense and counteroffensive against Russia. Russia made incremental gains in 2024 and 2025, capturing some territory along the eastern front, but its progress has stalled in 2026.
During the Biden administration, the United States and top European allies considered Russia’s invasion an illegal war of aggression on NATO’s frontier that, if successful, would subjugate millions of Ukrainians; encourage Russian President Vladimir Putin’s revanchist aims; and invite similar aggression from other rival powers, especially China.
