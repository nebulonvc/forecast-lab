Fast forward two and a half years (and enough geoeconomic change for a lifetime) to this month’s release and my headline today is: “same metric, still a mess.”
Youth unemployment, as measured among “urban” residents between sixteen and twenty-four years old, stands at roughly 18 percent, after hitting a twelve-month high of 18.9 percent in August 2026. Seasonally, rates peak in September; this is the worst September reading since official reporting began again. More troubling, even the good months are getting worse—annual lows (seen in June each year) have crept up from 13.2 percent in 2024 to 14.5 percent in 2025 to 14.9 percent in 2026. Among the overall population, the urban jobless rate is 5.2 percent, meaning the youth rate is now about three and a half times higher. This is not simply a new graduates problem: The rate for individuals between twenty-five and twenty-nine years old is 7.4 percent, with 12.7 million graduates set to enter a labor market constrained by weak demand.
What appeared in 2024 to be primarily a domestic labor market challenge increasingly looks like a geoeconomic one. In terms of strategic competitiveness, Beijing’s ambitions in artificial intelligence (AI), electric vehicles, and biotechnology rely not only on industrial policy but on effectively harnessing human capital. Persistent youth unemployment suggests a worsening skills mismatch and weak labor market absorption. It is hard to see these ambitions coming to fruition when nearly one in five young urban workers is unemployed—the underutilization of its workforce is a heavy drag on its productivity.
