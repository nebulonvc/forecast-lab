The United States is the world’s second largest agricultural trader, behind the European Union. U.S. agricultural exports and imports increased significantly over the last 25 years due to the economic ascension of many emerging economies, as well as the implementation of foreign and domestic policies that expanded U.S. access to foreign markets. Over the same period, the geographic and product composition of U.S. agricultural trade shifted, as rising incomes and growing supply capacity of emerging economies reshaped global supply and demand for agricultural and food products.

With U.S. agricultural output growing faster than domestic demand for many products, U.S. farmers and agricultural firms have looked toward export markets to help sustain prices and revenues. As a result, U.S. agricultural exports have grown steadily over the past 25 years—reaching $171.5 billion in 2025, up from $57 billion in 2001.

Destinations for U.S. agricultural exports have also shifted over the last 25 years. Rising household incomes and changing trade policies in East and Southeast Asian countries have also driven export growth—especially to China, where the share of U.S. agricultural exports grew from about 7 percent during 2001–05 to 15 percent during 2021–25. Meanwhile, there has been a decline in the U.S. agricultural export share going to both Europe and high-income East Asia, particularly Japan (for data on U.S. agricultural destinations, see figures 3 and 4).

U.S. agricultural imports have expanded steadily over the past 25 years. Between 2001 and 2025, total U.S. agricultural imports increased from $43 billion to $212 billion. This growth was largely driven by a growing domestic demand for an array of consumer-oriented products, with almost 98 percent of the value of U.S. agricultural imports being high-value products, as opposed to bulk commodities (figures 5 and 6). Consumer-oriented products have grown, on average, by nearly 7 percent annually since 2001. Much of this demand is for imports like fruits and vegetables, alcoholic beverages and processed foods. Many such goods often can’t be easily or economically produced in the United States, such as tropical products or off-season produce. Horticultural products (a grouping that broadly includes fruits, vegetables, alcoholic beverages, essential oils and cut flowers) accounted for 45 percent of U.S. agricultural imports in 2025, by value. Sugar and tropical products (such as coffee, cocoa, and spices) accounted for approximately 19 percent of imports.

The top three agricultural exporters to the United States are Mexico, Canada, and the European Union, accounting for almost 60 percent of all agricultural sales to the United States between 2021–25.
