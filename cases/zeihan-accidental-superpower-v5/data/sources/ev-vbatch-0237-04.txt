Europe was left out of the Shenzhen summit , but it wasn’t forgotten, at least in Chinese minds. The state media was quick to send signals that it was thinking of Europe (which in turn has been thinking of targeting Chinese imports). Xi Jinping’s November 19 appearance with US President Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin, the Russian leader, ended with a statement speaking of peace and co-operation in broad terms. There was little for diplomats to get their teeth into beyond the images of the US and Chinese leaders standing together, accompanied by the head of a lesser Eurasian power. Europe was absent, and yet Beijing’s messaging suggests it still matters a great deal. The Chinese party-state is adept at using its coordinated media outlets for coded dispatches in the absence of official position-taking. As initial clues to what comes next, these are worth noting because they serve as a guide to official thinking. In decades past, China-watchers would scrutinize the front page of the People’s Daily for hints in the choice of a headline or the position of a photograph. Today the messaging is seamless across that newspaper’s website and the operations of the state news agency, Xinhua, together with the regime’s broadcasting networks. There could be no confusion, then, when all of them decided in an act of editorial spontaneity to headline the 500 th departure of a cargo train from the central China city of Wuhan to Europe on November 20. The train was bound for Moscow, but its journey underlined the surge in Chinese land exports to Europe. According to Xinhua, the volume of cargo sent by the service is up more than 44% year-on-year so far in 2026, at 454,800 tons. It served to remind the Europeans of the big economic stakes, and of China’s resilience as a supplier that has options to avoid choke points (like the straits of Hormuz or the Bab el-Mandeb) and other trouble spots in what President Xi calls the “winds and rain” of turbulent world affairs.
