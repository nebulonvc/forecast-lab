DIIS Policy Brief 8 July 2026 Repairing under fire - recovery chain constraints on Ukraine’s energy resilience Izabela Surwillo Illustration © ADA_photo via Shutterstock The 2025–2026 winter points to growing structural constraints on Ukraine’s energy system resilience. The key question is whether damaged infrastructure can be restored faster than Russia can disable it. This depends on the full recovery chain – from procurement to installation, commissioning and protection. Air defence and operational adaptation remain central, but delays across this chain increasingly constrain recovery under sustained attack. KEY FINDINGS Ukraine’s energy system is still operating thanks to domestic adaptation, inherited redundancy and international support, but cumulative damage and depleted stocks are narrowing its resilience margins. Sustained attack on the full recovery chain is becoming an increasing constraint on system resilience. Recovery is constrained by scarce equipment, long production and procurement, compatibility problems, wartime logistics, and personnel shortages. INTRODUCTION Ukraine’s energy system has continued to operate despite sustained and evolving attacks on generation, transmission and fuel infrastructure. Since 2022 it has avoided systemic collapse through a combination of inherited Soviet-era infrastructure that proved oversized after the post-Soviet decline in demand; operational adaptation; reduced consumption; rapid repair; and extensive international support. However, the winter of 2025–2026 suggests that these foundations are under growing strain, as repeated attacks — including on repaired assets — have eroded resilience buffers and depleted equipment stocks. Since October 2025 Russian attacks have damaged more than 9 GW of thermal, combined heat and power, and hydropower generation capacity. [i] More than 4 GW had been restored by early April 2026. [ii] By late June, repairs were continuing, with government planning focused on restoring around 6 GW before the 2026–2027 heating season. [iii] The damaged capacity was equivalent to approximately half of the 18 GW severe winter peak demand benchmark. [iv] These figures exclude damage to transmission and distribution infrastructure – including substations, transformers and power lines – which continues to constrain system recovery. Ukraine’s energy resilience depends on the interaction between protection, system operation, and recovery capacity. Recovery is also constrained by the availability of engineers, installation teams, drivers, and manufacturer-certified technicians. Equipment delivered to a warehouse does not restore system functionality until it has been transported, installed, tested, commissioned and protected. The constraint is therefore not manufacturing lead time alone, but the duration and reliability of the entire recovery chain. PROCUREMENT AND EXTERNAL SUPPORT International support has been critical to sustaining Ukraine’s energy system. By February 2026 the EU Civil Protection Mechanism had offered more than 11,200 generators and more than 7,200 transformers to Ukraine. [xiii] Alongside bilateral support, this assistance has enabled emergency power supply, repairs and equipment replacement. However, public reporting does not consistently distinguish between standardised equipment and complex transmission-level assets, making it difficult to assess how closely aggregate delivery volumes match the most acute needs.
