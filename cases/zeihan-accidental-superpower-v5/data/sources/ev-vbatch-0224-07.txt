Contemporary Chechnya is a region where army regiments and battalions have become part of everyday life.

In February 2022, on the eve of Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, Chechen Head Ramzan Kadyrov lined up around 12,000 security personnel in Grozny’s central square. Two years later, in January 2024, a similar formation involved 25,000 men. These figures are more than a demonstration of loyalty — they reflect a large-scale process of militarisation in Chechnya.

According to human rights researchers, over four years of war, the ‘foot soldier of Putin’ has roughly doubled the size of the armed formations under Kadyrov’s control: from an estimated 16,000–18,000 personnel in 2022 to as many as 33,000 today.

Today, the structure of the ‘Kadyrov army’ is a complex conglomerate of units formally subordinate to different agencies but effectively controlled from Grozny. Its core consists of formations within the Russian National Guard (Rosgvardiya) and the Defence Ministry.

The most significant growth, however, has occurred within the Defence Ministry. Kadyrov, government chair Magomed Daudov, and commander of the Akhmat Unit Apti Alaudinov regularly report the deployment of ever more ‘Kadyrovites’ to the ‘special military operation (SVO) zone’ — the Russian government’s term for the full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

‘These units now include the 78th Special Purpose Motor Rifle Regiment Sever-Akhmat (‘North-Akhmat’), the 349th Motor Rifle Regiment Akhmat-Russia, the 1434th Regiment Akhmat-Chechnya, the 270th Motor Rifle Regiment Akhmat-Caucasus, the Yug-Akhmat (‘South-Akhmat’), Zapad-Akhmat (‘West-Akhmat’) and Vostok-Akhmat (‘East-Akhmat’) motor rifle battalions, and the Sheikh Mansur Reserve Training Rifle Battalion. These are not Rosgvardiya gendarmerie units but fully fledged military formations in terms of structure, personnel, weapons, and equipment. They formally belong to the Ministry of Defence but are primarily loyal and accountable to Ramzan Kadyrov’, the Memorial report states.

At the same time, despite official rhetoric, the war remains deeply unpopular in Chechnya, NIYSO reports, and there is a shortage of people willing to fight voluntarily. As a result, what human rights groups describe as a ‘volunteer market’ based on coercion and extortion has emerged.

According to Memorial volunteers, in 2024, Kadyrov ordered every district police department and patrol regiment to recruit between 25 and 50 ‘volunteers’.

‘We have repeatedly warned troublemakers and lawbreakers. From now on, the conversation with them will be brief. If you do not respect the law, then either prison with the maximum possible sentence, or sign a contract and go to the SVO zone’, Kadyrov said at the time.

Analysts from the human rights organisation Memorial support this assessment in a recent report, arguing that the war has provided Kadyrov with a unique ‘window of opportunity’. The creation of new military units has allowed him to gain influence not only over Rosgvardiya gendarmerie formations but also over full-fledged army regiments designed for conventional warfare.

According to the report, this gives the Kadyrov ‘army’ a capability that neither Dzhokhar Dudaev nor Aslan Maskhadov — former presidents of Ichkeria, a short-lived breakaway Chechen state — possessed: ‘the potential, under certain circumstances, to confront the federal centre on something approaching equal terms, thereby raising the possible cost of any attempt to challenge Kadyrov’s sovereignty’.

‘The increase in security forces overall was approved by the Kremlin, but the degree to which a given unit was under the Kremlin’s control depends on the unit itself and its chain-of-command. As long as the forces were contributing to the invasion, however, the Kremlin was okay with the increased forces’, Chambers tells OC Media.
