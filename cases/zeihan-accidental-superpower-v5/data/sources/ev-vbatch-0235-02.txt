The United States launched new strikes on vessels suspected of transporting drugs in late August—the first attacks after a two-month hiatus. The latest missile strikes suggest the Trump administration is sticking with a tactic that evidence suggests has so far failed to curb drug trafficking. US President Trump has said that maritime drug trafficking to the United States dropped dramatically since the strikes began in September 2025, even claiming it had fallen 98% by January. But US Border Patrol (USBP) and Office of Field Operations (OFO) drug seizures tell a different story. Drug seizures can operate as a proxy, albeit a far-from-perfect one, for the amount of drugs passing through an area. Seizures of cocaine, the drug most likely to be aboard the targeted vessels, at or between ports of entry to the United States fell by less than 3% in the 11 months following the beginning of the strikes when compared with the same period the year prior. A supply shortage also typically pushes the prices of a drug up, but cocaine prices in the United States have also remained largely stable, further challenging the notion that supply has been reduced. There is evidence that strikes have disrupted specific routes forcing smugglers to adopt alternate routes or methods, but they have not substantially reduced drug trafficking. Some traffickers initially abandoned coastal drug trafficking export points in Venezuela following the strikes, and trafficking to nearby islands such as Aruba, Curaçao, and Trinidad all but froze for a time, according to InSight Crime investigations. But seizures near Aruba in June and July show that traffickers are continuing to use these routes. Alternatives have also sprung up, with a reported increase in drug flights from Venezuela into Guyana after the strikes began in the Caribbean Sea near Venezuela. Recent drug confiscations in or near Guyana’s neighbor Suriname suggest that routes departing these countries could be becoming more fixed. Drug traffickers are also using larger boats and sticking closer to coastlines to reduce the likelihood of potential attacks, according to a Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) assessment the Washington Post reviewed.
