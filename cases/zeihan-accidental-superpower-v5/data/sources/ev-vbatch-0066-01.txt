Highlights 
In the first half of 2026, household electricity prices increased in 13, decreased in 11, and remained rather stable in 3 EU countries, compared with the first half of 2025. 
In the first half of 2026, compared with the first half of 2025, household electricity prices in the EU showed the highest increase in Romania (51.9%). The largest decrease was observed in Denmark(-31.7%). 
Electricity prices for non-household consumers in the EU fell by 5.4% in the first half of 2026 compared to the same period in 2025 and by 2.0% from the second half of 2025. 
Non-household electricity prices in the EU were highest in Ireland (€0.2666 per kWh) and lowest in Finland (€0.0928 per kWh) in the first half of 2026. 
Source: Eurostat ( nrg_pc_204 ) This article highlights the development of electricity prices both for household and non-household consumers within the European Union (EU) . When available, it also includes price data from Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Albania, Serbia, Türkiye, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Kosovo*, Moldova, Georgia, and Ukraine. 
The price of energy in the EU depends on a range of different supply and demand conditions, including the geopolitical situation, the national energy mix, import diversification, network costs, environmental protection costs, severe weather conditions, or levels of excise and taxation. Note that the prices presented in this article include taxes, levies and VAT for household consumers, but exclude refundable taxes and levies for non-household consumers. 
* This designation is without prejudice to positions on status, and is in line with UNSCR 1244/1999 and the ICJ Opinion on the Kosovo declaration of independence. 
Electricity prices for household consumers 
Highest electricity prices in Ireland and Germany 
For household consumers in the EU (defined for the purpose of this article as medium-sized consumers with an annual consumption between 2 500 Kilowatt-hours (kWh) and 5 000 kWh), electricity prices in the first half of 2026 were highest in Ireland (€0.3871 per kWh), Germany (€0.3618 per kWh), and Belgium (€0.3575 per kWh) (see Figure 1). 
The lowest prices were observed in Hungary (€0.1139 per kWh), Malta (€0.1268 per kWh), and Bulgaria (€0.1341 per kWh). 
For Irish household consumers, the per kWh cost was 37% above the EU average price, whereas households in Hungary, Malta, and Bulgaria paid less than half the price of the EU average. 
The EU average price in the first half of 2026 – a weighted average using the most recent (2024) consumption data for electricity by household consumers – was €0.2832 per kWh. 
Figure 1: Electricity prices for household consumers, first half 2026 Source: Eurostat ( nrg_pc_204 ) Figure 2 shows the development of electricity prices for household consumers in the EU since the first half of 2008. 
Between 2008 and 2021, electricity prices remained within a relatively stable range: prices excluding taxes generally fluctuated between €0.10 and €0.15 per kWh, while prices including taxes were mostly between €0.15 and €0.23 per kWh. 
Since 2022, however, electricity prices have risen sharply, marking a clear break with earlier trends. The increase was particularly pronounced for prices excluding taxes, which reflect energy, supply, and network costs. By contrast, the rise in prices including taxes was partly moderated by government interventions, as EU countries absorbed part of the shock through tax reductions and other consumer relief measures. This is reflected in the sharp fall in the tax share, which dropped from 41.0% in the second half of 2019 to 14.7% in the second half of 2022, the lowest level in the whole period observed. 
After the surge in 2022 and early 2023, electricity prices stabilised, but at levels well above those seen before the energy crisis. Since 2022, prices excluding taxes have broadly remained in the range of €0.20 to €0.24 per kWh. They peaked at €0.2385 per kWh in the first half of 2023 before gradually declining to €0.2059 per kWh by the second half of 2025 and falling slightly further to €0.2027 per kWh in the first half of 2026. 
Prices including taxes have likewise stabilised at a high level, remaining between €0.25 and €0.30 per kWh, with a peak of €0.2916 per kWh in the first half of 2024, then at the level of €0.2896 per kWh in the second half of 2025, and slightly lower at €0.2832 per kWh in the first half of 2026. 
From 2023 onwards, the gradual phasing-out of relief measures led to a progressive rise in the tax component. This increased to 28.9% in the second half of 2025, before declining slightly to 28.4% in the first half of 2026. As a result, the gap between prices excluding taxes and including taxes widened until the second half of 2025, before starting to stabilise in the first half of 2026.
