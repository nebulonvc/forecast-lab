This scenario-planning product assesses Russia's capacity to operate as a regional or global power through 2026–2030 and outlines the strategic implications of various potential futures for U.S. vital interests. 1 Employing the Shell/Kahane four-box method, we select two independently varying critical uncertainties from our list of predetermined structural trends—Regime Resilience and Strategic Isolation—to generate four plausible and analytically rigorous scenarios. Each scenario includes causal drivers, clear early-warning indicators with Green/Amber/Red thresholds, as well as a robust discussion of implications and playbooks tailored for policymakers. This product is intended for U.S. and international policymakers, foreign affairs, intelligence and defense planners, and regional specialists who require a prioritized monitoring dashboard and operational contingency options concerning Russia. 2
The economic trajectory in this scenario compounds two distinct pressures that interact more severely than a simple additive account would suggest. Sanctions pressure, hardened by demonstrated military failure rather than eased by political fatigue, continues to constrain access to technology, finance, and markets. Simultaneously, the war's failure removes the political justification for continued high defense spending that, in a victorious scenario, gets repurposed toward a confident reconstruction and modernization narrative as defense spending continues at high levels (the war's settlement does not eliminate the security threats that justified it) without producing a corresponding strategic or economic dividend.
Fiscal sustainability registers Red: hydrocarbon revenues remain constrained by hardened sanctions enforcement, the National Wealth Fund drawdown accelerates as the regime faces simultaneous pressure to fund continued defense commitments, manage reconstruction obligations in any settlement, and provide the social transfers necessary to maintain compliance amid declining real wages and rising inflation. The financial system experiences recurrent volatility, but the underlying cause is sharper than generic crisis: markets price in not merely sanctions risk but specific doubt about the regime's medium-term political stability given the elite fracture described above.
The demographic predetermined that constrains every scenario in this matrix takes on particular severity here because the war's failure removes any narrative justification for the casualties it produced. A victorious framing allows war losses to be folded into a vindicating national narrative; defeat removes that psychological and political buffer, and the accumulated demographic and social cost of mobilization, casualties, and emigration registers more acutely as ungrounded sacrifice rather than necessary cost of victory. This is consistent with the documented social effects of the historical Crimean War's casualties on Russian elite and educated public opinion, which historians generally treat as a contributing factor to the reform pressure Alexander II faced. The defeat made sacrifice that had been tolerated as the price of strength suddenly intolerable as the price of failure.
Russia's position across the broader post-Soviet space deteriorates more severely than in scenarios where the war's outcome remains contested, because partners calibrating their own alignment decisions are responding to a comparatively clear signal of Russian limitation. Central Asian diversification accelerates; the South Caucasus's fluidity increases as regional actors more confidently test the limits of a demonstrably weakened Moscow; and Belarus's position becomes more precarious, with the added dimension that Minsk must now calculate its alignment against a patron whose capacity to provide security guarantees has been directly called into question by events rather than merely by abstract economic indicators.
