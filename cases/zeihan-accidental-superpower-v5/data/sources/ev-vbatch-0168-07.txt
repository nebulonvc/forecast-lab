Mexico’s public debt is approaching a level that could put pressure on its investment-grade sovereign rating, according to analysts at Monex Casa de Bolsa. The firm estimates that debt exceeding 60% of GDP by 2030 could prompt a downgrade by one or more rating agencies, as the government faces pressure to consolidate public finances and continue supporting state-owned companies.
“Each time we are closer to the threshold that denotes investment grade,” said Janneth Quiroz, Director of Economic, Foreign Exchange and Equity Analysis, Monex, during a presentation reported by El CEO .
Monex estimates that public debt will reach 54.7% of GDP by the end of 2026 and 55% in 2027, based on the figures cited in its analysis. The firm identifies three factors that could increase credit risk: failure to meet fiscal consolidation targets, additional financial support for Petróleos Mexicanos (Pemex), and economic growth below government expectations.
The 60% figure is Monex’s estimated risk threshold, rather than a formal cutoff established by rating agencies. Credit assessments also consider the composition of debt, government revenue, economic growth, institutional conditions and the capacity to meet financial obligations.
The government’s latest public debt report provides a different measure of the country’s debt position. According to the Ministry of Finance and Public Credit (SHCP) , the Historical Balance of Public Sector Borrowing Requirements (SHRFSP) stood at 52.6% of GDP at the end of September 2026. This measure includes the broader financing requirements of the public sector, while other debt indicators cover narrower government obligations.
The SHCP reported that the federal government's net debt represented 49.4% of GDP. It also said that most federal government debt was denominated in pesos, carried fixed interest rates and had long-term maturities, factors that reduce exposure to exchange-rate movements, interest-rate changes and refinancing risks.
Fiscal consolidation remains central to the government’s strategy. The SHCP reported that the budget deficit was MX$244 billion below the amount programmed through September, while the primary deficit was MX$84 billion lower than expected. The government has set a gradual reduction in public-sector borrowing requirements as one of its fiscal objectives.
However, the outlook for public finances is also linked to the financial position of Pemex. The company continues to receive federal support to meet financial obligations and manage its debt. According to the International Monetary Fund’s October 2026 assessment of Mexico , Pemex’s financial obligations declined from approximately US$98 billion in 2024 to US$78 billion in June 2026. Nevertheless, the IMF noted that government support remained necessary.
