Aviation remains one of the safest forms of transport globally. In 2025, airlines carried just under five billion passengers on more than 38 million flights while continuing a two-decade decline in accident rates. The industry’s commitment to continuous safety improvements has driven a long-term reduction in accident rates, from 3.72 accidents per million sectors in 2005 to 1.32 in 2025. Although 2025 recorded 394 onboard fatalities, ongoing safety improvements across the industry helped prevent more severe outcomes. A particularly notable achievement was the absence of any Loss of Control Inflight (LOC‑I) accidents, a significant milestone given LOC‑I’s historical role as a leading cause of fatalities. Despite periodic fluctuations in fatality numbers, fatal accidents involving large commercial passenger aircraft remain exceedingly rare. Aviation’s risk landscape is evolving rapidly, driven by technological, geopolitical, environmental, and human factors. The industry must respond through unified global standards, enhanced data capabilities, strengthened crisis management, and deeper international collaboration. The aviation risk profile is dynamic and continually evolving, demanding constant vigilance. Safety outcomes cannot always be attributed solely to pilots or operators. The accident involving PSA Airlines, which collided mid-air with a helicopter, in Washington, DC, illustrates how airspace design, traffic procedures, and safety oversight can influence system-wide. In this case, multiple deep‑rooted failures aligned to create the conditions that led to the accident. Apart from the Ahmedabad accident, most accidents in 2025 were largely linked to adverse weather and operational threats, including manual handling and decision‑making errors, procedural non-compliance, systemic and regulatory failures, bird strikes, and maintenance issues. As investigations remain on-going, the full set of contributing factors has yet to be confirmed. Airport‑environment hazards also played a notable role in accident severity. The presence of solid obstacles such as buildings, concrete walls, and perimeter fencing near runways increased the likelihood that otherwise survivable events would become fatal. The freighter accident in Louisville, Kentucky, involved a takeoff crash traced by the NTSB to fatigue‑ and overstress‑related cracking in the engine‑pylon attachment lugs. The event also underscores how airport infrastructure constraints, such as layout limitations and limited buffer zones, can elevate risk and reduce recovery margins when mechanical failures occur. A similar dynamic was evident in the Jeju Air crash at Muan International Airport, where a runway overrun resulted in aircraft striking a rigid concrete structure supporting the airport’s instrument landing system. Additional runway excursion accidents in 2025, further highlighted the risks posed by non-compliant or hardened perimeter structures. An Ethiopian Airlines aircraft veered off the runway during heavy rain in Mekele, Ethiopia; its left main landing gear collapsed after impacting with two concrete manholes located within the runway strip, causing the left main gear to collapse and the tire to burst. Likewise, an Air Ocean Maroc overshot the runway at Fès–Saïss Airport and struck a perimeter wall. These accidents reinforce the importance of runway safety areas, frangible installations, and compliance with international requirements and standards. IATA urges airports and regulators to remove or mitigate hazardous structures and to maintain clear runway safety areas and runway end safety areas. Where immediate removal is not feasible, interim risk‑reduction measures should be implemented. Operations in conflict zones remain amongst the most serious threats to civil aviation. The tragic downing of Azerbaijan Airlines, which claimed 39 lives in 2024, should never have occurred. No civil aircraft should ever become a target—whether intentionally or unintentionally—of military activity. Governments bear a critical responsibility to strengthen intelligence‑sharing and coordination mechanisms to prevent such tragedies. Persistent threats such as GPS jamming and spoofing, alongside emerging hazards including rocket and space‑launch debris, further underscore the need for proactive and adaptive safety approaches. Ensuring the highest levels of aviation safety requires a proactive, data-driven approach. Strengthening data-sharing mechanisms, such as through IATA's Global Aviation Data Management (GADM) program, is essential to this effort. By pooling safety intelligence, emerging risks can be identified more effectively, enabling timely, targeted interventions. Safety is a shared responsibility. Robust risk management, timely publication of accident investigation reports, and proactive mitigation measures are essential to reducing risks. IATA remains fully committed to data-driven safety strategies and close collaboration with industry stakeholders to continuously strengthen aviation safety.
