When this week’s trilateral meeting between the leaders of China, the United States, and Russia was first announced, commentators with an eye for history were soon asking whether the Shenzhen summit was going to be a second Yalta. The original Yalta was a conference held towards the end of World War II that brought together Joseph Stalin, Winston Churchill, and Franklin Roosvelt to decide the fate of Europe once Nazi Germany was defeated. Yalta was followed by the Potsdam Conference shortly after Germany’s surrender, leading to further agreement on the postwar territorial disposition of the European continent. As a result of these summits, Russia’s imperial grasp became far greater than at any time in its history and included substantial control over the countries of Eastern and Central Europe. This arrangement was to a large extent driven by the presence of more than a million Soviet troops in these territories. Putin famously stated early in his long tenure as Russian president that the greatest geopolitical tragedy of the twentieth century was the fall of the Soviet Union. Like many other Russian imperialists, Putin has long yearned for the return of Kremlin control over the lands it once ruled. The war against Ukraine is designed to reestablish control over a nation and territory that were formerly critical to the sustenance of the Russian and Soviet empires. But as Putin made clear on the eve of the full-scale invasion in a late 2021 memo to NATO, he also wants the return of the boundaries between NATO and Russian-controlled areas that existed at the end of the Cold War. Putin has had a hard time taking control of Ukraine. The Russian military made substantial gains in the first few months of the full-scale invasion but could not take Kyiv or control any other major Ukrainian cities for long. By the early fall of 2022, a Ukrainian counteroffensive had liberated over half of the territory seized by Russian forces during Putin’s initial blitzkrieg. Since then, Russia has been unable to capture all of eastern Ukraine’s Donbas region, even though Putin “annexed” it in September 2022. And this year, with the extraordinary development of Ukraine’s defense industries and domestic drone production, Russia has made virtually no advances on the battlefield despite suffering horrendous casualties. Meanwhile, Russian occupation forces have been subject to a punishing Ukrainian drone campaign that has isolated Crimea for resupply purposes. In parallel, an escalating strategic bombing campaign led by long-range Ukrainian drones has reportedly destroyed almost 50 percent of Russia’s oil refining capacity in the past few weeks alone, putting new pressure on the Russian economy. With Russian generals feeding him false information, Putin still thinks he is making gains on the battlefield.
