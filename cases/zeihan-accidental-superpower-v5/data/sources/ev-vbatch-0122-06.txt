Japan's Current Account Surplus and Corporate Adjustment Speech at the 2026 Michel Camdessus Central Banking Lecture, International Monetary Fund (November 6, 2026) Governor of the Bank of Japan Full Text [PDF 282KB] It is an immense honor to speak today in celebration of Michel Camdessus. Few leaders have shaped modern international economic cooperation with such profound moral clarity, intellectual depth, and enduring goodwill as Mr. Camdessus has. He taught a generation of policymakers that macroeconomic policy is never merely technical; at its best, it is an act of shared global stewardship. That wisdom was made especially clear in the mid-1980s, when global imbalances placed Japan's current account surplus at the heart of international economic debate. Mr. Camdessus was among the most articulate voices calling for surplus nations to act as locomotives for global demand. Japan embraced that call, yet history took an unexpected turn: the post-Plaza Accord adjustments narrowed the surplus only briefly before it transformed into a structural, income-driven surplus that persists to this day. In honoring Mr. Camdessus, the best tribute we can offer is to examine these difficult economic realities with the same analytical honesty he brought to global governance. Today, I would like to examine what Japan's persistent surplus reveals about external adjustment, focusing in particular on the evolving role of corporate saving and investment. I. Japan's Current Account Surplus in Perspective Japan's current account has undergone a striking shift since the mid-20th century (Chart 1). Until the early 1980s, it alternated between deficits and surpluses, sometimes within just a few years. It then moved into persistent surplus. Despite short-term fluctuations, the surplus generally ranged from 2 to 4 percent of GDP for roughly four decades. Its composition has changed just as markedly (Chart 2). The goods trade surplus peaked at more than 4 percent of GDP in the mid-1980s, but has since given way to a deficit. Meanwhile, the primary income surplus, which was negligible in the mid-1980s, has risen to more than 6 percent of GDP. Net income earned on foreign investments has thus become the principal source of Japan's current account surplus (Chart 3). Japan is not unique in this respect. The United Kingdom in the late 19th and early 20th centuries offers a well-known historical parallel (Chart 4). IV. After Balance Sheet Repair: The Rise of Foreign Direct Investment Balance sheet repair eventually wound down in stages. Financial pressures began to ease in the mid-2000s once the Japanese banking crisis was resolved, although recovery was interrupted by the global financial crisis. A more definitive, structural turning point emerged around 2011-2012. Domestic investment and borrowing finally began to edge upward (Chart 9). Corporate saving, however, remained exceptionally high. Instead of channeling those funds aggressively into domestic factories, firms directed a substantial share of net saving into foreign direct investment (FDI). Since 2012, outward FDI has eclipsed portfolio investment as the dominant vehicle for Japan's capital This capital reallocation was rational: returns on outward FDI remained remarkably resilient, holding at above 8 percent for much of the post-adjustment era, even as yields on debt and equity investments declined A substantial portion of the earnings generated by overseas affiliates was retained and reinvested abroad. These earnings directly swelled Japan's primary income surplus, while their automatic reinvestment was recorded as fresh FDI. By constructing international production networks, Japanese multinational firms positioned themselves directly within rapidly growing overseas markets. A 2024 Bank of Japan survey of firms provides a firsthand window into why domestic investment remained subdued for so long (Chart 12). When reflecting on the period since the mid-1990s, managers most frequently pointed to structurally declining growth expectations.
