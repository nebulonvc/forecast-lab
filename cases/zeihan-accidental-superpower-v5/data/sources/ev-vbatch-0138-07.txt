Sovereign vulnerabilities are also building up, and debt levels are high in many advanced economies, including some euro area countries. At the same time, sovereign funding costs have risen. Fiscal consolidation is overdue and increasingly important in an environment where the presence of news-sensitive, leveraged investors in sovereign bond markets is growing. Rising exposures to AI and sovereign debt warrant attention, with vulnerabilities being more pronounced in some segments of the non-bank financial sector. Even if banks are not exposed to a potential correction in AI-related assets in material terms, they could face spillovers via strong links with non-banks. Moreover, a prospective deterioration in macro-financial conditions due to the energy supply disruptions could lead to rising credit risk. Banks have weathered past shocks well overall, thanks in part to the regulatory reforms introduced since the global financial crisis. They remain strongly positioned to absorb shocks, with resilience underpinned by ample liquidity buffers, sizeable capital headroom and robust profitability.

Rising sovereign exposures coupled with shifts in sovereign risk perceptions have reignited market concerns about the re-emergence of the sovereign-bank nexus. Much of this increase in sovereign holdings has, however, been driven by investments in non-domestic government bonds, which has reduced home bias. While this diversification somewhat mitigates concerns about the sovereign-bank nexus, higher sovereign exposures also expose euro area banks to valuation losses on their sovereign portfolios subject to mark-to-market accounting in the context of rising sovereign yields. Banks and non-banks may see their credit risks increase if macro-financial conditions deteriorate markedly as a result of the Middle East war. The quality of euro area bank assets has remained sound, with the aggregate non-performing loan ratio touching a record low of 2.2% in September 2026, albeit with some variation at country and sector levels (see Section 3.

However, banks’ cost of risk, as measured by the ratio of LLPs to total loans, edged up by just 2 basis points from its end-2025 level due to the offsetting impact of robust loan growth.
