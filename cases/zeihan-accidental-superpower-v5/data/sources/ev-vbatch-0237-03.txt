One of the first bills the newly convened State Duma discussed in October as part of the government’s budget package concerned regional debt. Namely, the federal government suggested postponing the repayment of federal budget loans maturing in 2027−29 to 2030, giving regions more fiscal space. This comes on top of a range of lending and debt-reduction programs from recent years; the postponement itself had originally been endorsed by Vladimir Putin in April, for debt maturing in 2026. At the beginning of 2026, total regional debt stood slightly below 3.5 trillion rubles . While this is only 18.9 percent of regions’ own revenue, it represented growth of more than 10 percent year on year. Regional debt is still on a rising trajectory, even though estimates for the end of 2026 have varied between 3.6 and 4 trillion rubles. Still, this is far from the near-miss debt crisis of 2015−16, when total indebtedness as a share of own revenues was twice the current level. That crisis triggered a federal response: the Finance Ministry introduced stricter rules for regional budgeting while issuing cheap budget loans for regions to refinance commercial debt taken out at much higher rates. Over time, this worked: by 2025, 80 percent of regional debt was held by the state , and debt-servicing costs fell. But the current situation is not without dangers; they are simply of a different nature. Finance Minister Anton Siluanov spoke openly about concerns over regional debt at the St. Petersburg Economic Forum in June. What now worries decision-makers is the trajectory—or, more simply, what exactly is growing, and where. A return to commercial debt 2025 saw not only an expansion of regional debt, but also an expansion of commercial lending to regions, reversing some of the gains of the debt-replacement program. Specifically, bank loans tripled to 676 billion rubles, with 17 regions borrowing for the first time . The value of market debt—bank loans and regional bonds— dropped slightly in the first eight months of 2026, despite earlier estimates of further growth, but this is only partial good news. These loans were provided at steep interest rates. To keep inflation under control—a federal priority—the Central Bank slowed, and in September 2026 stopped , its rate-cutting. Banks lend to regions above the key rate, within a federal cap of 3.5 percentage points. Even so, debt-servicing costs in consolidated regional budgets grew by more than 41 percent in 2025. Naturally, regions turn to such financing when no alternatives remain and spending on priority goals—public-sector salaries, social support and, increasingly, the war—must be sustained.
