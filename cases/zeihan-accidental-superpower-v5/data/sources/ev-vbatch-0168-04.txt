Mexico has set itself a demanding goal. Under Plan México, a national strategy to expand investment and industry, the government aims to create 1.5 million additional jobs while moving the country up the manufacturing value chain. That means a power grid that can keep factories running, highways and ports that can carry the goods, hospitals staffed with skilled professionals, and financing for the small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) that already employ most Mexicans.
Public budgets alone cannot shoulder the scale of this challenge. The money Mexico needs actually exists in pension funds, global institutional investors, and commercial banks, but it has stayed on the sidelines for myriad reasons: long construction timelines, revenue uncertainty, currency mismatches, and banking rules that make loans to SMEs inaccessible for many. Investors were not waiting for more money from the state. They were waiting for someone to absorb the first slice of risk.
Closing that gap became a priority for the Mexican government, and it is exactly where the World Bank Group (WBG) found its catalyst role.
Between fiscal year 2022 and fiscal year 2026 (a July-June period), the WBG helped cumulatively attract nearly US$12 billion in private capital in Mexico, placing the country among the top five destinations for WBG private capital mobilization worldwide. In fact, in just four years, annual investments more than doubled, jumping from US$1.8 billion in fiscal year 2022 to US$3.8 billion in 2026.
Behind the numbers is a deliberate shift, enabled by the government's decision to prioritize private financing of infrastructure. Alongside direct lending the WBG increasingly used guarantees, anchor investments, and risk-sharing structures targeting specific obstacles in key sectors. Mexican institutions built the platforms; private clients pioneered new structures; WBG supplied the risk capacity and credibility that let investors commit. Here is how this strategy worked, and what it means for jobs.
The groundwork was laid by the government. As Mexico strengthened the framework for its energy sector, the WBG supported the Ministry of Energy (SENER), at its request, with technical assistance and advisory work. That support contributed to opening the door for private capital to invest alongside the state in generation and transmission, and gave investors added confidence when a market-based financing vehicle for the national grid came to market.
To support new factories, Mexico's state electric utility, CFE, needed to expand the national grid between 2025 and 2030 without taking on more debt. The solution was CFE Fibra E, a trust listed on the stock exchange, which pays investors back using a share of the grid's steady, regulated income.
When the trust sold its first bond in September 2025, the WBG's private sector arm, the International Finance Corporation (IFC), bought US$75 million of it, a signal to other investors that the deal was solid. They responded in force: over US$6 billion in orders coming from some 240 institutions in 32 countries for a US$725 million bond. This money is now funding power lines and substations that waste less power, bring more renewables onto the grid, and keep the lights on in the factories Plan México is counting on.
Cajeme Villarreal, CEO of CFE Capital, said that the investment "will help achieve the energy justice and security goals set by CFE."
Mexico's infrastructure development bank, BANOBRAS, has long backed roads and transit projects through FONADIN, the federal infrastructure fund, by promising to cover part of the losses if a project runs into trouble, but only one project at a time. In December 2025, the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD) approved a guarantee of up to US$500 million that backs FONADIN's own guarantees, so that BANOBRAS can offer that protection across a whole pipeline of sustainable transport projects.
