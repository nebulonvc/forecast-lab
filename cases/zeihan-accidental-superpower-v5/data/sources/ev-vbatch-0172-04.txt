Retirement in an Aging Mexico: What Will Public Pensions Cover?
A public pension can make the difference between paying for groceries and depending on relatives to get through the month. For an older person who spent decades working without social security coverage, that income can provide a measure of independence.
Mexico has good reason to protect it. But a benefit that helps someone meet basic expenses serves a different purpose from one that replaces a paycheck. As the country ages, promising both through the public budget will become harder.
For people who still have years of work ahead of them, now is the time to build savings that can supplement what they expect to receive in retirement.
Mexico Is Aging Faster Than Many People Realize
Mexico still has a relatively young population compared with many other OECD countries.
This provides adequate time to prepare; however, the window of opportunity is narrowing quickly.
The OECD projects that the number of people aged 65 and older, relative to the working-age population, will rise more than three times as fast between 2025 and 2050 as it did between 2000 and 2025.
As smaller generations enter the workforce and larger ones reach retirement, there will be fewer people of working age for every older adult.
People of working age are only a rough measure of who supports the public budget. Some are studying, some are looking for work, and many others work in jobs that offer no social security coverage.
Older adults can also keep working and paying taxes. Even so, the demographic shift matters. If the number of people receiving benefits grows faster than the tax revenue available to pay for them, the government will have to find more money, adjust benefits, or make room elsewhere in the budget.
Higher productivity, better wages, and more formal employment could help. How much they will help is difficult to know decades in advance.
A Public Pension Should Provide a Floor
Mexico’s P ensión para el Bienestar de las Personas Adultas Mayores is financed by the government and does not require a record of social security contributions. That matters in a country where people can spend much of their working lives in informal jobs, running a small business, or caring for family without pay. A regular public benefit gives them an income of their own in old age. Its value is especially clear for people who reach retirement with little savings and limited ability to earn more.
The challenge is deciding how much that benefit can reasonably cover.
A payment that helps with food, utilities, and medicine can improve someone’s life even if it falls short of their full monthly budget. Housing, transportation, medical costs, and help with everyday tasks can add up quickly.
Public pensions already take up a growing share of Mexico’s resources. The OECD reports that pension spending rose by 2.4 percentage points of GDP over seven years, driven largely by the expansion of non-contributory benefits. As the number of recipients grows, a more generous payment for each person could mean higher taxes, more borrowing, or less money available for services such as healthcare, which will also face greater demand as the country ages.
A durable public pension should help people afford the essentials, especially those who have few other resources. For households planning their retirement, it makes sense to treat that benefit as a source of basic income and estimate separately how much they would need to maintain the standard of living they want.
Today’s Retirement Rules May Change
Retirement planning also involves a question that is easy to overlook: when will the income actually start?
If people spend more years in retirement, a pension that begins at the same age may have to pay out for longer.
Future governments could respond by changing the age of access to some benefits, requiring longer contribution histories for certain pensions, or revising how payments increase over time. These are possible policy choices. They would depend on the program, the law, and the decisions of future governments.
Any change would affect people differently. A person with a desk job and good health may be able to work a few extra years. Someone in a physically demanding job or caring for a family member may have much less flexibility. The same is true of people who lose their jobs late in their careers.
Personal savings can help cover a gap between the date someone stops earning a salary and the date pension payments begin. That flexibility is valuable even if the rules stay the same.
For most salaried workers in Mexico, an AFORE is the starting point for retirement savings.
Staying in formal employment for as much of your career as you can helps you build your balance through mandatory contributions and accumulate the weeks of contributions that can determine whether you qualify for a lifelong pension.
While those contributions can give you a head start, your retirement expenses may exceed what your AFORE and public benefits can cover. Saving on your own can help close that gap.
A Plan Personal de Retiro , or PPR, offers a way to save beyond mandatory AFORE contributions.
It serves a similar purpose to an individual retirement account, or IRA, in the United States, with its own Mexican tax and withdrawal rules. Its appeal goes beyond the potential tax deduction. Depending on the provider and the plan, a PPR can give savers more control over how their retirement money is invested.
That flexibility matters most when retirement is still decades away. The funds that invest mandatory AFORE savings follow limits set by CONSAR. The maximum share they can hold in stocks ranges from 60% for the youngest group to 15% for the retirement-stage fund. Those limits help reduce exposure to a market downturn near retirement, but they also cap how much savers can participate when stock markets rise. A PPR with a broader investment menu can let a saver put more of their long-term savings in stocks. That can raise the potential for growth over decades, with the trade-off of larger losses in bad years. How much of that growth the saver keeps also depends on the plan’s fees.
The practical question is how much income you will need beyond your AFORE and any public benefit. If you expect to spend MX$40,000 pesos (US$2100) a month in today’s money and estimate that those sources will provide MX$15,000, you have a gap of 25,000 pesos a month to plan for. A PPR can help close it.
For workers who have room to save, starting early gives even modest contributions more time to grow. The public pension can help cover basic needs, while the savings you build over your career give you more say in what you can afford in retirement.
