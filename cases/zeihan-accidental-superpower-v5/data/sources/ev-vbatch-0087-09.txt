Absent congressional action, the U.S. will most likely reach the debt limit in February or March 2027. Cash on hand and extraordinary measures would then allow the Treasury Department to meet all of the federal government’s obligations for roughly six to nine months thereafter before hitting the X Date.
Debt subject to the limit has risen faster than was projected at the time of the last debt limit increase, in part due to lower net tariff receipts than expected.
With the X Date still more than half a year away, its timing remains highly uncertain. Key factors include the level and pace of fiscal year 2027 appropriations (including defense spending), elevated interest costs, tariff collections and refunds, Treasury’s cash management, and economic conditions.
Operating near the borrowing limit undermines investor confidence and limits the nation’s capacity to respond expeditiously to an escalation of the Iran conflict, a sudden economic downturn, or another crisis.
In July 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act increased the federal debt limit by $5 trillion, from $36.1 trillion to $41.1 trillion. Since then, debt subject to the limit has increased by $4.2 trillion—leaving just $764 billion in remaining borrowing authority. At the end of fiscal year 2026, debt subject to the limit was $398 billion above the Congressional Budget Office’s (CBO) projections . Borrowing accelerated in part because net tariff receipts fell short of expectations.
The Bipartisan Policy Center now estimates that, absent congressional action, the U.S. will most likely reach the debt limit once again in February or March 2027 , when large tax refunds typically produce sizable monthly cash flow deficits.
