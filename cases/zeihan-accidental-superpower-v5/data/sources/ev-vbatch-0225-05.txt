Few leaders around the world can claim to win elections more emphatically than Vladimir Putin. North Korea’s Kim Jong Un, whose party in March picked up over 99% of the vote from over 99% of the electorate, is one. Another is Ramzan Kadyrov, leader of the Chechen Republic, who in September was re-elected for a fifth term with 99.85% of the vote, up from 99.7% in 2021.

Kadyrov was re-elected at the same time that United Russia, the pro-Putin party, won a record mandate in the first election since the invasion of Ukraine in 2022. The country’s only anti-war party was blocked by court order from participating. As Russia is Putin’s fiefdom, so the Chechen Republic, nominally a part of the Russian Federation, is Kadyrov’s, with Moscow’s only demand being fealty. In exchange for that loyalty to Putin, the Chechen dictator gets to do what he likes, particularly to dissidents whom he routinely jails, tortures and kills, say human rights organizations.
It could have been different. In 1991, amidst the collapse of the Soviet Union, there was a Chechen revolution, leading to a partial breakaway and the creation of the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria. Recognised by no one except neighbouring Georgia, the rebel republic, led by Dzokhar Dudayev, managed to hold Russia at bay. The First Chechen War (1994 – 1996) ended in a stalemate, with Russian troops departing only to re-enter in 1999, sparking nearly a decade of conflict which ended with Chechnya back in the Russian fold and Kadyrov, whose father had switched sides to support Putin, bankrolled by the Kremlin and able to crush the opposition.
Akhmed Zakayev was the Minister of Culture in Dudayev’s rebel government back in the early 1990s. Tall, grizzled, charismatic, he is the closest thing there is to political continuity with the Chechen Republic of Ichkeria. Now in his mid-sixties, the self-declared Prime Minister of Ichkeria’s government-in-exile seems an unlikely threat to Vladimir Putin’s imperial pretensions. Nonetheless, in September, as Kadyrov claimed his election victory in Chechnya, it was reported that Zakayev was the target of a Russian assassination plot.
Months earlier, in March, this alleged plot was foreshadowed in a story about the arrest of a Russian agent in Colombia. He had been stopped at an airport at the behest of U.S. authorities, leading to the uncovering of a squad of assassins that had been formed in December 2022 and tasked with killing Putin’s opponents around the world. Among the highest priority targets were Zakayev and his family. The Kremlin’s fixation on Zakayev was also evident in April, 2026, when the Russian Foreign Ministry accused Germany of threatening national security after a German official met with him in Kyiv.
Watching Putin fraternise in Shenzhen, China with Xi Jinping and Donald Trump, ahead of a trilateral summit on November 19 that appeared to be a self-conscious reprisal of the Yalta Conference, with the Big Three carving up the world between them, you could understand why a figure like Zakayev represents a threat — his life represents a rejection of great power politics, and the questioning of Russia’s notions about its own sovereignty.
In March, before word of the assassination attempt came to light, I met Zakayev in his office in Kyiv. A portrait of Dzhokhar Dudayev in military uniform stares down at us from a wall.
