Libya's political landscape remains deeply fractured, yet diplomatic momentum has quietly quickened. UNSMIL's Structured Dialogue concluded in June 2026 with nearly 600 governance and electoral recommendations. In addition, UNSMIL’s 4+4 committee has called for election within 18-24 months and the reconstitution of the electoral commissions, actions that were approved by the House of Representatives (HoR) but not the High Council of State (HCS). These are meaningful steps forward, though unresolved electoral-law disputes and competing political interests mean that institutional unification — agreed to in principle — remains fragile and contested in practice.
Libya’s economy remains heavily dependent on hydrocarbons, which accounted for more than 60 percent of GDP and 90 percent of exports and government revenues, leaving the country highly exposed to production and global price shocks. Weak fiscal discipline has worsened macroeconomic instability. Increasing public spending depleted foreign reserves by USD 8 billion annually during 2023-2025. As a result, the Central Bank of Libya (CBL) tightened access to foreign exchange and devalued the Libyan Dinar (LYD) twice in less than a year (April 2025 and January 2026) to control the widening gap between official and parallel market exchange rates.
The economy is characterized by a large public sector, state-owned enterprises, extensive subsidies, and informality, with limited private sector job creation. Reforms in public financial management, subsidy rationalization, energy sector efficiency, and financial modernization remain critical. Risks stem from deepening political frictions, resumption of conflict, oil disruptions, instability, and climate shocks, while upside potential lies in sustained oil stability, reconstruction, and structural reforms.
Institutional fragmentation continues to undermine fiscal discipline despite a landmark step forward. The HoR and the HCS agreed in April 2026 to Libya's first unified state budget since 2013 — worth 190 billion dinars (approximately US$30 billion, or 76 percent of GDP). Social conditions have deteriorated markedly as worsening electricity and fuel crises — culminating in a total blackout that cascaded into water supply disruptions — alongside fuel scarcity and cash liquidity shortages, weigh heavily on Libyans across the country.
In 2025, the economy grew at a robust rate of 13.4 percent, supported by strong performance in oil and non-oil sectors, recovering from a slowdown in 2024 caused by the Central Bank of Libya (CBL) governance crisis, which disrupted oil output and kept overall GDP growth to 1.9 percent. Oil production extended its multi-year recovery, averaging 1.3 mbpd by mid-2026, though output remains below pre-2010 levels. This improvement was driven by ongoing field rehabilitation across the Sirte and Waha basins and renewed international investment.
The Government of National Unity’s (GNU) operations registered a surplus of 9.4 percent of GDP during the first eight months of 2026—4.3 percent higher compared to the same period in 2025. Revenues increased by 17 percent in nominal terms, driven by growing hydrocarbon and tax revenues (16 and 96 percent, respectively). Estimated total public spending increased by 5 percent (y-o-y), following a 25 percent increase in wages and a 51 percent decrease in subsidies.
The trade balance remained comfortably in surplus in January–April 2026 at 8.6 percent of GDP (0.7 percentage points above the same period in 2025), reflecting higher exports and lower imports. Merchandise exports rose 2 percent, driven mainly by elevated oil prices following the Strait of Hormuz closure, while imports fell 2 percent as access to foreign exchange and trade financing tightened. However, unchecked public spending and rising parallel-market foreign-exchange demand widened the exchange-rate premium, which averaged 42 percent in the first half of 2026, despite the CBL’s US$2 billion intervention in April to narrow the spread.
The weaker Libyan Dinar and the higher costs of imported goods have fed through to a marked 11.1 percent (y-o-y) pickup in consumer prices during the first half of 2026. Tighter access to trade financing led to episodic shortages of some essential consumer goods, such as diesel, cooking oil, and flour, in the eastern and southern regions of Libya, and quantity rationing in Tripoli during the month of Ramadan.
Looking ahead, and assuming oil production continues to average 1.3 mbpd throughout 2026—consistent with the average observed during the first half of 2026—oil sector growth is anticipated to reach 0.8 percent. With non-oil GDP growth expected to grow by 2.5 percent - supported primarily by private consumption - overall GDP growth would reach 1.4 percent. Over the medium term, continued recovery in oil output (to 1.35 and 1.4 mbpd in 2027 and 2028, respectively) is projected to lift overall growth to an average of 2.5 percent.
Launched in December 2023 with Germany and the United Kingdom, the Libya Development Trust Fund (LDTF) provides a strategic coordination framework for the World Bank Group and its development partners, drawing on two decades of engagement with Libya. By pooling funding and aligning objectives, the LDTF enhances donor harmonization, policy dialogue, and shared risk management.
Political fragmentation limits public debate and excludes large segments of the population — particularly youth — from discussions on socioeconomic priorities. In June 2024, the LDTF partnered with Luiss Guido Carli University and the Mediterranean Universities Union to launch the Youth Economic Research Platform, creating an inclusive space for young Libyans to engage on their country's economic development trajectory.
Following the devastating September 2023 floods in eastern Libya, the World Bank joined the European Union and United Nations in developing a Rapid Disaster and Needs Assessment (RDNA). Building on this, the World Bank — together with the Netherlands, UNICEF, and the International Commission for Large Dams — assessed the failures of the Wadi Derna Dam to inform nationwide dam safety recommendations.
