Mexico’s FDI Hits Record as New Investment Falls 42% English
Mexico attracted US$43.09 billion in foreign direct investment (FDI) between January and September 2026, a record for the first nine months of a year. However, new investments fell 42.1% year over year to US$3.8 billion, while reinvested earnings reached US$34.98 billion, accounting for 81.2% of total inflows. The figures point to continued expansion by companies already operating in Mexico, alongside a decline in capital entering through new projects.
The Ministry of Economy described the results as evidence of Mexico’s position in international investment and the impact of Plan México. The figures, however, show that the record was largely supported by existing foreign-owned companies retaining earnings in the country rather than by new investors establishing operations.
According to the ministry’s data, total FDI increased 5.3% compared with the US$40.91 billion originally reported for January-September 2025. Reinvested earnings grew 26%, while intercompany accounts declined 34.6% to US$4.32 billion. New investments represented just 8.8% of the total, down from 16% in the comparable period of 2025.
The distinction between these components is relevant when assessing investment momentum. Reinvested earnings indicate that foreign companies are maintaining or expanding their existing operations, but new investment provides a more direct measure of capital entering the country through new ventures, acquisitions or expansions classified under that category.
Manufacturing remained the largest recipient of FDI, attracting US$19.23 billion, equivalent to 44.6% of total inflows and a 26.6% annual increase. Within the sector, new investment rose 58.8% to US$1.12 billion. The increase was associated primarily with transport equipment manufacturing, where new investment rose 999.3%, and computer equipment and electronic components, which increased 605.2%, according to the Ministry of Economy’s figures reported by El Economista .
The manufacturing figures suggest that investment is not declining uniformly across industries. Demand linked to electronics, computing and transport equipment continues to support capital allocation, particularly in activities integrated into North American production networks.
Financial and insurance services ranked second, receiving US$10.64 billion, or 24.7% of total FDI. Transport, postal services and warehousing attracted US$3.10 billion, representing 7.2% of the total and an annual increase of 59.3%.
The United States remained Mexico’s largest source of investment, contributing US$19.33 billion, or 44.9% of the total. Spain followed with US$6.21 billion, Germany with US$2.90 billion, the United Kingdom with US$2.59 billion and Canada with US$2.45 billion. Together, the United States and Canada accounted for 50.5% of inflows, reflecting the integration of production and trade between Mexico and its North American partners.
Investment was concentrated geographically. Mexico City received US$19.60 billion, equivalent to 45.5% of the national total. Nuevo Leon attracted US$4.43 billion, followed by Jalisco with US$2.71 billion, Baja California with US$2.36 billion and Puebla with US$1.75 billion. These five entities accounted for 71.6% of reported FDI.
The figures come as companies assess the conditions for establishing or expanding operations in Mexico ahead of the review of the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA). In September, Reuters reported that uncertainty over trade policy and domestic reforms was influencing investment decisions, with some manufacturers reconsidering the timing or location of projects. The decline in new investment reported for the first nine months of 2026 adds to the relevance of those concerns.
Mexico’s policy response includes Plan México, which seeks to attract investment, increase domestic production and strengthen supplier networks. The OECD’s 2026 Latin American Economic Outlook identifies the country’s development hubs, industrial parks and incentives for investment in strategic sectors such as semiconductors, electromobility, pharmaceuticals, medical devices and aerospace as part of this approach.
Regulatory changes will also influence investment decisions. On Nov. 19, 2026, Mexico published amendments to its Foreign Investment Law establishing a national security review process for certain foreign acquisitions.
