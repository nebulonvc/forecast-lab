Limited Economic Growth Risks Mexico’s Pension Solvency
The Mexican Institute of Financial Executives ( IMEF ) warns that Mexico's current economic growth is insufficient to generate public revenue needed to cover future formal pension obligations. Annual pension expenditures represent 6% of GDP and will reach 9% by 2030.
The growth of non-discretionary commitments is straining public funds, with pension payments rising faster than fiscal revenue.
According to figures from the Ministry of Finance and Public Credit ( SHCP ), public revenues grew by 1.3% annually between January and August, while pension expenditures increased by 4.5% and social subsidies rose by 9.5%.
"Today, the annual expenditure on formal pensions represents 6% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP), but by 2030 it will reach 9% of GDP," says Gabriela Gutiérrez, President, IMEF. "By that year, this item alone will exceed MX$4 trillion, which represents 40% of the total revenue budget for 2027. Currently, there is not enough economic growth to generate sufficient revenue to cover this future obligation."
Demographic shifts in Mexico are increasing fiscal pressures on the pension system. Data from the National Institute of Statistics and Geography (INEGI) indicates that the median age in Mexico rose from 29 years in 2020 to 32 years in 2025. During the same period, the national fertility rate declined from 2.1 to 1.9 children per woman, leading to an aging population structure and a smaller relative base of younger formal workers.
Structural labor market conditions complicate this demographic transition. The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development ( OECD ) notesd that coverage gaps remain a challenge, as approximately 55% of Mexico’s 60 million informal workers do not participate in the private pension administrator ( Afore ) system. Career interruptions in informal employment reduce total pension contributions, limiting the self-sustainability of retirement accounts.
The expanding fiscal burden comes as the federal government attempts to reduce its budget deficit. SHCP presented preliminary economic policy guidelines targeting a reduction in the fiscal deficit from 4.1% of GDP to 3.5% through a MX$275.20 billion (US$14.7 billion) contraction in programmable spending, reports according to Mexico Business News . However, ratings agency HR Ratings downgraded Mexico's debt rating to " BBB " with a Stable outlook, citing structural rigidities in public spending, mandatory transfers, and sustained deficits above 4% of GDP over five consecutive years.
IMEF projects that without annual economic growth exceeding 2.5% and structural expenditure realignments, maintaining sovereign investment-grade status will become difficult before 2030. Mandatory entitlement growth, combined with rising public debt service costs, reduces available capital for public infrastructure and productive investments required to sustain long-term growth.
Mexico faces a growing fiscal challenge as formal pension outlays are projected to expand from 6% of GDP to 9% by 2030, exceeding expected economic growth and public revenue generation. Demographic shifts, including an aging population and declining birth rates, combined with persistent informal employment, exacerbate the structural deficit risk. Managing this entitlement growth is critical for public finance, institutional investors, and labor markets to maintain sovereign stability, budget solvency, and financial sector confidence.
