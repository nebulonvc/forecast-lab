Last week, Indian Defense Secretary Rajesh Kumar Singh said that New Delhi had successfully shipped its Akash air-defense system to Tajikistan and Turkmenistan. While neither country has publicly announced the deals, and details like the number of systems, delivery dates, and prices remain secret, the sale gives the two Central Asian states an alternative to the Russian systems that have in the past dominated their arsenals. Indian media previously reported that Armenia ordered 15 Akash systems in 2022 for around $720 million, though neither government has officially confirmed that number. Akash is a mobile surface-to-air missile system built to shoot down planes, drones, and missiles. For decades, Central Asia’s air defenses have relied almost entirely on Soviet and Russian equipment, with Moscow providing the hardware, training, and maintenance. Alliances such as the Russian-led Collective Security Treaty Organization (CSTO) further cemented Russia's role as the region’s primary security provider. Pavel Baev, a research professor at the Peace Research Institute Oslo (PRIO), sees these new purchases in Central Asia as a clear political signal to Moscow. “My impression is that this...is more about making a point than about real strategic capabilities,” Baev said. Tajikistan is a CSTO member and hosts a major Russian military base. Turkmenistan, on the other hand, is officially neutral. "Both [countries] are eager to signal to Moscow that its dominance based on the capacity to project power and provide security for the regimes is over,” Baev said. Beneath the political message, there is also a very practical problem: With Western sanctions and Ukrainian strikes squeezing its defense industry, Russia is increasingly unable to supply and maintain the equipment Central Asian militaries rely on. Data published by the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI), a body that tracks global arms transfers, shows that Russian arms exports fell by 64 percent, when comparing the periods 2016–20 and 2021–25, cutting Russia’s share of global arms exports from 21 percent to 6.8 percent.
