A plane landed, and an alarming rumor took off. On August 25, Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) Director John Ratcliffe flew to Moscow for an unannounced visit. One reported reason for the trip was to warn Russian leaders against attacking NATO allies. Russia has long carried out hybrid warfare across Europe in the form of sabotage, misinformation, and cyberattacks. It is also continuing its war on Ukraine, a conflict that has already spilled over into other countries in the form of drones and missiles. Yet Ratcliffe’s unexpected visit has raised new concerns and drawn comparisons to then CIA chief Bill Burns’s trip to Moscow shortly before Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine.
Below, Atlantic Council experts go point-by-point across the map to assess where and how Russian President Vladimir Putin might escalate next.
Justina Budginaite-Froehly , PhD, is a nonresident senior fellow with the Atlantic Council’s Europe Center and Transatlantic Security Initiative within the Scowcroft Center for Strategy and Security.
Russia is consistently testing the boundaries of what it can do against the Baltic states and their allies without provoking a decisive response. The pattern of actions linked to Russia ranges from drone incursions and sabotage of critical infrastructure to arson , assassination plots , cyber operations, and GPS jamming and spoofing .
Moscow is likely to keep pushing until it faces credible costs. While Russia remains materially consumed by its war against Ukraine, that does not necessarily make it less dangerous. It makes Russia more willing to bring the war closer to Ukraine’s staunchest supporters in an effort to reduce their backing. If Putin grows desperate about his stalled war effort while perceiving political uncertainty within NATO, he could conclude that escalation offers a way to alter the strategic trajectory of the war in Ukraine.
That is why the Baltic states are preparing across the spectrum of conflict. They are aware that the boundary between a “hybrid” operation and conventional aggression can be extremely thin: sabotage, cyberattacks, electronic warfare, and disinformation can be ends in themselves, but they can also serve as shaping operations for subsequent military action. Whether escalation proceeds largely depends on what Moscow learns from the initial NATO response. Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia are therefore strengthening national defenses and improving interoperability with reinforcing allies. They are also pressing NATO to treat repeated sub-threshold attacks as elements of a coherent Russian strategy to identify and exploit seams in national and allied defenses.
Ultimately, the target of any Russian escalation in the Baltics would be NATO itself. Moscow has long sought to weaken the Alliance and undermine confidence that collective defense will work when tested. Deterrence must therefore leave no ambiguity that even a geographically limited attack would bring the full weight of the Alliance into play. NATO’s recent Kaliningrad-focused maneuver was useful signaling in this respect, demonstrating allied readiness close to one of Russia’s most militarized outposts. But ad hoc signaling needs to be backed by everyday allied consistency. A strong allied forward presence, underpinned by the return of urgently needed US military deployments , is therefore central to deterring Russia. Stability in the Baltics is a strategic interest for the United States: a successful Russian test of NATO would cripple the credibility of US-led alliances far beyond Europe.
Aaron Korewa is the director of the Atlantic Council’s Warsaw Office, which is part of the Atlantic Council’s Europe Center.
In Poland, the possibility of Russia expanding the war beyond Ukraine is treated seriously, simply because the Poles cannot afford not to do so. Russia has already conducted hostile operations here. The most serious case was in September 2025, when nineteen Russian-made drones penetrated Polish airspace in what most believe to have been a deliberate test of Poland’s defensive capabilities. Add to that the Russian armed cruise missile that crashed in eastern Poland in June this year, the 2024 arson attack on a shopping mall in Warsaw, which Polish prosecutors concluded was commissioned by Russian intelligence, and the November 2025 sabotage of the Warsaw–Lublin railway. Poland is preparing for all kinds of threats. Recently, Minister of National Defence Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz warned that, as Polish–Ukrainian relations deteriorate, Russia could use captured Ukrainian drones in false-flag operations.    One also cannot rule out a larger conventional attack from Kaliningrad and/or Belarus (effectively a Russian satrapy). This explains Poland’s Eastern Shield border defense concept and the enormous modernization of its armed forces, with defense spending at 4.8 percent of gross domestic product (GDP), the highest in NATO. Poland is no longer a lightly armed eastern frontline state waiting for NATO to rescue it. Rather, it possesses significant national capabilities for the opening phase of a conflict, including Abrams and K2 tanks, HIMARS and Chunmoo rocket artillery, Apache attack helicopters coming into the force, large artillery formations, F-16 and F-35 aircraft, and rapidly expanding drone capabilities. Impressive as this is, the Polish armed forces are still a work in progress. They are also not battle-tested.
Poland aims to provide the first line of defense itself, but a sustained conflict would require NATO reinforcement in critical areas, particularly air defense. NATO maintains forward forces in Poland, and the multinational battlegroup can expand by thousands of troops to brigade strength. The approximately ten thousand American soldiers stationed in Poland are particularly important. The bottom line is that the Poles can handle themselves, but their strategy ultimately depends on making sure that any Russian attack on Poland immediately becomes a war with NATO.

For the Nordic states, Russia is the most significant, direct, and long-term threat to Euro-Atlantic security. As all Nordic and Baltic states underlined at an NB8 meeting in Estonia in April, this requires “ urgent, determined and coordinated action .” For more than a decade, Russia has targeted the Nordics with hybrid tactics—from cyberespionage targeting Norway’s parliament to instrumentalized migration that prompted Finland to close its eastern border and GPS jamming that disrupted ambulance navigation in Kalmar County and Gotland.
Russia’s near-constant hybrid warfare has prompted the Nordic countries to develop their Cold War-era total defense structures and put societies on alert through information campaigns and resilience measures. In parallel, the Nordics are investing in both their defense industrial bases and overall militaries, with defense spending ranging from 2.5 percent to 3.34 percent of their respective GDPs in 2025 (excluding Iceland, which does not maintain a standing army). They also lead in support to Ukraine as a share of GDP, which they see as an investment in regional security.
