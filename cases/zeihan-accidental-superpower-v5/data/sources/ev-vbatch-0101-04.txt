The ‘Zapad’ exercise and how Lukashenko learned to love the Bomb By Gabriela Rosa-Hernández , Decker Eveleth | Analysis | November 6, 2025
Belarusian President Alexander Lukashenko comments on the location of maneuvers in Belarusian territory ahead of the "Zapad-2025" joint Russian-Belarusian strategic military exercise during an interview with Time magazine on July 25, 2025. (Credit: Andrei Stasevich / Belarusian Telegraph Agency)
Copy link Linked copied Email Facebook Bluesky Twitter LinkedIn WhatsApp Reddit Spread In September, Russia conducted a strategic military exercise known as “Zapad-25.” The Zapad series exercises have been held regularly since the 1970s. They are Russia’s largest military exercises intended to both practice theatre-level military operations and conduct geopolitical signaling to communicate the costs and potential escalation risks of a NATO-Russia conflict. This year, Zapad was especially anticipated by analysts. It was the first iteration of the exercise since Russia and Belarus announced their nuclear sharing arrangement and Russia updated its public official deterrence policy—the two most significant changes in Russia’s nuclear posture since Russia started its full-scale invasion of Ukraine.
Media and officials in Belarus made numerous statements before the beginning of the exercise, alluding to Zapad-25 as involving some showstopping nuclear exercises that would showcase Belarus and Russia’s new nuclear ties. But either these exercises did not occur or Belarusian and Russian media simply did not show them, subverting Western expectations. The lack of imagery could be explained by Russia or Belarus being unsure whether to show sensitive operational details. But that does not explain the sudden change in public rhetoric. Instead, the shift in the public statements about Zapad suggests that the Belarusian-Russian relationship may be more complicated than it appears, with Minsk having to balance between its desire for autonomy and its dependence on Russia’s security guarantees.
A nuclear component. In the run-up to the Zapad exercise, Belarusian officials leveraged nuclear rhetoric to frame Russian nuclear deployments in Belarus as a response to regional threats. Meanwhile, Moscow neither confirmed nor denied that the exercise would involve a nuclear component. Belarusian Minister of Defense Viktor Khrenin announced in August that the exercise would include Moscow and Minsk working out the planning for the employment of nuclear weapons and the new mobile nuclear-capable Oreshnik ballistic missile system, which Belarusian President Alexander Lukashenko recently announced will be deployed in Belarus by the end of the year. Khrenin also noted that nuclear weapons could inflict unacceptable damage against a potential adversary. He suggested that the exercise would serve as a vehicle for Russia and Belarus to practice operationalizing Russia’s new declaratory policy, as Zapad exercises generally feature counterattacks against NATO attacks into Belarusian or Russian territory.
A few weeks later, Belarusian Deputy Defense Minister Pavel Muraveiko stated that, as part of the exercise , Russia and Belarus were planning for all types of weapons. This announcement allowed Minsk to portray independence and urgency in its rhetoric about perceived threats. Russia may also have quietly benefited from the signaling effect of Belarusian statements, which highlighted the expansion of Russia’s nuclear posture in Belarus and the forward deployment of dual-capable systems, strengthening deterrence against a common threat.
Minsk drew attention to the recent actions of Polish military leadership: Just a few days before the announcement, Poland announced that it would increase the core size of its military to 300,000 soldiers, with the ability to increase to 500,000 if necessary. Khrenin directly referred to this statement in his remarks about the Russian-Belarusian nuclear sharing arrangement, noting that “most alarming of all is the decision of the Polish military leadership to create a group of more than 30-34 thousand troops.” He added, “This is already a serious grouping, in our opinion. We need to look very carefully (we will do this) and react. If they show some kind of aggression towards the Republic of Belarus, we have something to respond with.”
This episode built on longstanding Russian and Belarusian concerns about a deteriorating regional security environment. Even before the nuclear-sharing arrangement, President Alexander Lukashenko had publicly offered in 2022 to host Russian nuclear weapons if the United States relocated its nuclear weapons from Germany to Eastern Europe. For its part, Poland has been a staunch supporter of Ukraine and has expressed interest in hosting US nuclear weapons on its territory.
Russian strategists have also linked the nuclear-sharing arrangement to the perceived vulnerability of Kaliningrad and the idea of a possible proxy-war in Belarus, given the deployment of new US and NATO forces along the Alliance’s eastern flank. In 2024, Lieutenant General Igor Kolesnikov, head of the 12th GUMO—the Russian military formation responsible for the safekeeping of Russia’s nuclear arsenal—and the man in charge of operationalizing the Moscow-Minsk nuclear sharing arrangement, and retired Major General Vyacheslav Kruglov argued that Russia and Belarus must adopt a set of nuclear countermeasures to deter evolving threats.
What happened? Despite all the signaling, the 2025 edition of Zapad turned out to be significantly smaller and largely focused on conventional systems. Although it was underpinned by similar threat perceptions , it was a very different exercise compared to previous theatre-level exercises such as the 2021 edition .
The scenario underpinning this year’s Zapad is consistent with previous iterations of the exercise: A fictional adversary attempts to destabilize a fictional country through covert means, then employs a fast and decisive air attack to paralyze the military and destroy objects of critical infrastructure. According to Deputy Defense Minister Muraveiko, the Russian and Belarusian militaries focused on “repelling enemy attacks” and conducting counter-offensive operations to “restore the territorial integrity of the state.”
This planning scenario highlighted the importance of what the Russians called the “initial period of war,” defined by the threat posed by NATO stand-off range precision munitions in Russian military thinking, and many Russian military elites contend that a NATO-Russia conflict would be decided during this short period. Russian and Belarusian arsenals of dual-capable missile and air systems are critical for repelling fast-paced NATO attacks utilizing conventional precision weapons. Moreover, like previous exercises, the dual-capable systems featured prominently during this year’s Zapad, but in different ways.
Still, Zapad-25 did not appear to go much further beyond that. Or if it did, Belarus and Russia seem to have been hesitant to show it. Videos and media reports on the exercises contained very little in the way of operations involving nuclear-capable systems and broke from the previous trend of Russia and Belarus being increasingly explicit about the presence and operationalization of nuclear weapons in the country.
During the exercise, the Russian Ministry of Defense showed footage of electronic launches by crews operating the Iskander-M mobile short-range ballistic missile system in Kaliningrad. These included reloading to highlight the readiness of these crews to pave the way for Russian bombers by striking air-defense systems. But the warheads of the missiles were not blurred, unlike joint-training videos, and featured a white stripe around the warhead. This suggests that the crews may have had simple conventional training forms, not real warheads. (During the Russo-Belarusian nuclear exercises of the summer of 2024 , Russian and Belarusian media blurred out warheads in released videos to suggest that the missiles were armed with real nuclear warheads that may have classified external features.)
More broadly, Russian Tu-160 strategic missile carriers and Tu-22M3 long-range bombers rehearsed cruise missile strikes against the facilities of the mock adversary during patrols over the Barents Sea this year. But long-range aviation played a similar role as during the 2021 Zapad exercise.
