In February, Abdullah Öcalan — the long-imprisoned leader of the Kurdistan Workers’ Party (PKK) — called on the group to lay down its arms and “dissolve itself” after more than 40 years of fighting Turkish forces. In an initial step, the PKK called for a ceasefire on March 1. In addition to significant implications for politics and security in Turkey, the development affects Kurds in Syria, Iraq, and Iran. Shifts in regional power structures over the last few months played a role in Öcalan’s decision, which in turn will help shape the geopolitics of the Middle East. We asked four experts to lay out how the PKK’s ceasefire and potential disbanding affects Turkey and its neighbors.Read more below.
Gönül Tol
Director of the Turkish Studies Program at the Middle East Institute
If the PKK cadres heed Öcalan’s call and dissolve the organization, it would hand President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan a major political victory ahead of the 2028 elections — potentially reshaping Turkey’s political landscape for decades to come. By securing both Kurdish and Turkish nationalist support, Erdoğan could overcome the biggest obstacle to extending his rule: the constitutional limit preventing him from running again.
At the heart of Erdoğan’s talks with Öcalan is not a genuine effort for peace but a calculated bid for survival. Turkey’s economy is struggling, Erdoğan’s support is waning, and legal barriers stand in the way of another term. A deal that dissolves the PKK could deliver him Kurdish backing in parliament, easing his path to rewriting the rules once again. Turkish nationalists — long fixated on eliminating the PKK — would rally behind him.
The true cost of another Erdoğan term is clear: further democratic backsliding. Without meaningful reforms to address Turkey’s eroding democratic institutions, the end of the PKK will not bring peace — it will cement Erdoğan’s autocracy, narrowing political freedoms for both Turks and Kurds alike.
Aron Lund
Fellow at Century International
Middle East Analyst at the Swedish Defense Research Agency
There are many moving parts to this problem, both in Turkey and in Syria. In particular, no one seems to know how Öcalan’s missive will apply to the PKK’s appendages in Syria like the Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF).
So far, SDF leaders have respectfully ignored the letter and said it was not for them. They’re keeping their guard up while negotiating a new modus vivendi with the Ankara-backed Islamists in Damascus — a deal announced on March 12 was more of a photo op than a solution. The clock is ticking though, since a U.S. troop pullout would leave them extremely vulnerable.
The SDF isn’t exactly a PKK branch, but it’s PKK-led and depends on the PKK’s Syrian network to function. Should that network disappear, disarm, or split, the SDF could unravel. Then again, a slackening of Turkey’s hostility to the PKK could facilitate SDF–Damascus accommodation.
Denise Natali
Director, Institute for National Strategic Studies, National Defense University
The ceasefire could halt armed conflict between PKK terrorists and the Turkish military in the Kurdistan Region of Iraq and enhance stability. This situation — alongside other incentives — could expand trade, energy, and business development, as well as political cooperation between Iraq, Syria, and Turkey. The ceasefire could also enable or revive partnerships between certain Kurdish groups in Syria and Iraq that are aligned with Turkey. Yet, like the nine ceasefires the PKK has declared with Turkey over the past 32 years, this ceasefire will not result in the PKK unconditionally disarming, disavowing violence, or leaving the Qandil Mountains or other Iraqi territories anytime soon. Hardliner PKK factions or affiliates will act as spoilers. Like dozens of other militia groups that have captured the Iraqi and Syrian states, they will continue to benefit from state weakness and external support from Iran, advance their parochial interests, and fuel local power struggles.

Alex Vatanka
Senior Fellow at the Middle East Institute
Iran views Turkey’s PKK peace deal through the lens of its broader rivalry with Ankara. While officially supportive, Tehran privately sees it as a blow. For decades, both states have weaponized Kurdish groups against each other. Furthermore, the PKK deal comes after five years of Turkey outmaneuvering Iran in the South Caucasus, Africa, and Syria — now, Iraq is the next battleground. For example, Turkey’s Development Road project, linking Basra to Mersin, is more likely to happen if the PKK can no longer act as a spoiler. If the project proceeds, it would shift Iraq further into Ankara’s orbit, undermining Iran’s economic ambitions in Iraq. Though Iran still holds much political sway in Baghdad, Turkey could become Iraq’s top economic partner, especially as the United States seeks to curb Tehran’s reach. The Trump administration’s recent decision to revoke a sanctions waiver that allowed Iraq to buy energy from Iran has already resulted in Iraq increasing energy imports from Turkey and Saudi Arabia. Iran won’t give up its influence in Iraq easily but must now juggle escalating tensions with Turkey alongside its high-stakes conflicts with the United States and Israel.
