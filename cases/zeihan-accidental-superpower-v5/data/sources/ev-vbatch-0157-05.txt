Even though Canada is a net exporter of natural gas, Canada does still import natural gas, primarily from the U.S. to supply central Canada and the Maritimes. Footnote 35 In 2025, Canada imported an average of 2.5 Bcf/d of natural gas, including LNG imports, representing 19% of the country’s total natural gas demand. Figure 2 shows the major natural gas import points in central Canada and the Maritimes. These points receive U.S.-produced natural gas and also re‑import natural gas that originated in western Canada but was transported through the U.S. via transmission pipelines.

Natural gas imports increased significantly in the early 2010s, largely due to the rise of low-cost supply from the Appalachian Basin in the U.S. Footnote 36 This shale gas displaced some western Canadian natural gas supply in central Canada, as well as export markets in the U.S., changing traditional export patterns and reducing eastbound flows on the TC Canadian Mainline. This new U.S. gas supply could often be delivered to central Canada and the Maritimes at lower cost than gas from western Canada because of its closer geographic location and competitive pricing. In the early 2010s, rising pipeline tolls for western Canadian gas heading east contributed to this shift, as long-haul flows on the TC Canadian Mainline declined.

Since around 2019, growth in U.S. imports has levelled off, and shipments of western Canadian gas to central Canada have increased. Between 2021 and 2025, flows from Alberta to Ontario on the TC Canadian Mainline rose by 59%. Footnote 37 This change indicates that western Canadian gas became more competitive over this period. From 2021 to 2025, western Canadian NIT prices were generally lower than Appalachian prices, with a difference of more than $2/GJ on average in 2025. Lower long-haul transportation costs on the TC Canadian Mainline, due to toll restructuring, also supported this shift. As a result, U.S. imports have fallen slightly but remain an important part of the supply mix. Limited spare pipeline capacity to move additional western Canadian gas to central Canada means imports from the U.S. are still necessary to meet the region’s demand.

Several points that once primarily exported gas began transitioning to become bi-directional as U.S. production surged in the late 2000s. Since 2012, Ontario has increasingly relied on U.S. imports via the Mainline, driven by rising output from the Appalachian Basin. Niagara began importing in 2012, followed by Chippawa in 2015, after both were converted to bi-directional service to accommodate Marcellus Shale gas flows. TC Energy also enabled bi-directional capability at Iroquois in 2017; while it remains primarily an export point, intermittent imports have occurred since 2018. As a result, throughout the 2000s and 2010s, flows of western Canadian natural gas on the Northern Ontario Line generally declined.

Flows have continued to evolve in recent years. Figure 9 below illustrates how flows reflect the relative economics of supply sources alongside seasonal demand patterns. Between 2021 and 2025, total flows across the four key points increased from an annual average of 2.5 Bcf/d to 3.1 Bcf/d, representing a 25% increase. Over this period, deliveries on the Northern Ontario Line accounted for a growing share of total volumes, rising from 46% to 60%.
