## Abstract

In 1660, the Earl of Clarendon urged the MPs of the Convention Parliament to turn the page on the old names of 'Cavalier, and Round-Head, and Malignant', and practice the 'Excellent Art of Forgetfulness'. 1 He was frustrated by their slow progress in ratifying The Act of Indemnity and Oblivion, which laid out the terms of the king's amnesty, and legally defined the Restoration as a threshold of memory. Many of the public poems written about Charles's return address this threshold: they interpret and narrativize the imperative to forget and move on and reflect on their own agency in periodising collective memory. But in doing so, they come up against problems implicit in Clarendon's paradoxical 'Art of Forgetfulness': if the return to the status quo ante is an art, then how can one practice this art without reminding oneself of what is to be forgotten, namely the interregnum? And if the effacement of the interregnum is indeed a piece of art, is the Restoration of monarchical culture that rests on this fiction not also an artifice, something made not by God or nature, but people? The Act of Indemnity and Oblivion was designed to be a key component in the Restoration settlement. 2 As the political future of the three kingdoms hung in the balance during the winter of 1659-1660, Charles and his advisors, in dialogue with General Monck, developed a platform for the Restoration designed to be of broad political appeal, which included a tolerant religious settlement and indemnity for those who had supported or profited from the interregnum regimes. This was set out in the 4th April Declaration of Breda, which promised 'a free and general Pardon' and hoped that 'all Notes of Discord, Separation, and Difference of Parties, [would] be utterly abolished among all Our Subjects'. 3 The Declaration tactfully left it to Parliament to determine the details of the act. The oblivion clause of the act was its most provisional, and only aimed to supress that language for 'the space of three yeares'. 29 More fundamentally, Ross Poole argues that early modern acts of oblivion were not intended to erase the memory of war, so much as change the agency of that memory. 30 Discussing the relationship between collective memory and acts of oblivion, he draws on a philosophical distinction between the cognitive and the conative: the cognitive aspect of memory transmits information, but the conative aspect transmits affect and will, and directs us to responsibilities and actions. Poole argues that in normal circumstances, the marking of a crime conatively conveys a responsibility for the appropriate officials to discover the perpetrator and bring them to justice, but an act of oblivion or amnesty blocks this imperative. Collective memory can continue to operate cognitively to provide information about the past, and it may even be helpful for it to do so, but it does not prompt justice to action. This also helps to show why a crime subject to pardon and a crime subject to oblivion are different: in the former case, guilt has been established, but in the latter, the break with the normal course of law occurs earlier in the process, before discovery and judgement. 31 The allegory largely depersonalises the conflicts of the recent past, by reducing them to abstract, ahistorical forces. To his Sacred Majesty, A Panegyric on the Coronation develops a neoclassical decorum which speaks to the challenge of moving on from the past. The poem begins with a backward-looking biblical simile, which apologises for the unfinished business which delayed the coronation until May 1661: even after finding land, Noah needed to wait for it to dry out, for the sun to evaporate all damps and vapours, just as Charles waits for the new year lest 'Some guilty Moneths had in your triumphs shar'd' (l.18).49 Like Cowley and Waller, Dryden foregrounds art's role in the construction of new historical perspectives. The present displaces the past like the illusionistic scene-change of a festive pastoral masque: Now our sad ruines are remov'd from sight, The Season too comes fraught with new delight: Time seems not now beneath his years to stoop Nor do his wings with sickly feathers droop: Soft western winds waft ore the gaudy spring And opend Scenes of flow'rs and blossoms bring To grace this happy day, while you appear Not king of us alone but of the year. (ll. 25-32) In the context of the business which had delayed the coronation, the 'ruines' of the interregnum might suggest the trials of regicides and perhaps the interim Convention Parliament too, which would shortly be replaced by the Cavalier Parliament. But the ruins also suggest structures that went to ruin in the wars and interregnum, and it is in keeping with the poem's openness to political compromise that these are to be tidied away rather than restored. 50 Recalling Shakespeare's The Winter's Tale, the personification of Time allays the potential abruptness of this shift in mood by anchoring rebirth in the changes of the classical festive year. After legislative negotiations met with resistance from Anglican MPs and clergy, supporters of religious comprehension focused on the crown's prerogative powers. Dryden's lines praise Charles's statesmanship in prioritising compromise, but delight in the historical irony of puritans staking everything on the royal ecclesiastical prerogative. He puns on the Act of Indemnity and Oblivion to clarify the kind of forgetting which is intended by the Act, opposing the cognitive 'oblivion' (OED, 1b) that made Charles's subjects seditious, to the conative, strategic forgetting that Charles must perform now to ensure peace. This distinction contributes to the sceptical irony which runs through Dryden's panegyric, subtly undercutting the natural imagery of seasonal change, and distinguishing his poem in tone and implication from those texts which hail Charles as a merciful Christ-figure. Forgetting is a policy, which shows the king's political skill, but it is not the same thing as forgiveness. Dryden's irony manages the contradictory demands of the occasion: on the one hand to represent the Restoration as a natural and inevitable return to the status quo ante, and on the other to celebrate the skill of those -poets as much as politicians -who are making it happen. In his Restoration ode, witnesses to the times become passive in their shock, and perplexedly search scripture for orienting parallels (ll. 101-23). When the poem cites particular injustices such as the Committee of Safety (l. 108) or the trial of Charles I (ll. 52-54), it interrogates history for the supernatural influences that caused injustice, rather than the human culprits. The winding associative form dramatizes an agon of hope and despair felt by royalists in the 1650s. This helps to contextualise the stoic resignation of his earlier Pindaric poems by placing it within a volatile affective context.
