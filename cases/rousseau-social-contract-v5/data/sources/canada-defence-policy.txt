Stacey, Canada is \&quot;an unmilitary community.\&quot; This is no accident. Canadians have paid no price for unpreparedness. &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/fortification\/\&quot;&gt;FORTIFICATIONS&lt;\/a&gt; built at British or French expense serve crowds of tourists as monuments to folly. Capital which might have been lavished on defence was made available for the Intercolonial and the Canadian Pacific railways and to finance the social programs of the 20th century. &lt;p&gt;Before 1870, the defence of Canada was a costly burden for France and then for Great Britain, invariably against enemies to the south, be they Iroquois, English or the American invaders of 1775-76 (see &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/american-revolution\/\&quot;&gt;AMERICAN REVOLUTION&lt;\/a&gt;) or of 1812-14. The &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/american-civil-war\/\&quot;&gt;AMERICAN CIVIL WAR&lt;\/a&gt; persuaded the British that there could be no successful replay of the &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/war-of-1812\/\&quot;&gt;WAR OF 1812&lt;\/a&gt;. Confederation became, in British eyes, a device to help their North American colonists accept the hopeless burden of their own defence. The day the last British garrison left central Canada, November 11, 1871, might be celebrated as Canada&#039;s Independence Day. More accurately, the few Canadians who thought about it realized that they had been left to their own devices.&lt;\/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Canadians faced the paradox of being at once invulnerable and indefensible. Distance and the Royal Navy safeguarded both ocean frontiers from all but occasional raids. The North remained impassable until the advent of long-range aircraft in the 1930s. To the south, whatever George T. &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/george-taylor-denison\/\&quot;&gt;DENISON&lt;\/a&gt; and other militia colonels might assert, defence was impossible without a level of preparedness that would, itself, be provocative. In fact, the British departure was a signal for the United States to close its border forts and move their garrisons west to protect their western settlers. Canada&#039;s policy, as A.A. Dorion had suggested in 1865, was \&quot;to keep quiet and give no cause for war.\&quot; The Treaty of &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/treaty-of-washington\/\&quot;&gt;WASHINGTON&lt;\/a&gt;, 1871, and the &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/alaska-boundary-dispute\/\&quot;&gt;ALASKA BOUNDARY DISPUTE&lt;\/a&gt;, 1903, removed threats to peace. So did the &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/north-west-mounted-police\/\&quot;&gt;NORTH-WEST MOUNTED POLICE&lt;\/a&gt;, created in 1873 to prevent the banditry and border violence that could draw United States troops into the \&quot;Great Lone Land\&quot; as they did in Mexico, Nicaragua, Haiti and other countries to the south. &lt;\/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Canada&#039;s second line of defence was a British guarantee, offered in 1865, to defend every part of the empire \&quot;with all the forces at its command,\&quot; in return for a Canadian pledge to \&quot;devote all her resources, both in men and money, to the maintenance of her connection with the Mother Country.\&quot; The Venezuela Crisis of 1895-96 showed that the British had given no thought to their commitment. Nor had Canada. A militia of 40&amp;nbsp;000 on paper, costing $1 million a year, was described by an American observer as \&quot;a kind of Military Tammany.\&quot; Reformers were not welcome. Both Conservatives and Liberals promoted Canada as a refuge from conscription and a haven from what Sir Wilfrid &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/sir-wilfrid-laurier\/\&quot;&gt;LAURIER&lt;\/a&gt; called \&quot;the vortex of European militarism.\&quot;&lt;\/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;When wars came in the 20th century, Canadians could answer the call of a noble cause or a half-remembered homeland because their own country seemed inviolable. Others, unmoved by crusading fever, would grow resentful that fellow citizens should involve them in what Colonel Armand Lavergne termed \&quot;a somewhat interesting adventure in a foreign country.\&quot; In the First World War, the external threat was sufficiently remote that the few thousand defenders allotted to Halifax or the Welland Canal were denied the status of veterans. &lt;\/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;In the Second World War, a more real threat from German or Japanese submarines hardly justified assigning 3 army divisions or the large Home War Establishment of the RCAF to protecting Canada. Instead, it was initially intended to save them from being conscripted for overseas service. Wartime Canada remained, as Senator Raoul Dandurand had described it 16 years earlier, \&quot;a fireproof house, far from the sources of conflagration.\&quot;&lt;\/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;That status ended abruptly in 1945. Five years earlier, at Ogdensburg, NY, US President Franklin Delano Roosevelt had invited Canada into the American defence system largely to meet Washington&#039;s anxiety about potential hostile use of Canada&#039;s North as a staging area for air attacks. Convinced that he was serving his linchpin role between the US and Britain, PM Mackenzie &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/william-lyon-mackenzie-king\/\&quot;&gt;KING&lt;\/a&gt; enthusiastically agreed. Canadians had largely ignored the military significance of their North; Americans did not. Only belatedly did Ottawa recognize the threat to Canada&#039;s &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/arctic-sovereignty\/\&quot;&gt;ARCTIC SOVEREIGNTY&lt;\/a&gt; as Americans poured north to build the &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/alaska-highway\/\&quot;&gt;ALASKA HIGHWAY&lt;\/a&gt; and northern air bases. &lt;\/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;In the postwar years, as Soviet-American hostility rapidly developed into a &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/cold-war\/\&quot;&gt;COLD WAR&lt;\/a&gt;, Canada found itself between two belligerent superpowers. Canadian defence faced a new paradox: the Soviet Union was the ultimate enemy but the US was the immediate threat. Washington would be sole judge of North America&#039;s security; Canadians could play their assigned role or Americans would do it for them, and possibly send them the bill.&lt;\/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;By the 1950s, the USSR had thermonuclear weapons and a limited capacity to deliver them to US and Canadian cities. Canadians became partners in a continental air defence system with 3 northern radar lines and fighter-interceptor squadrons that flew mainly over Canadian soil. &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/nato-north-atlantic-treaty-organization\/\&quot;&gt;NATO&lt;\/a&gt;, Canada&#039;s \&quot;providential solution\&quot; to the dangers of a lopsided bilateral alliance, was rejected by Washington as an agency for North American defence. &lt;\/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;The North American Air Defence (&lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/norad-north-american-air-defence-agreement\/\&quot;&gt;NORAD&lt;\/a&gt;) agreement, accepted by Ottawa in 1957, made military sense for a continent in danger; politically it gave Washington effective control over Canada&#039;s right to make war or peace. That fact, belatedly absorbed by the &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/john-diefenbaker\/\&quot;&gt;DIEFENBAKER&lt;\/a&gt; government during the 1962 &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/cuban-missile-crisis\/\&quot;&gt;CUBAN MISSILE CRISIS&lt;\/a&gt;, left a gap in NORAD defences and a political crisis. The ensuing 1963 election led to a change of governments - a rare intervention of defence issues in peacetime Canadian politics. &lt;\/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;&lt;a href=\&quot;javascript:DisplayMedia(&#039;787&#039;, &#039;787&#039;, &#039;787&#039;, 460, 334, 0, 0, 0);\&quot; name=\&quot;787\&quot;&gt;&lt;\/a&gt;&amp;nbsp;Technology temporarily rescued Canadians from domestic defence dilemmas. Nuclear warfare was transformed by the stratospheric trajectory of intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs) and the abstractions of MAD (mutually-assured destruction). Nuclear-tipped rockets would pass far over Canada to fall on superpower soil. Defence priorities which, in 1949, 1959 and 1964, had formally listed NATO, NORAD and Canada&#039;s &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/peacekeeping\/\&quot;&gt;PEACEKEEPING&lt;\/a&gt; contribution to the &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/united-nations\/\&quot;&gt;UNITED NATIONS&lt;\/a&gt; at the top, were abruptly reversed by the &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/pierre-elliott-trudeau\/\&quot;&gt;TRUDEAU&lt;\/a&gt; government. Defence \&quot;Priority One\&quot; became &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/sovereignty\/\&quot;&gt;SOVEREIGNTY&lt;\/a&gt;, whether defined as surveillance of Canada&#039;s frontiers or overawing opponents of national unity in the October Crisis of 1970. &lt;\/p&gt; &lt;p&gt;Fishery patrols, arctic overflights and a tiny Northern Command at Yellowknife were combined with drastic reductions in armed forces strength, largely at the expense of Canada&#039;s NATO commitment. Armed Forces strength, which had dropped from 120&amp;nbsp;000 to 100&amp;nbsp;000 in the &lt;a href=\&quot;\/article\/lester-bowles-pearson\/\&quot;&gt;PEARSON&lt;\/a&gt; years, was slashed by the Trudeau government to 78&amp;nbsp;000 men and women in the regular forces and less than 20&amp;nbsp;000 in the reserves.
