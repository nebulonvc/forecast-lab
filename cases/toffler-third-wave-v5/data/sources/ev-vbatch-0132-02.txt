The Senate filibuster has effectively set a 60-vote supermajority threshold for passing many types of legislation through the Senate. Traditionally, the Senate filibuster was reserved for only the most controversial issues, but its use has escalated in recent years, often slowing business in the chamber to a halt. The use of the filibuster, once reserved for only the most controversial issues, has increased dramatically in recent years alongside growing polarization in Washington. There have been more than 2,500 votes to end a filibuster since 1917; more than half have been in just the last 12 years. Today, the 26 least populous states are home to only around 21 percent of the U.S. population. This means that a group of senators representing a small minority of the country can use the filibuster to prevent the passage of bills with broad public support. The relative stagnancy of Congress — which is at least in part due to the filibuster — has pushed presidents to increase their use of executive power, which in turn often goes unchecked because of Congress’s inability to act.
