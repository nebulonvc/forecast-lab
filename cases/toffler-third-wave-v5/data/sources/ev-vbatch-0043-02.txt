What changes has the Act made to the law? 
The new measures aim to support access to flexible working, ensuring that employers accept requests where it is reasonable and feasible to do so. 
Flexible working helps people achieve a better work life balance, which can lead to happier, healthier and more productive employees. This is good for employees as well as for businesses. 
It is also particularly valuable for those with health conditions and caring responsibilities, including parents with young children. 
The Employment Rights Act 2025 makes amendments to legislation to make it more likely that flexible working requests are accepted where it is reasonable to do so. 
If employers are considering rejecting a request, they will follow a new process to first consult the employee. This will prompt employers and employees to think creatively about the types of flexible working that might be feasible and suitable in their circumstances. We ran a public consultation on these reforms earlier this year, and published a government response setting out next steps on 15 September 2026. 
We are also introducing a reasonableness test. This means that an employer may only refuse a request if it is reasonable for the employer to do so, drawing on one or more of the eight business reasons already set out in legislation If, after consulting with their employee, an employer decides to reject a request, they must explain to the employee why their decision is reasonable and state the reason or reasons for their decision. 
We intend that these changes will take effect in autumn 2027. 
Summary 
The Employment Rights Act 2025 amends legislation with the aim of making it more likely flexible working requests are accepted. We are introducing a process that employers must follow to meet the existing requirement to consult an employee if they are considering rejecting their flexible working request. The process will encourage both parties to discuss ways to overcome challenges with proposed arrangements or agree an alternative form of flexible working. If, after this consultation meeting, the employer still chooses to reject the request, they will have to set out why they feel it is reasonable to do so, drawing on the business reasons already set out in legislation. 
How is this different from the previous legislation? 
Employees currently have the right to request a flexible working arrangement from their first day in a job. Flexible working arrangements include, but are not limited to: 
part time 
flexible hours (‘flexitime’) 
staggered shifts 
compressed hours 
term-time hours 
job shares 
remote work 
hybrid work 
Employers can refuse a flexible working request for one (or more) of eight possible business reasons set out in legislation, and they must consult with the employee before rejecting a request. However, the current legislation provides no detail on how this consultation should be conducted. 
There is currently no requirement for employers to explain their decision. This has led to a lack of transparency around decisions on flexible working, with some requests that could be reasonably implemented being turned down. 
According to Timewise’s 2023 Flexible Jobs Index , while nearly 9 in 10 employees want to work flexibly, only 6 in 10 currently do. 
We are retaining the approach of the existing legal framework for flexible working, where an employee makes a request which an employer can agree or reject. But we are making some important changes to make it more likely that flexible working requests are accepted. 
The Employment Rights Act 2025 amended existing legislation so that: 
employers can only reject a flexible working request where it is reasonable to do so on the basis of one (or more) of the eight business reasons already set out in primary legislation 
if an employer wants to reject a request, they will first need to consult with the employee (as they currently do), but in doing so follow a specified process, to be set out in secondary legislation 
if an employer rejects a request, they must explain to the employee why their decision is reasonable 
Key statistics 
According to a 2023 Chartered Institute for Professional Development ( CIPD ) survey , almost two-fifths (38%) of organisations say that more home or hybrid working has increased their organisation’s productivity and efficiency. Just 13% say it has decreased their organisation’s productivity or efficiency. 
Research by the Equal Parenting Project found that over 75% of UK managers believe flexible working increases productivity, and 62.8% believe it boosts motivation.
