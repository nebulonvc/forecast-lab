This paper documents the evolution of contemporary industrial policy across China, the European Union, and the United States between 2009 and 2024 using an extended panel from the New Industrial Policy Observatory. Focusing on selective industrial actions that plausibly alter relative competitive conditions, the analysis characterises industrial policy as a persistent, market-shaping phenomenon rather than a temporary response to macroeconomic shocks. Ten stylised facts document a sharp and sustained expansion in industrial policy activity after 2019, a reorientation of stated policy motives toward security and resilience and increasing concentration of interventions in strategic and dual-use sectors. Industrial actions increasingly propagate across jurisdictions through rapid cross-country sequencing. Subsidies diffuse quickly among major economies and are frequently followed by import restrictions in large domestic markets, while defensive trade measures become more rival-directed. At the same time, policy instruments diverge in persistence: export restrictions have become structurally durable, whereas import barriers remain more contingent. Taken together, the evidence points to a shift from episodic intervention toward a competitive and sequential industrial policy regime organised around strategic value chains and chokepoints, with important implications for trade governance and the multilateral trading system. Authors Fernando Martín Espejo Date Published 28 Jan 2026 Related Topics: Industrial Policy ZG Series Report file Introduction Industrial policy has returned, but in a form that differs markedly from earlier episodes of state intervention. What began in the aftermath of the global financial crisis as selective support for economic recovery has, since 2020, become increasingly intertwined with concerns over resilience, security, and geopolitical rivalry. Governments are no longer primarily focused on correcting isolated market failures or smoothing cyclical adjustment. Instead, they are actively reshaping market structure—altering relative competitive conditions across firms and borders through subsidies, localisation requirements, procurement preferences, and restrictions on imports, exports, and investment.
