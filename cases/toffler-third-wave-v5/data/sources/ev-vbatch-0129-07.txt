Critics say the Security Council’s outdated composition undermines its legitimacy and the increasing use of the veto inhibits its functionality—as seen in its paralysis over the wars in Gaza, Iran, and Ukraine.
Structurally, the Security Council has remained largely unchanged since its founding in 1945, aside from an expansion from eleven to fifteen members in 1965 that fueled debate over the need to reform it to improve regional representation and reflect modern geopolitical realities. In recent years, members’ competing interests have often stymied the Security Council’s ability to respond to major conflicts and crises , including the COVID-19 pandemic, Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine, the Israel-Hamas war, and the Iran war. The paralysis has renewed long-standing concerns about the Security Council’s effectiveness and ability to address twenty-first-century challenges.
Outdated structure. Many critics, including member states from the developing world, contend that the Security Council’s structure does not reflect current geopolitical realities. Its membership was expanded from six elected members to ten in 1965, and in 1971, the People’s Republic of China took the permanent seat previously occupied by the Republic of China (Taiwan). Since then, the body’s composition has remained unchanged. Regional powers such as Brazil, Germany, India, Japan, Nigeria, and South Africa have sought to enlarge the Security Council or secure permanent seats of their own. In 2021, Britain announced its support for Germany receiving a permanent seat. In 2023, China, France, and Germany called for two permanent seats for African countries on the Security Council.
Despite broad agreement among UN member states that the Security Council’s makeup is outdated, the prospects for substantial reform remain slim. Amending the UN Charter to change the Security Council’s composition or voting rules requires a two-thirds majority in the General Assembly and domestic ratification by two-thirds of member states—including all five permanent members, which are unlikely to support measures that would curb their own influence.
UN Secretary-General António Guterres (2017–2026) has long acknowledged the need for reforming the Security Council, describing it as “a serious problem of legitimacy and of ineffectiveness,” and calling for expanded membership to better reflect global demographics.
