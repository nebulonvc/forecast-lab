Productivity Puzzles: Then and Now Nobel Prize-winning economist Robert Solow famously wrote in 1987 that "you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics." 3 He may have been expecting too much from the computers he saw. While 32 percent of workers used computers at work in 1989, the computers were not very powerful. To illustrate, the processor in your phone today can do about as many calculations per second as 10 million 1987 vintage personal computers (PCs). 4 In the aggregate, national spending on computers—shown in figure 1 —was barely detectable at the time, despite PCs being an important part of the zeitgeist. 5 Ten years later, computer investment had taken off and some observers, notably Chairman Greenspan, believed they could see it in the productivity statistics, though the link between the two was only established later. 6 Investment in information technology (IT) capital—computers, software, and communications equipment—increased the annual growth rate of labor productivity about 3/4 percentage point in the late 1990s. 7 While that expansion of the IT capital stock directly boosted worker productivity, there was more than that to the IT boom. Businesses also reorganized their operations around computers, which further supported productivity growth. Advancements in technology changed the cost of creating, storing, and communicating information and provided an opportunity for firms to rethink their scope, scale, and production functions. 8 E-commerce, a wave of globalization, and software in the cloud eventually emerged from that process. One sign that reorganization was beneficial and significant was the rapid rise in the amount of output per unit of input outside the IT sector in the early 2000s. That productivity growth was not purely due to investing in more capital; instead, it also reflected increases in what economists call total factor productivity, or TFP. 9 The seeds of the 1990s productivity boom were planted long before Solow's observations in 1987. Basic research that laid the foundations for modern computing included the invention of the transistor in 1947 and the Fortran compiler in 1954.
