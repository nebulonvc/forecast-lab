Two centuries later, thanks to a confluence of business and consumer interests, the popularity of personals in America boomed. For publications, personals were a source of revenue and readership because people both paid for their own ads and bought publications to read others’ ads.
For singles, they were a useful way to find a companion, and in the late 20th century, there were a lot of singles looking for partners. From 1960 to 1980, the percent of women in their early 20s who had never married went from 30% to 50%, the divorce rate more than doubled, and the median age of first marriage for women increased from 20.3 to 24.4 (Stein 1981, 13). At the same time, women’s liberation and the sexual revolution empowered singles to take charge of dating. Personals were a perfect tool for singles seeking control over their search for love.
Thus, in the 1980s, personals spread across the country. They were in Miami magazine, the Washingtonian, The New York Review of Books, The Los Angeles Times, and more. TV Guide even introduced its own “Classified Mart,” and law journals started running personals, too (Span 1989; Hoffmann et al. 2023). There was also a proliferation of personals-only newsletters. For instance, Edward Donath charged $3 a year for singles to advertise in and receive his newsletter. For the same price, the California-based Golden State Club promised “large generous lists” of the opposite sex in your state. (Interested in joining either newsletter? Their application forms are in the National Museum of American History’s archives.) Personals were everywhere, and by 1993, seventy million American adults read at least one classified ad per week (Merskin 1993, 102).
The personals developed a particular style in response to the tensions between the revenue-driving interests of the publications and the love-finding interests of the advertisers.
For instance, most publications charged a couple dollars per word to run a personal, and the average ad could set someone back $50. This incentivized singles to keep their ads as brief as possible, and to do so, they turned to abbreviations like SWF (single white female) or GBM (gay Black male). This abbreviation culture took on a life of its own. One 1980s Village Voice ad opened with: “GWM-BiM, 44 rara avis! Attr, strt lkg/actg WASP wd lk exceptal G or BiM for frndshp, prhps more.” Dating advice author Sharyn Wolf called this phenomenon “initialitis,” saying it served “only to confuse and camouflage” readers (Wolf 1993, 111). In turn, some publications like the Seattle Weekly even outlawed abbreviations altogether (Block 1984, 116).
Personals gave advertisers the freedom to express themselves. Because publications rarely verified the authenticity of an advertiser’s claims, many singles turned to euphemisms. For instance, advertisers might say “not materialistic” for “flat broke” or “Rubenesque” for “fat” (Lavin 1998). In 1983, one study compared personal ads with answers on a questionnaire from those advertisers and found that, while ads rarely contained outright lies, they frequently exaggerated the truth (Austrom and Hanel 1983).
Even though publications didn’t verify the authenticity of what people said, they often had rules around what they deemed “acceptable”. For example, some publications prohibited homosexual ads. In the 1990s, the San Diego Union-Tribune banned words like “gay” and “lesbian.” Ted Seastrom, who was gay, explained: “You’d put in words like ‘seeking loving, committed relationship,’ and they’d change it to ‘seeking a friend.’” Readers would have to infer that, for instance, “male seeks male friend” was likely a gay advertisement (Anonymous 1998).
Therefore, a cat-and-mouse game arose between the strictures of a publication and the desires of an advertiser. When Charles wanted to place an ad in Baltimore Magazine stating he was “married,” it was rejected. But he noticed ads in the publication that featured “MWM,” generally understood as “married white male.” He asked the classifieds manager about this, and she explained the “M” was “mature,” so Charles revised his ad to read “MWM.” The responses he received implied people understood exactly what “M” actually meant (Block 1984, 143).
The personal ad hasn’t gone away. It’s just moved. With the rise of Internet dating in the 1990s, the personal went online, which led to a drop in demand for the print counterpart. Then, in the 2010s, the personal ad went mobile with dating apps.
