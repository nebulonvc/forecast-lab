On August 18, gross national debt crossed $40 trillion . That is $2 trillion more than at the end of 2025 and twice as high as in 2017, just under a decade ago. In fact, we are adding debt at a faster rate than ever . Through the 2020s, the government has been adding $1 trillion in debt every 5 months, on average. That is twice as fast as during the 2010s, at 11 months, and nearly five times faster than in the 2000s, at 24 months. At this rapid rate of borrowing, the federal government is on track to hit the current debt ceiling of $41.1 trillion early in 2027 . 2. Our debt is now as large as the entire U.S. economy. Debt held by the public (DHBP) , which includes the amount the U.S. Treasury borrowed from outside lenders, but not intragovernmental debt, eclipsed $32 trillion in 2026. That represents approximately 100 percent of gross domestic product (GDP), the highest debt-to-GDP ratio since World War II. 3. Welcome to the era of $2 trillion deficits. In fiscal year (FY) 2026, the federal government ran a budget deficit of $2 trillion, the third-largest deficit in U.S. history and the largest outside of the COVID-19 pandemic years. This deficit represents a long-term, structural imbalance between spending and revenue . While there were gains in individual income and payroll taxes ($253 billion more than in FY25), they were partially offset by decreases in corporate income taxes ($71 billion less, due to changes from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act ) and customs duties ($22 billion less, due to refunds of tariffs after a Supreme Court ruling). Revenues totaled $5.4 trillion, $167 billion more than in FY25, while spending totaled $7.4 trillion, $386 billion more than in FY25. 4. Interest costs reached $1 trillion for the first time. Interest payments on the national debt have grown rapidly in recent years, driven by higher interest rates and increased borrowing. In FY26, net interest costs surpassed $1 trillion for the first time, an increase of 8 percent from FY25.
