The NFRA oversees China’s 4,070 banking and lending institutions with RMB 480 trillion (about $67.2 trillion) in total assets at end-2025. China’s “Big Six” state-owned commercial banks dominate the sector and are largely stable:
Agricultural Bank of China
Bank of China
Bank of Communications
China Construction Bank
Industrial and Commercial Bank of China and
Postal Savings Bank of China
Smaller regional banks, however, have higher non-performing loan rates and less-stable liability structures. In its 2025 China Financial Stability Report, PBOC reported that 312 banking institutions, or 8.8 percent, received a “fail” rating in a first-half 2025 review. They held RMB 9.4 trillion (about $1.3 trillion), or 2.1 percent, of total banking assets. The official non-performing loan rate among PRC banks is low at 1.5 percent at end-2025, though analysts believe the actual figure may be significantly higher. Bank lending is Chinese companies’ primary source of capital (reportedly 60.7 percent in 2025), though corporate bonds, equity financing, and private equity are quickly expanding in scope, reach, and sophistication in China.
Chinese regulators have implemented measures to rein in China’s “shadow banking” sector, including wealth management and trust products, as part of a campaign to reduce debt and financial risk. China’s $4.5 trillion trust industry, as of June 2025, came under renewed scrutiny in 2023 when Zhongzhi Enterprise Group missed payments on several high-yield investment products, highlighting residual vulnerabilities amid weak property-market conditions. Regulators say the “barbaric” growth of shadow banking has been fundamentally curbed since tightening began in 2017, with high-risk “quasi-credit” shadow banking dropping by RMB 30 trillion (about $4.4 trillion), from its historical peak. Beijing has not released official figures on the sector’s overall scale, but Chinese private research institutions estimated it at around RMB 85 trillion (about $11.9 trillion), in 2025, while the IMF’s 2025 Article IV Consultation put the shadow-banking-to-GDP ratio at 13 percent. More broadly, China’s financial system remains tightly controlled and dominated by domestic institutions. Although foreign-owned banks may establish wholly owned banks and branches, onerous licensing requirements and the dominance of domestic banks have limited foreign banks’ market penetration, with foreign banks accounting for just 1.3 percent of system assets as of 2025.
Foreigners may open a bank account in China but must present a passport or Chinese government-issued identification and are often asked for their social security numbers.
