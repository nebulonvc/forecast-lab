This Pew Research Center study tries to answer two broad questions:
First, how do the world’s major religious groups differ in their age profiles, fertility, migration patterns, and rates of attracting and losing followers?
Second, how will these differences shape the future of religion around the world?
We project future changes in the size and geographic distribution of seven religious groups – Christians, Muslims, Hindus, Buddhists, Jews, followers of all other religions and religiously unaffiliated people – between 2020 and 2070. The projections cover 201 countries and territories, which collectively account for 99.98% of the world’s population.
The results are not intended to predict all the ways the world could change. There are many events that could affect religious group populations (including wars, natural disasters, epidemics, and changes in economic or social circumstances) that are not analyzed here.
This research is part of the Pew-Templeton Global Religious Futures project , which analyzes religious change and its impact on societies around the world. It builds on our recently published work measuring the world’s recent religious makeup .
Many countries make demographic projections to try to forecast how their populations may change. The most common projection method – used by researchers for more than a century – starts by estimating the size of a country’s population in groups (“cohorts”) divided by age and sex, such as the number of girls ages 0 to 4, 5 to 9, 10 to 14, etc. Each cohort is then projected into the future, modeling how its size will change every five years with the addition of expected births (based on fertility rates) and the subtraction of expected deaths (based on mortality rates). Demographers also often attempt to forecast future levels of immigration and emigration.
Our projections use an advanced version of this method. We pulled data from national censuses and surveys around the world to estimate the size and age distribution of each religious group in the baseline year, 2020. We incorporated data on differences in fertility rates between religious groups and on past patterns of movement into and out of each group, known as “religious switching.” Our projections also drew on data from the United Nations’ 2024 revision of its World Population Prospects, global migration flow estimates from University of Hong Kong professor Guy Abel, and the Center’s previous research on the religious composition of the world’s migrants . For centuries, Christianity has been the world’s largest religion. That may soon change.
Muslims are expected to outnumber Christians by 2035, according to new projections from Pew Research Center. Both religious groups are growing in size, but Muslims are growing faster. By 2070, if recent trends continue, there will be more than 3.6 billion Muslims and just under 3 billion Christians worldwide.
The world’s total population is projected to increase by 29% between 2020 and 2070. Muslims and Christians are the only religious groups analyzed here that are expected to keep pace, with Christians growing by 30% and Muslims by 80%.
As a result, the size of many religious groups will change relative to the world’s overall population. Muslims will make up a larger percentage of the global population in 2070 (36%) than they did in 2020 (26%). Christians, meanwhile, will hold fairly steady at 29%.
Although Hindus are growing in absolute number, they are projected to decline as a percentage of the global population. Jews are expected to follow a similar pattern. 1
Meanwhile, Buddhists are expected to shrink both in number and as a portion of the world’s population in the coming decades.
All other religions combined (including Baha’is, Daoists, Jains, Sikhs, adherents of folk religions and many other groups) are expected to experience a modest increase in number. However, they will slip below 2% of the global population by 2070. 2
And people who have no religious affiliation – sometimes called religious “nones” – are projected to drop substantially as a share of the world population, from 24% in 2020 to 17% in 2070. 3

The future size of religious groups is heavily influenced by demographic trends in the countries and regions in which each group is concentrated .
In the 21st century, most of the world’s population growth is expected to occur in Africa, particularly in countries south of the Sahara Desert. By 2070, more than a quarter of all people worldwide will live in sub-Saharan Africa . Since the vast majority of people who currently live there are either Christians or Muslims, the region’s high fertility and rising life expectancy will boost the global numbers of both faiths.
By comparison, the populations of other major geographic regions are expected to grow more modestly, or even to shrink. For example, between 2020 and 2070, the number of people living in Europe will decline, and their share of the world’s population is projected to drop from 10% to 6%.
Fertility differences have big effects on populations. Globally, Muslims tend to have more children than other major religious groups. During the 2020-2025 period, the total fertility rate for Muslims was 3.1 children per woman. 4 By comparison, the rate for non-Muslims was 2.0 children per woman.
The current age profile of the people in each religious group also plays a role in the projections. In younger populations, a larger share of people have yet to complete their childbearing years. On average, Muslims around the world are younger than any other religious group in this study. As of 2020, the median age of Muslims (24) was nearly a decade lower than the median age of non-Muslims (33).
If recent trends continue, by 2070:
More than half of the world’s Christians will live in sub-Saharan Africa.
An African country, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, will surpass the United States as the country with the greatest number of Christians.
The United States will no longer have a Christian majority, though Christians will remain the biggest religious group in the country.
Pakistan will have the largest Muslim population, followed by India, Nigeria and Indonesia (which currently has the most Muslims).
