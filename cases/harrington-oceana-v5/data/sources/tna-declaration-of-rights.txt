# James II and the Declaration of Rights

In 1689, the role of the British Monarchy and Parliament would change forever. Legally, this began with a single document: the Declaration of Rights.

Date
: 1689

In November 1688, William of Orange landed on the South Devon coast with a large army. The Dutch stadtholder (head of state) had invaded England by invitation of Parliament. England’s Protestant elite wanted to replace the Catholic King James II with a Protestant monarchy. Mary, the King’s Protestant daughter, and her husband, William of Orange, were seen as suitable successors. In April 1689, the pair were crowned William III and Mary II. These events have sometimes been referred to as the ‘Glorious Revolution’.

To justify the overthrow of James II and explain their reasons for replacing him with his daughter Mary and her husband William, Parliament issued the ‘Declaration of Rights’ in February 1689.

The Declaration listed 13 complaints against James II, describing how he ‘did endeavour to Subvert and Extirpate the Protestant Religion, and the Laws and Liberties of this Kingdom’. The grievances included James’ unilateral suspension of laws (without Parliamentary consent), his maintenance of a standing army, and his misuse of the British legal system to pursue his enemies. Essentially, Parliament felt that James II had taken too much power for himself, putting his desires before his people’s wishes.

Finally, the Declaration stated Parliament’s formal intent to recognise William and Mary as King and Queen. This included an agreement that they would rule only according to these new limitations laid down by Parliament.

Later in 1689, the Declaration of Rights was passed into law as the ‘Bill of Rights’. This confirmed the limits of British royal power, and the growing importance of Parliament, within Britain’s system of constitutional monarchy. Parliament would keep its powers and freedoms as the state’s lawmaking body, and act as a safeguard against any monarch’s absolute power.
