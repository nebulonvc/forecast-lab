International Literacy Day 2025
Promoting literacy in the digital era
FACTSHEET
In 2025, International Literacy Day (ILD) will explore the theme “Promoting literacy in the digital
1
era.” In today’s rapidly evolving digital landscape, the importance of literacy is more critical than
ever. Beyond traditional literacy associated with paper-based reading and writing, it now serves as a
foundation for digital skills, safe and critical engagement with digital texts and tools, and an inclusive
digital transition. Digitalization is also changing the ways in which literacy policies, programmes and
practices are designed, implemented and monitored.
ILD 2025 will explore both the opportunities and challenges for ensuring an inclusive digital transition
through and for literacy. It will showcase effective solutions that promote literacy as a human right
and a cornerstone for sustainable, just and peaceful societies.

YOUTH AND ADULT LITERACY
Globally, steady progress has been made since world leaders adopted the Sustainable Development Goal 4
(SDG 4) on education and lifelong learning.
2

Between 2015 and 2024, literacy rates among adults (population aged 15+) rose slightly from 86% to 88%. The top two regions
with the fastest progress are Central and Southern Asia, where the adult literacy rate increased from 72% to 77%, and subSaharan Africa, with an increase from 65% to 69%.
The global youth (population aged 15 to 24) literacy rate reached 93% in 2024, higher than for adults, reflecting the expansion of
basic education from which the younger generations are benefiting.

But progress remains inadequate and uneven.
In 2024, 739 million adults still lacked basic literacy skills.
Nearly 77% lived in sub-Saharan Africa (225 million) and Central and Southern Asia (347 million)
– a share that has grown by 2 percentage points since 2015.

The adult literacy rates of the 15 countries with the lowest rates among those with the latest data available for the 2015-2024 census
3
decade were 29% to 60% .
According to the latest data from the 2015–2024 census decade, more than half of the world’s illiterate adults — 441 million people —
are concentrated in just 10 countries, many of which have large populations4.

Persistent gaps continue to limit opportunities and reinforce social and economic inequalities, especially for women,
older adults and marginalized people.
In 2024, women made up to nearly two-thirds of the 739 million illiterate adults (466 million).
Among 108 million forcibly displaced people, literacy and digital divides are severe - only 30% of youth in crisis-affected countries
5
are literate .
In some sub-Saharan African countries, only 31% of elderly women are literate, compared to 55% for elderly men, and near-universal
(99%) male literacy rate in parts of Europe and North American countries.

The state of literacy is more complex when it considers other factors, such as evolving understanding of literacy, demographic
trends, language and data collection. More efforts are needed to improve literacy monitoring.

How we define literacy matters.

6

The conventional statistics, such as youth and adult literacy rate (SDG indicator 4.6.1) and number of illiterate youth and adults
provide a relatively comprehensive picture but have their limitations. Focusing on basic literacy - reading and writing - they are
calculated based on self- or household-declaration of whether persons are ‘literate’ or ‘illiterate’. These statistics do not include
the numeracy and specific literacy skills required in digital societies.
7

Monitoring skills proficiency is still challenging and costly. But when skills are directly measured, even in 31 developed countries
participated in the Programme for International Assessment of Adult Skills (PIAAC), 18% of adults are reported to lack the most
8
basic levels of proficiency in literacy, numeracy and adaptive problem solving . In these countries, adult literacy has actually
stagnated or declined since 2012 with some exceptions.

Literacy and numeracy are basic cognitive skills and essential components of broader knowledge, skills, and competencies such
as digital and financial literacy. In the SDG monitoring framework, however, SDG target 4.3 on ‘equal access to technical,
vocational, tertiary and adult education’ and SDG target 4.4 on ‘increase the number of youth and adults who have relevant skills,
including technical and vocational skills, for employment, decent work and entrepreneurship skills for work’ are artificially
separated. More coordination between monitoring of these targets is needed in light of the evolving concept of literacy, which
embraces several skills domains, including numeracy and some digital skills.

When demographic trends are counted, in two regions, in which youth and adult literacy rates increased between
2015 and 2024, illiterate youth and adults increased.
In sub-Saharan Africa, in which the literacy rate increased from 65% to 69%, illiterate adults increased from 196 million to
225 million. In Western Asia and Northern Africa, in which the literacy rate increased 82% to 84%, illiterate adults increased
from 59 million to 65 million.
The global number of illiterate adults declined from 754 million in 2023 to 739 million in 2024. This decrease reflects both revised
population estimates from the UN Population Division’s 2024 World Population Prospects 9 (WPP) and updated national literacy
statistics. Notably, India’s literacy rate, given its large population, was updated upward from 77% in 2023 to 81% in 2024,
contributing significantly to the global reduction. The 2024 UN Population Division revision of WPP also affected a number of outof-school children, adolescents and youth.

Often, languages are not counted in literacy provision and statistics.
While multilingualism is a norm in many countries, 40% of the world population does not access
10
education in a language they understand. In literacy monitoring, many countries use a single
language, while a handful of countries allow respondents to read or write in any language.

CHILDREN’S LITERACY
More children are going to school and completing education than ever before.
Since 2015, 110 million more children, adolescents and youth have enrolled in school – the
highest number in history.11
40 million more young people are completing secondary school today than 2015. Global
completion rates increased from 85% to 88% in primary, from 74% to 79% in lower secondary
and from 53% to 59% in upper secondary education. Girls now outperform boys by 2–312
percentage points at all levels.

LITERACY IN THE DIGITAL ERA

In a digitalized world, literacy is more essential than ever. It is at the heart of digital learning, enabling everyone to
participate fully in today’s connected world.
Beyond access to the internet and digital devices, the ability to read, write and use numbers on paper and digital materials is
crucial for thinking critically and navigating information-rich society and economy in a safe, effective and responsible manner.

But not everyone is equipped with skills to navigate a digital world
18

In OECD countries around one-third of adults aged 55 to 65 do not possess computer skills, and only 1 in 10 is assessed as having
medium-to-good problem-solving skills in a technology-rich environment.
Many teachers feel unprepared for teaching with technologies. Only half of countries have standards for developing teacher ICT
19
skills.

Many people are not literacy skills are not solid enough in the face of disinformation and misinformation
20

In OECD countries, only 9% of 15-year-olds could distinguish ‘fact’ from ’opinion‘ in digital texts.

provision and access well-trained educators.
Digital technologies can help reach disadvantaged learners,
reduce costs and increase engagement when they are used

FINANCING FOR LITERACY

Funding for adult literacy and basic skills remains limited
worldwide. Adequate and sustainable funding is a key to
building more literate, inclusive, sustainable and peaceful
societies.
Among 102 countries surveyed by UNESCO, 57% allocated less than 4%
of their national education budgets to adult literacy and education—
highlighting persistent underinvestment in this critical area.26
Of the 146 countries that responded to UNESCO’s survey, only 39% of
countries reported an increase in funding for adult literacy and basic
skills since 2018, while 53% saw no change and 8% reported a decrease.
A basic digital transformation of education in the lower-middle income
countries would add 50% to the total financing gap for achieving
27
national SDG 4 targets.
