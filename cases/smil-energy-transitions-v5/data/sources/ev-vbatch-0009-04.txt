Geothermal technology extracts the heat found within the subsurface of the earth, which can be used directly for heating and cooling, or converted into electricity. However, to generate electricity, medium- or high-temperature resources are needed. These are usually located close to tectonically active regions where hot water and/or steam is carried to the Earth’s surface or can be accessed at shallow depths. The total installed capacity of geothermal energy reached 15.4 GW globally by the end of 2024, representing a modest increase from around 13.0 GW at the end of 2020. Only 0.4 GW of new geothermal capacity was added in 2024. According to IRENA's latest cost analysis, the levelised cost of electricity (LCOE) from geothermal power projects has shown limited cost reduction compared to other renewable technologies. Between 2010 and 2024, the global weighted-average LCOE increased, from USD 0.055/kWh to USD 0.060/kWh . Many of the power plants in operation today are dry steam plants or flash plants, harnessing temperatures higher than 180°C. However, medium-temperature fields are increasingly used for electricity generation or for combined heat and power thanks to the development of binary cycle technology, in which geothermal fluid is used via heat exchangers to heat a process fluid in a closed loop. Additionally, new technologies are being developed such as enhanced geothermal systems, which are at the demonstration stage.
