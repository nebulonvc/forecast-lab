### Catharine Macaulay’s Republican Enlightenment

The ‘celebrated’ Catharine Macaulay was both lauded and execrated during the eighteenth century for her republican politics and her unconventional, second marriage. This comprehensive biography in the ‘life and letters’ tradition situates her works in their political and social contexts and offers an unprecedented, detailed account of the content and influence of her writing, the arguments she developed in her eight-volume history of England and her other political, eth ical, and educational works. Her disagreements with conservative opponents, David Hume, Edmund Burke, and Samuel Johnson are developed in detail, as is her influence on more progressive admirers such as Thomas Jefferson, Jacques Pierre Brissot, Mercy Otis Warren, and Mary Wollstonecraft. Macaulay emerges as a coherent and influential political voice, whose attitudes and aspirations were characteristic of those enlightenment republicans who grounded their progressive politics in rational religion. She looked back to the seventeenth-century levellers and parliamentarians as important precursors who had advocated the liberty and political rights she aspired to see implemented in Great Britain, America, and France. Her defence of republican liberty and the equal rights of men offers an important corrective to some contemporary accounts of the character and origins of democratic republicanism during this crucial period.

Karen Green is Associate Professor and Principal Research Fellow at the University of Melbourne. In 1995 she published The Woman of Reason: Feminism, Humanism and Political Thought; in 2009, with Jacqueline Broad, A History of Women’s Political Thought in Europe, 1400–1700; and in 2014, A History of Women’s Political Thought in Europe, 1700–1800. She is the editor of The Correspondence of Catharine Macaulay (2019) and author of many art icles in philosophy, feminism, and political theory.

### Introduction

In December 1790, the thirty-one-year-old Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–97) sent a copy of A Vindication of the Rights of Men (1790)—her response to the Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), by Edmund Burke (1729–97)—to the fifty-nine-year-old Catharine Macaulay Graham (née Sawbridge, 1731–91). She apologized for writing without having been introduced, explaining that she wanted to express her great respect for the older woman, who contended for laurels, unlike so many other female writers, who were content with flowers. 1 Immediately prior to commencing her critique of Burke, Wollstonecraft had written a very favourable review of Macaulay’s Letters on Education (1790) and the influence of that work on Wollstonecraft’s subsequent productions is palpable. 2 Later, in her Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792), she would regret that Macaulay had died too early to be able to appreciate this elabor ation of themes already touched on in the Letters. 3 At this point in her career, Wollstonecraft was almost unknown. Her correspondent was already famous, even to the extent of being notorious, for the ‘celebrated Mrs. Macaulay’ had been a public figure since 1763, when she published the first volume of The History of England from the Accession of James I. to that of the Brunswick Line, which ultimately extended to eight volumes, the last appearing in 1783. 4 In the early twenty-first century, the situation is completely reversed. Wollstonecraft is now well known for her two Vindications and is often assumed to be the origin ator of contemporary feminism. Macaulay, despite a handful of book-length studies, and the appearance of a growing number of articles, is still a relatively marginal figure. 5 In this study I join a resurgence of interest in restoring Macaulay’s reputation as an important contributor to the development of republicanism, democratic thought, and feminism. 6 She was an influential popularizer of democratic pol itical theory, and a well-connected hostess, whose social gatherings made her a central actor in radical political circles in London, during the second half of the eighteenth century. Prior to the American and French Revolutions she was recognized as an authoritative, republican theorist on both sides of the Atlantic. She played a significant role in the evolution of democratic and republican theory, developing central strands of progressive thought that illuminate how the concept of enlightenment was connected with the democratic revolutions

of America and France. In the years leading up to the American Declaration 
of Independence, Macaulay defended the people’s right to change its govern 
ment when it failed in its duty to protect them and secure their best interests. 
Like other ‘patriots’, in whose circles she moved, she believed that knowledge 
of such rights followed from the exercise of ‘enlightened reason’. 7 The vigour 
and influence of her works has remained largely unrecognized by contemporary 
historians and political theorists, perhaps because the utopian elements in her 
thought were belied by the outcome of the French Revolution and arguably, 
because they were penned by a woman. During her lifetime Macaulay’s sex left 
her vulnerable to innuendo and scandal mongering. After her death, her works 
were not deemed worthy of sustained study or re-edition, resulting in the sig 
nificance of her histories and pamphlets in the development of republican ideas 
being almost completely overlooked. 
The first five volumes of Macaulay’s eight volumes of history played a cen 
tral role in disseminating a broadly republican political agenda and a positive 
assessment of the English Commonwealth tradition to readers on both sides 
of the Atlantic, while the coteries she hosted were attended by radical Whigs 
and foreigners, so that her circle of acquaintance spanned a broad spectrum of 
British, American, and French societies. 8 Thus, a study of her life, her works, 
and their relationship to the ideas and activities of the people with whom she 
was acquainted provides a distinctive snapshot of radical London during the 
second half of the eighteenth century. In fact, Macaulay’s acquaintance ranged 
from the conservatives, Samuel Johnson (1709–84), James Boswell (1740–95), 
David Hume (1711–76), and Tobias Smollett (1721–71), who opposed her ideas, 
to the notorious John Wilkes (1725–97), whom she and her brother supported 
for a time. She knew and entertained Benjamin Franklin (1706–90), Horace 
Walpole (1717–97), James Burgh (1714–75), Richard Price (1723–91), Joseph 
Priestley (1733–1804), Benjamin Rush (1747–1814), Jacques-Pierre Brissot 
de Warville (1754–93), and many other less well-known writers and polit 
ical activists of the period. She knew many of the famous literary women of 
her time; the two Robinson sisters, Sarah Scott (née Robinson, 1720–95) and 
Elizabeth Montagu (née Robinson, 1718–1800), as well as other members of the 
so-called Bluestocking circle, including Elizabeth Carter (1717–1806), though 
her relationship with these female contemporaries was not always entirely posi 
tive. Carter, for instance, initially thought her sensible and well read, but by the 
time of Macaulay’s second marriage, the older woman was of the opinion that, 
‘Mrs Macaulay has shown herself a genius of so very eccentric a kind, that, 
except poor Dr Wilson, who has aided and abetted her eccentricities, nobody has 
any great reason to wonder at any thing she does’. 9 The triumphs and disasters 
of her celebrated career highlight both the opportunities that were emerging for 
intellectual women during the eighteenth century and the pitfalls that still faced 
those among them who aspired to laurels. 
While widely read and often celebrated while alive, Macaulay was unlucky 
that she left no descendants who strove to keep her memory alive. Her second 
husband, William Graham (1757–1845), occasionally defended her reputation in 

print, but unlike William Godwin (1756–1836), who published a controversial, laudatory memoir of Wollstonecraft, Graham did nothing to keep his deceased wife in the public eye. 10 Mary Hays (1759–1843), who honoured her with a bio graphical essay that remains a major source for her life, herself descended into obscurity. 11 Her only daughter failed to share her mother’s radical ideas, and while a substantial portion of her correspondence was preserved, it has only recently been made available to scholars. 12 At some time during the early twen tieth century an attempt was made to produce copies of the surviving letters, with a view to publication, probably by one of her descendants. Remains of this attempt are evident from typescripts found among the manuscript letters that emerged from the shadows when auctioned in 1992, but clearly this attempt did not come to fruition. 13 No nineteenth-century scholar chose to delve deeply into her works, and most have not been reissued in modern editions. It was not until after the entrée of the first generation of female historians into the academy that Macaulay began to attract some notice and to be slowly recognized as having played a significant part in eighteenth-century political dramas. Lucy Martin Donnelly’s important 1949 article provided the first twentieth-century overview of her life and works and rescued her from the oblivion into which she had fallen. In 1992, it was superseded by Bridget Hill’s monograph, The Republican Virago, and more recently Kate Davies has thrown light on her connections with Mercy Otis Warren. Concluding her well-researched, yet rather embarrassed and belittling article, ‘The Celebrated Mrs Macaulay’, Donnelly urged that ‘ “a pedestal in the Pantheon of British Liberty”—and of French and American liberty too’ should be set up to Kate Macaulay. 14 Slowly, the desired pedestal is being erected. Yet including her within the Pantheon is not a simple matter. For, a careful assessment of her works and place in the evolution of democratic thought involves looking back at the influences that shaped her and the contem poraries with whom she engaged, as well as assessing the extent of her impact. Ultimately, also, it raises questions about the integrity of the political history that is generally taught, our concept of political liberty, and the accepted account of the historical foundations of many political doctrines that we take for granted. Anyone with the slightest knowledge of the history of the rise of modern democratic institutions in North America and Europe knows that the conflicts out of which these representative forms emerged took place during the second half of the eighteenth century. Yet much contemporary discussion of the pol itical philosophy that fuelled these conflicts focuses on texts written during the seventeenth century, or even earlier. Macaulay’s histories and political pamphlets were published in the years between 1763 and 1790, so that they, along with those of her friends and acquaintances, Price, Burgh, and Priestley, allow us to understand how seventeenth-century political philosophies were popularly interpreted and applied, during the period in which they had the most dramatic impact. Macaulay’s histories, which recounted the struggles of the Commons against the tyrannical incursions of the Stuarts into their established liberties, represented the Parliamentary period as a lost opportunity for the foundation of a just political regime and played an important role in disseminating republican

ideology during this crucial period. She was called a ‘republican virago’ at a time when little distinction was made between those who advocated a strictly limited constitutional monarchy and advocates of a purely republican government. She tirelessly argued for a broader franchise and more frequent parliaments in Great Britain and was opposed to aristocracy, but was not necessarily against constitutional monarchy, when appropriately constrained. Her texts thus offer important evidence of what it meant to be a republican, during the eighteenth century. They demonstrate the continued influence of Locke, Milton, Sidney, and rational religion during the period and offer a partial corrective to the currently popular view, according to which eighteenth-century republicanism was a theory of non-domination, in the sense made popular by Philip Pettit. 15 Macaulay was an early adopter of the language of human rights and her works were particularly significant in relation to the use of the phrase, ‘the equal rights of men’. Since she is so little known, the first aim of the book is to provide a narrative biography, which follows her life and the development of her works chrono logically. The second is to provide an overview and exposition of her ideas, the political and social context in which they were written, and their reception and influence. The first chapter focuses on her family history, neighbouring lumi naries, and the context provided by the discourse on the equality of the sexes and emergence of literary women, during the seventeenth and first half of the eighteenth century. It argues that these developments lead to an intellectual environment sufficiently encouraging for a young woman to take on the enor mous task of writing an authoritative history of the seventeenth-century repub lican struggles. The next chapter turns to the political situation at the accession of George III, Macaulay’s contacts with Thomas Hollis (1720–74) and John Wilkes, and her marriage to the Scottish physician, George Macaulay (1716–66), which introduced her into a milieu with close connections to the intellectual world of the Scottish Enlightenment, in particular, with David Hume’s history, ethics, and political philosophy, which she set out to correct. We then turn to the developing controversy over taxing the Americans, which made her account of the seventeenth-century struggles over arbitrary taxation particularly attractive to readers in America and led to her correspondence with a number of the fathers and mothers of the American Revolution. The story of her relations with American correspondents is continued in the following chapter, along with her involvement in the Wilkes campaign, and the way in which her widowhood left her increasingly vulnerable to salacious gossip and innuendo. Her illness and the destruction of her public persona, her short sojourn in France, and scandalous marriage to William Graham, her junior by twenty-six years follow, as well as the productive period of her second marriage, when she completed her histor ical writings and turned to more philosophical subjects. Her travels in America and France and relationships with both American and French reformers are dealt with in the seventh chapter, while the penultimate chapter provides a detailed account of her two most influential works, the Letters on Education and Observations on the Reflections of the Right Hon. Edmund Burke.

In the last chapter I turn to the examination of Macaulay’s significance for contemporary debates in political philosophy and the history of ideas. There I argue that the republicanism that one finds in her writing is grounded in a tradition of Christian eudaimonism, which combines ancient emphasis on the importance of virtue to a good life with Christian doctrine. Macaulay adopts a generally rational, but at times millennial version of this widespread eighteenth century account of human happiness. The eudaimonistic account of human nature results in a politics based on the rationality of altruism, the possibility of ethical and political progress, and a justification for democracy and repub licanism that differs significantly from standard contract theories. As a result, acknowledging the place played by Macaulay’s writings in the development of ideas of universal human rights, democracy, and feminism acts as an important corrective to a number of influential contemporary narratives in the history of democratic, political thought. Her liberal democratic theory is not based on atomistic or possessive individualism, nor is it a theory of rational egoism, nor is it simply a theory of non-domination. While it does embody an enlighten ment faith in reason, this is a fully embodied human reason, liable to frailty and failures, and requiring tender cultivation and education, by sensitive individuals, who are fully aware of the destructive potential of lack of sympathy, fear, and the many other unhelpful passions to which humanity is subject.

Kent, 1731–60

1.1 Birth and Family 
When, in 1763, Macaulay published the first volume of her history of England, 
she was thirty-two years old. The densely footnoted text that she produced was 
the result of considerable research and intellectual labour and initiated a lifelong 
project that would not be completed for twenty years. It is somewhat extraor 
dinary that a woman, whose family were country gentry of substantial financial 
means, who had married just three years earlier, should have embarked on such 
a huge scholarly enterprise. What was the social and political context that led 
to a woman becoming one of the leading, radical political voices of the second 
half of the eighteenth century in Great Britain? This is a difficult question to 
answer. In an age when many educated women engaged in intense epistolary 
relationships with their female friends, we have no remains of a juvenile corres 
pondence to throw light on Macaulay’s formation. There are no extant diaries 
or memoirs, and the correspondence that does exist goes back only to 1762. In 
the light of these lacunae, we are forced to rely on more general evidence for the 
backstory of Macaulay’s sudden emergence as a fully fledged radical historian; 
her family history, and the political and social milieu in which she grew up in 
Kent during the 1740s and 1750s. 
According to the Canterbury baptism records, Catharine Sawbridge was born 
on March 23, 1730, and baptized on April 18. 1 Bridget Hill gives her birth date 
as April 2, which corresponds to the day of an infamous birthday celebration 
that took place in 1777. 2 The discrepancy is explained by the Calendar Act of 
1751, which omitted eleven days from the calendar. Since this also changed the 
first day of the year from Lady Day (March 25), to January 1, when she was born 
the date was 1730, but now corresponds to 1731. Hence, her date of birth is gen 
erally accepted to be April 2, 1731. 
Her childhood home, Olantigh, in the parish of Wye, Kent, is a grand house 
that still exists, though it was substantially rebuilt in the early twentieth century 
after a destructive fire. The original house had a history stretching back to the 
period of Henry VII. 3 The mansion stands between the Great Stour river and 
a high mound called Barrow Hill, and had been purchased by her grandfather, 
Jacob Sawbridge (c.1665–1748) in 1717, during the years when the South Sea 

Company, of which he was a director, was flourishing. The estate was subse 
quently significantly increased by his eldest son, John Sawbridge (1699–1762), 
so that, by the time Catharine was born, it comprised almost half the parish of 
Wye. 4 Eighteenth-century images show a grand building in the Palladian style, 
and the subsequent rebuilding has retained a columned portico, which can cur 
rently only be glimpsed from the surrounding hills of the now gated and very 
private estate. 
Jacob Sawbridge had been elected the Member of Parliament for Cricklade, 
Wiltshire, with Sir Thomas Read, in 1715, but with the collapse of the South 
Sea Company in 1721, was arrested and expelled from Parliament. Curiously, 
the grandfather of the far more famous historian, Edward Gibbon, born just six 
years later in 1737, had also been a director of the South Sea Company. 5 Unlike 
the young Gibbon—who began reading history at a young age—if we accept 
the testimony of Benjamin Rush, it was not until she reached her twenties that 
Macaulay began to seriously study history, though Mary Hays’s biography gives 
the impression that the young Catharine Sawbridge began educating herself at 
a younger age. 6 
That her early education under the care of an ignorant governess was 
deficient is borne out by her rather rough and inelegant hand and somewhat 
erratic spelling. When the much more carefully educated Elizabeth Montagu 
heard that Macaulay had produced the first volume of her history, she wrote 
to her brother dismissively, ‘If she took her sentiments from her Father, & 
her language from Mrs Fuzzard it must be an extraordinary performance’. 7 
Nevertheless, Mrs Fuzzard, the derided governess, must have provided an 
adequate basic foundation, on which her pupil was later able to build. Since 
Catharine, her sister Dorothy, and two brothers were very close in age, they 
may well have been initially taught together and have received the kind of 
co-education, at home, that she was later to recommend in her Letters on 
Education. 8 
Unlike, Gibbon, Macaulay left no surviving memoir, so it is necessary to piece 
together what she read from hints and probabilities. It has been suggested that 
the scapegoating of their grandfather lay at the origins of his grandchildren’s 
radical inclinations, but there is really little to substantiate this. Sarah Scott, 
Elizabeth Montagu’s sister, observed in the letter she wrote to her sister, sharing 
the news of Macaulay’s emergence as a historian, ‘her father was a republican by 
chance, without knowing how or why, she has taken great pains to make herself 
one’. 9 That this observation that her father was a republican is justified can be 
seen from the works to which he subscribed during Macaulay’s youth, and most 
of his children followed his political predilections. 
The Sawbridges, like the Gibbons, were lucky in having acquired sufficient 
means to have amassed a substantial library, and to have subscribed to many his 
torical works, so that both Gibbon and the young Kitty Sawbridge (as she was 
then called) read extensively, having little else with which to occupy themselves. 
Their ideas were formed by what was available to them in those libraries. Both 
found history more congenial than the other material that was available, though 

Gibbon became deeply involved in religious controversies that had rather less attraction for Macaulay. Macaulay’s grandfather had been able to keep Olantigh in the family by the fortuitous circumstance of having given it to his son, John, as a wedding present. On 20th April the Weekly Journal or Saturday Post had announced,

A treaty of marriage is on foot between the eldest son of Mr Sawbridge of Hackney, and the daughter of Mr. Turner of the Sword-Blade Company; the Gentlewoman is to have 20000 pounds down, and the like sum at the death of her father; and we hear Mr Sawbridge designs to surrender to his son 3000 pounds per annum. 10

Despite its name, the Sword-Blade Company did not make swords, but was a 
banker to the South Sea Company, in which Sawbridge and Turner were part 
ners, with Sir George Caswell. 11 Less than a year later, in January 1721, The 
Weekly Packet, the Daily Post, and other newspapers announced Jacob’s arrest 
and expulsion from Parliament, and the flight of Mr Robert Knight, the Cashier 
of the Company. In May, the particulars and inventories of Jacob’s effects with 
those of the other directors were published by order of the House of Commons. 12 
By February 25 he was reported discharged. Exactly when Olantigh passed to 
John is unclear, but the published documents imply that it was early enough for 
it not to have been included in Jacob’s estate. The assessment and sale of the 
latter’s assets, as valued by the appointed commissioners, dragged on until 1723, 
when John Sawbridge’s financially strong position enabled him to buy back a 
significant portion of the family property. 13 
This first marriage resulted in no children, but John made up for lost time 
with his second marriage to the fifteen-year-old Dorothy Wanley, daughter of a 
goldsmith of Fleet Street, which took place in November 1728, just over a year 
after the death of Elizabeth Turner in July 1727. One year later, on November 9, 
1729, their first child, Dorothy, was christened in Westminster. They must have 
waited for some time after the birth before christening her, for—according to 
pre-Calendar Act dating—Catharine followed in March 1730, a son, John, was 
baptized in April 1732, and another son, Wanley, in February 1732. 14 Dorothy 
Wanley, who was born in 1713, probably had not turned twenty when she was 
buried on March 4, 1733. 15 Her mother’s brief life of constant childbearing was 
rather more typical for the period than Macaulay’s would be. Perhaps fearing a 
similar fate, both sisters married quite late, Dorothy to Stephen Beckingham of 
Kent, in 1758. Two years later, Beckingham was a witness when Catharine and 
George Macaulay were wed. 

1.2 Women’s Literary Advances During the Eighteenth Century One is tempted to conclude that these young women had benefitted from the florescence of feminist works written earlier in the eighteenth century and had read the Reflections on Marriage, which Mary Astell (1666–1731) had published

to considerable acclaim, in 1700, and possibly also her Serious Proposal to the 
Ladies (1697). 16 The Reflections warned women of the dangers of young and 
foolish marriages, and both books recommend that women educate them 
selves sufficiently to be able to live singly, or in case of marriage, to choose 
their husbands wisely. Certainly, both Catharine and her sister put off being 
wed until they were mature and chose husbands of exemplary moral character. 
Stephen Beckingham’s father had been a guardian of the hospital for exposed 
and deserted children, now Coram Fields, and was a pall-bearer at the funeral of 
Captain Thomas Coram, the founder of the institution. 17 At his death at his seat 
in Kent, in 1756, he left large charitable bequests, and his son appears to have 
followed in the philanthropic footsteps of his father. 18 George Macaulay, also, 
was deeply engaged with charitable enterprises, in particular, with the Brownlow 
St Lying-in hospital, set up to care for the wives, when giving birth, of poor 
sailors, soldiers, and working men. 
While her family background does not entirely explain what inspired 
Macaulay to become a historian, the broader context of eighteenth-century 
sociability and the representation of women’s role in the growth of civiliza 
tion helps to account for her aspiration to make her mark as a historian and 
literary figure. 19 The generation of women born towards the middle of the 
eighteenth century were beneficiaries of changing attitudes to women’s educa 
tion and public participation in the literary world, which had been gathering 
pace since at least the middle of the seventeenth century. 20 Renaissance women 
had risked their reputations by engaging in public conversation with schol 
arly men, and while such taboos had been partially lifted during the seven 
teenth century, many women, especially those from noble or wealthy families, 
still feared ‘getting a reputation’ by going into print. Mary Wortley Montagu 
(1689–1762), though a witty poet and opinionated critic, only circulated her 
works in manuscript, or published anonymously. She did not consent to her 
work being published until the very end of her life, when she returned from 
Italy and ensured the publication of the letters that she had written during 
her travels in 1718. These then appeared in the same year as the first volume 
of Macaulay’s history. 21 Despite such constraints, numerous women who were 
less concerned with their reputations had followed Aphra Behn in writing 
novels and plays, and during the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, the 
belief that women were the moral and intellectual equals of men was becoming 
more widely established. 22 François Poullain de la Barre’s De l’egalité des deux 
sexes was republished, unacknowledged, in a modified, English ‘translation’ as 
Female Rights Vindicated in 1751 and 1758. 23 While the 1750s had seen the 
publication of works celebrating Great Britain’s intellectual women, including 
George Ballard’s Memoirs of Several Ladies of Great Britain Who Have Been 
Celebrated for Their Writings and the Works of Catharine Trotter Cockburn. 24 
So that, by the middle of the century, women’s literary aspirations were being 
as often celebrated as execrated. 
The list of subscribers to these volumes gives some indication of the over 
lapping circles of gentry and aristocracy who advocated the promotion and 

celebration of women’s intellectual endeavours. It is unsurprising to find that the subscriber list to Ballard’s collection includes the Right Honourable Ladies, Elizabeth, Henrietta, and Margaret Cavendish Bentinck, as well as their mother, the Duchess of Portland (1715–85). For, in 1739, this duchess had engaged the penurious Saxon scholar, Elizabeth Elstob (1683–1756), to help educate her daughters. Elstob had assisted Ballard and provided him with material for his Memoirs of Several Ladies of Great Britain, and in particular, contributed the biography of Mary Astell that it contains. 25 The Duchess of Portland’s mother, Henrietta Cavendish Harley (1694–1755), had also been interested in literature, and had been Mary Wortley Montagu’s great childhood friend, con tinuing to correspond with her until her death. 26 This family were descended from the husband of the famous seventeenth-century philosopher, Margaret Cavendish (1623–73), and could be counted on to support intellectual women. It is rather more surprising to find among the subscribers, Stephen Beckingham, Commoner of Trinity College Oxford, Dorothy Sawbridge’s future husband, but this indicates that she had chosen as her husband a man sympathetic to the lit erary aspirations of women. 27 The Duchess of Portland also subscribed to The Works of Catharine Cockburn, but of more interest for the background to Macaulay’s endeavours than the subscribers is the editor, Dr Thomas Birch (1705–66), who like Ballard, and per haps for similar reasons, appears to have been particularly open to women’s claims to be taken seriously as thinkers and writers. 28 His family had been Quakers, a group who from their beginning were open to women’s public role as preachers. He had been educated in a series of dissenting colleges, where he applied himself diligently and worked as an usher. 29 Having joined the Anglican Church, and been the beneficiary of a number of livings, he spent his life editing and writing biographies and histories. During the late 1730s both he and Samuel Johnson were friends of the young Elizabeth Carter, who had been carefully educated by her father, and was then publishing works anonymously for the Rambler and The Gentleman’s Magazine. Perhaps inspired by Astell, she had determined not to marry, and her ‘Ode to Wisdom’ was introduced into Richardson’s Clarissa as the work of the heroine, who also expressed a desire to remain single. 30 Birch encouraged Carter to translate Francesco Algarotti’s digest of Newton, written for women, and corresponded with her in Latin, praising her to his friends, and in all probability asking her to be his wife. 31 Certainly, this relationship came to an abrupt end, and Carter—whose father apparently believed that the price that she should pay, for being a literary woman, was to remain single and live a retired life—left London, shortly after a trip to Oxford with Birch, and returned to Deal. Whatever the reality of the their relationship, Birch demonstrated his support for learned women by editing Cockburn’s Works, and near the end of his life we find Macaulay applying to him for books, in his capacity as a trustee of the British Museum. 32 She certainly knew of him as the author of the ‘Life of Milton’, which prefaced his 1738 and 1753 editions of Milton’s historical and political works, one of which is, undoubtedly, the work that she is represented as leaning on in a portrait by Katherine Read (1723–78). 33 Equally, she would have

been aware of his Heads of Illustrious Persons of Great Britain (1743) to which 
her father had subscribed. 
So, although Macaulay’s achievement was exceptional for her time, it was not 
totally unprecedented. By the mid eighteenth century, a substantial number of 
educated English women were going into print, often as poetesses or novelists, 
and women like Catharine and her sister were in the market for women’s works. 
One finds them, for instance, in 1740, among the subscribers to Sarah Dixon’s 
Poems on Several Occasions. 34 Sarah Fielding (1710–67), Charlotte Lennox (née 
Ramsay, 1730/31–1804), and many others had made names for themselves as 
novelists, and the increasing visibility of literary women is emphasized in the 
notice of Macaulay’s first volume in The Monthly Review. 

The great numbers among the Fair Sex, who have figured in the republic of letters, have given frequent checks to the vanity of such as presume that the privilege of thinking is confined to those who wear beards. Not to speak of the learned ladies of antiquity, how many among the moderns, from the French Dacier, to the English Carter, have distinguished themselves in sev eral branches of literature? But it was reserved for the fair Macaulay to tread the path of history, and undergo the laborious task of collecting and digesting the political fragments which have escaped the researches of so many learned and ingenious men. 35

The reviewer, William Rose (c.1719–86), was not entirely happy with this situ 
ation. 36 He warned the members of the ‘fair sex’ that intense study would spoil 
their looks, but his comments attest to the fact that Macaulay grew up at a time 
when women’s contributions to literature were becoming more plentiful and 
more widely accepted. She would very likely have found English translations of 
the essays on Marcus Aurelius, and on Homer’s Iliad, written by Anne Dacier 
(née Le Fèvre, 1651–1720) among volumes in her father’s library, and she cer 
tainly later referred to André Dacier’s Life of Plato. 37 Elizabeth Carter, then 
residing at Deal, was almost a neighbour, and an account of an early conver 
sation between Macaulay and Carter, which took place in the Assembly Rooms 
in Canterbury, during August 1757, is one of the few sources we possess that 
throws light on Macaulay’s early formation. 
At this time, Carter had not yet published the translation of Epictetus’s works 
for which she was to become famous, but was still soliciting subscriptions. 38 
Among the subscribers we find the two Miss Sawbridges, and the occasion when 
these were offered, is related in a letter from Carter, to her great friend, Catherine 
Talbot (1721–70). 

Did I tell you I added about a dozen names to my subscription while I was at Canterbury? Among the rest a very fine lady, who, after curtseying to me for several years past, with more civility than I had any title to, and with much more than fine ladies usually show to such awkward-looking folks as me, did me the honour this year to take to me mightily by way

of conversation, which she introduced by subscribing in a very handsome manner to me, and railing very heartily at the Stoics. She is a very sensible and agreeable woman, and much more deeply learned than beseems a fine lady; but between the Spartan laws, the Roman politics, the philosophy of Epicurus, and the wit of St Evremond, she seems to have formed a most extraordinary system. In walking about the rooms we were joined by one of the most celebrated beauties in the assembly, the study of whose life, as far as can be judged by appearances, has been Mrs Lenox’s sort of coquetry, and there was something extremely diverting to see her listen for a consider able time with the most profound attention to a discourse, which must have been for the most part as unintelligible to her as if it had been delivered in Arabic. To be sure I should have been mightily cautious of holding any such conversation in such a place with a professed philosopher or a scholar, but as it was with a fine fashionable well-dressed lady, whose train was longer than any body’s train, I had no manner of scruple. 39

This account makes it clear that Macaulay had known of and respected Carter for some years prior to the incident, when she used the excuse of making a sub scription, to make her acquaintance. It offers us a number of hints concerning the development of her ideas. We know from Mary Hay’s biography that two major sources for her acquaintance with ‘the Spartan laws, and the Roman pol itics’ were the English translations of the Ancient History and History of Rome, the first by the Jansenist Charles Rollin (1661–1741), and the second, begun by him, but continued by Jean Crevier (1693–1765). Rollin’s Ancient History was first published in 1734, and by the late 1780s was in its eighth edition, the History of Rome was less widely disseminated. Two volumes had appeared in 1739, while the full sixteen volumes were not published until 1754. 40 Both of Rollin’s works throw light on Macaulay’s understanding of the purposes of history, and on her goals in setting out to write the history of Great Britain. That she still set great store by him, twenty years later, is evident from the fact that she ensured that he was among the authors read by her daughter, along with Cicero and Conyer Middleton’s, The History of the Life of Marcus Tullius Cicero, written by the step-grandfather of the Robinson sisters. 41

1.3 The Impact of Rollin’s Histories Rollin’s Ancient History begins with a preface, ‘The Usefulness of Profane History, Especially with Regard to Religion’ which promotes history as neces sary for understanding the factors that promote the flourishing and decline of nations, ‘what it was that constituted their true glory and felicity, and the causes of their declension and fall’. 42 More importantly, Rollin claims to see in ancient history, ‘the greatness of the Almighty, his power, his justice; and above all, the admirable wisdom which his Providence displays in the govern ment of the whole universe’. 43 In this history, Macaulay would have read about the Spartan government, which Rollin outlines in his second volume. 44 It is

represented by him as ‘the most excellent commonwealth that ever had been’ and as offering a mixed system that balanced the various conflicting powers of the kings and the people, by means of the Senate. 45 Through the Senate, ‘the law became the only supreme mistress of the kings, and the kings never became tyrants over the law’. 46 He praises Lycurgus’s decision to divide the land into equal estates, distributed among the people, and his institution of iron money in order to curb the growth of luxury and inequality. 47 Echoes of these ideas will be found in Macaulay’s sketch of a democratic constitution for Corsica, and her abiding belief that excessive inequality is incompatible with good government. 48 Rollin’s History of Rome draws heavily on Livy, Plutarch, and Cicero, and, as well as offering a lengthy but readable account of Rome’s foundation and expan sion, it contains passages that constitute a primer of political theory, at the same time representing history as the workings of providence. According to Rollin,

the commerce of so many different nations, heretofore strangers to one another, and afterwards united under Roman dominion, and in a manner joined by the use of the same language, has been one of the most effec tual means in the hands of Providence to advance the propagation of the Gospel. 49

The second volume begins with ‘Reflections on Polybius’ that introduce the reader to the three forms of government, monarchy, aristocracy, and dem ocracy, and the then popular idea that the most perfect government would ‘include all the advantages, and obviate all the dangers and inconveniences’ of each of them. 50 The Spartans, and the Romans, according to Rollin, achieved this balance. 51 In the case of Sparta, this happened by the design of Lycurgus, whereas, in the case of Rome it simply evolved in such a way that the consuls played the role of kings, the Senate formed the public council, and the people shared in the administration. ‘It was this mutual dependence and concert of all the orders of the commonwealth, that rendered the government of Rome the most perfect that ever was in the world’. 52 In the first volume, his account of the ‘means which most conduced to the greatness’ of the Romans, was slightly different. There he credited, veneration for religion, for though they were ‘mis taken in the object’ they ‘reasoned justly as to the thing’. They were convinced of the importance of the veneration of religious principles ‘by good sense alone, or rather by the remains of natural religion, which cannot be entirely rooted out of the heart of man’. 53 Next in importance were love of country and love of lib erty. 54 ‘The Romans loved their country as the professed enemy of all servitude and slavery. … Their notion of Liberty was, a state wherein a man is subject only to the law, and the law is more powerful than men’. 55 Once again the influence of these fundamental ideas can be seen throughout Macaulay’s history of England, and elsewhere in her works. Just as the famous story of the ousting of Tarquin by Brutus, and the establishment of the consuls, is depicted by Rollin as a tri umph of virtue over vice, so too, the initial plan of her history of the civil war

was to depict the overthrow of the Stuart kings, by the parliamentarians, in the 
same light. 56 
The idea of writing a history of England modelled on the Roman histories 
may well have been suggested to Macaulay by Rollin. He explains that elo 
quence developed in Greece and Rome because the people had to be persuaded 
by politicians to accept legislation, and he draws an analogy between Rome 
and England, suggesting that English eloquence is cultivated because of the 
need to debate in Parliament. 57 He represents the authority of the Senate as 
the authority of virtue, and Macaulay will follow him in praising the virtues 
of the seventeenth-century Parliamentarians. 58 The importance of this late 
seventeenth-century educational work, written by a Jansenist to promote the 
education of young and relatively unsophisticated readers, is somewhat unex 
pected. But it highlights the influence of such popular educational texts for 
the dissemination of ideas during the period. William McCarthy has shown 
how the Conversations on the Plurality of Worlds by Bernard de Fontenelle 
(1657–1757) was decisive in the intellectual formation of Macaulay’s near con 
temporary, Anna Lætitia Barbauld (née Aikin, 1743–1825), who also grew up 
to be a successful literary and political commentator of the age. 59 Rollin’s his 
tories were composed with a similar pedagogic intent to Fontenelle’s, and they 
not only influenced Macaulay but also Brissot de Warville, who would become 
a leader of the Girondins during the French Revolution and Guiseppina di 
Lorena, princess of Carignan (1753–97), who was similarly inspired to develop 
her own political ideas. 60 These histories were also widely available in America, 
prior to the wars of independence. 61 Popular pedagogical works such as these 
allowed women and lower-middle-class men to bring themselves up to date with 
contemporary historical and scientific knowledge, and to participate in scien 
tific, ethical, and political debates, despite their lack of Latin and exclusion 
from formal, higher education. 

1.4 Other Contemporary Influences What can we divine concerning the influences on her thought, from the other elements of the ‘extraordinary system’ that Carter represented Macaulay as having developed—the philosophy of Epicurus, and the wit of Saint-Évremond? If we are to believe Carter, at this juncture, Macaulay railed ‘very heartily at the Stoics’ and was possibly sympathetic to the Epicureans. Later in life, she took the opposite view, arguing in her Treatise on the Immutability of Truth that if mor ality were based on rational self-interest, ‘moderate or true Epicureanism will, on these principles, be the most exalted kind of virtue which can prevail’ and rejecting such a morality as too inconstant and variable. 62 The study of Carter’s translation of Epictetus’s works appears to have rendered her far more sympa thetic to the Stoic doctrine, whose criticism of the Epicureans she is echoing. 63 Although the Treatise was only published in 1783, she there quotes liberally from Carter’s translation, suggesting that this late work built on views she had developed many years earlier. Since, as she points out, the most noble Romans,

‘Cato of Utica, Marcus Brutus, … the two Antonines, and almost every good 
emperor … were educated in the principles of the Stoic philosophy’, her love of 
Roman virtue combined very naturally with an appreciation of Stoicism. 64 
It is not entirely clear how Macaulay came to be influenced by Saint-Évremond, 
nor what lasting impression he made on her. His Works had been published in 
English in 1714, and it is probable that this was the source of her familiarity with 
his wit, though it is not impossible that she read him in French. 65 Her published 
works show little lasting influence of his attitudes, though they are not devoid 
of irony. They evince none of his scepticism with regard to religious belief or the 
afterlife. Indeed, her fundamental criticism of the Stoics is that ‘it is impossible 
that any sound system of religion or morals can be established, but on those 
principles which are consonant to the enlightened reason of man, and which 
form the principles of the Christian religion’. 66 By the time she had matured, she 
was convinced that only an intellectualist theism could ground genuine moral 
principles, and if she had been influenced by Saint-Évremond while young, few 
traces remain in her mature publications. 
Elizabeth Carter was not the only successful literary woman to be found in 
the environs of Olantigh, and the presence of other women, who had published 
to considerable acclaim, was surely a significant factor in Macaulay’s decision 
to undertake the intellectual labours to which she committed herself. The large 
Robinson family, of whom Elizabeth Montagu and Sarah Scott are the best 
remembered, consisted of two daughters and seven sons. As children they spent 
a good deal of time at their mother’s family estate, Mount Morris, near Monks 
Horton in Kent, about seven miles south-west of Olantigh. The Sawbridges 
and Robinsons were clearly acquainted, though not close friends. 67 The Drakes, 
the Robinson’s relatives on the maternal side, were an old established Kentish 
family, in contrast to the more newly arrived Sawbridges. Elizabeth and Sarah 
had received a better education than was usual for the time, and their mother, 
who had been a student of Bathsua Makin (c.1600–81), was probably even 
better educated than they had been. 68 Elizabeth, was an adept, witty, and eru 
dite correspondent and clearly thought little of Macaulay’s politics or intellec 
tual formation. Unlike their neighbour, who had depended for her education 
on a derided governess, the Robinson daughters spent some of their youth in 
Cambridge, where Conyers Middleton, their grandmother Drake’s second hus 
band, had instructed them. The family could be proud of its erudition and wit. 
Elizabeth married in 1742 and would become famous as Elizabeth Montagu, 
‘Queen of the Blues’. She never warmed to Macaulay, abhorred her republic 
anism, and was jealous of her success. 69 Gossips no doubt reported Macaulay’s 
singular choice not to retire after dinner with the ladies, at least when she 
dined with her brother, and this kind of behaviour meant that Montagu never 
shared Elizabeth Carter’s initially favourable assessment of Macaulay. 70 More 
constrained by the proprieties that had earlier governed the literary choices of 
her distant relative by marriage, Mary Wortley-Montagu, Elizabeth Montagu 
only published anonymously. She successfully defended Shakespeare against 
Voltaire’s critique and contributed to George Lyttleton’s Dialogues of the Dead,
