INTRODUCTION In his brief 1882 life of John Quincy Adams, John T. Morse declared that “not an event occurred” during John Quincy Adams’ presidency “which appears of any especial moment…. [N]o grave crisis or great issue was developed in domestic affairs. It was a period of tranquility.” 1 That a student of John Quincy Adams could have made such a statement is almost incredible. It is not, after all, every administration that must deal with a near-civil war, as the John Quincy Adams Administration did for almost its entire four years in power. This near-civil war was the result of tensions between the State of Georgia and the federal government. It began when, in his first year in office, President John Quincy Adams took the unprecedented step of disavowing a treaty between the federal government and an Indian nation. The treaty in question was the Treaty of Indian Springs; the Indian nation in question was the Muscogee Creek Nation. 2 President Adams gave the impression, likely disingenuous, that he disavowed the treaty because it was fraudulent. While the Treaty of Indian Springs’ fraudulency was genuine and was by no means unique among the United States’ treaties with Indian nations, President Adams’ stand against the treaty was unique, and a similar step would never again be taken by an American president. 3

Adams’ refusal to execute the terms of the Treaty of Indian Springs was met with extreme opposition from Governor George Troup of Georgia, who was determined to expel the

The following thesis will examine John Quincy Adams’ response to the Treaty of Indian 
Springs crisis of 1825-1828, attempting to delineate Adams’ complex and often contradictory 
thoughts, while also considering his response’s impact on the Creek Indians. The picture that will 
emerge is one of a moralistic president who became increasingly aware of the shortcomings of 
the US government’s policy towards Native Americans, yet whose long-standing expansionism, 
devotion to the Union, and political pragmatism as a harried ‘minority president’ caused him to 
sign a treaty he knew was fraudulent, and, after bloodshed forced him to abandon that treaty, to 
consistently attempt to have its terms replicated in new agreements with the Creek Indians. 
During the Treaty of Indian Springs crisis, John Quincy Adams’ evolving morality failed to 
change his basic policy towards the Creek Indians. 
An investigation of John Quincy Adams’ thinking during the crisis is much needed. This 
is due not primarily to the dramatic nature of the subject; or even to the light that it throws upon 
US-Indian relations in the 1820s, the history of the Creek people, and the workings of the mind 
of perhaps the smartest individual ever to occupy the White House. The subject of John Quincy 
Adams and the Treaty of Indian Springs is most in need of a new, thorough investigation because 
previous historians have failed to give full or even entirely accurate accounts of this dramatic 
moment in US-Indian relations. Much of the secondary writing on Adams and the treaty is 
spongy, and one is apt to find in it but a hazy view of the proceedings. Mary Hargreaves, in The 
Presidency of John Quincy Adams, claimed that Adams disavowed the Treaty of Indian Springs

This is not quite true, nor is any assertion that pins Adams’ motivation for renegotiating the treaty to his supposedly belated recognition of its fraudulency. Thus, when Daniel Walker Howe, in his history of the US from 1815-1848, wrote that John Quincy Adams “concluded that the Treaty of Indian Springs was a nullity … that the Creeks remained the rightful possessors of their lands,” and that the treaty should thus be overturned, he was also not quite right. 5 Nor was Samuel Flagg Bemis when, in his magisterial two-volume biography of John Quincy Adams, he stated that Adams, in prohibiting the surveying of Creek lands ceded in the Treaty of Indian Springs, was “act[ing] sympathetically to the dispossessed natives.” 6 All such theories, besides their factual inaccuracies, also contain logical confusion. For, why should President Adams, if he did take a moral stand against the Treaty of Indian Springs and show sympathy to the Creeks, subsequently pressure the Creeks to sign treaties that conformed to the governor of Georgia’s expansionist designs, and waffle over preventing the governor from illegally surveying Creek lands?

In histories of the Creek Indians, John Quincy Adams’ motivations during the Treaty of Indian Springs crisis are more accurately depicted, though only sketchily so. This sketchiness on the motivations and actions of the federal government is typical of much scholarship of pre 1830s US-Indian history, as recent historians have focused more on state aggressions against Indians than on federal Indian policy—even though it was federal Indian policy that, in the 1820s, foreshadowed the much-studied national initiatives of the 1830s. 7 It is hoped that the

This thesis follows a simple three chapter structure. Chapter one focuses on the origins of 
the Treaty of Indian Springs crisis and especially on John Quincy Adams’ decision to sign the 
Treaty of Indian Springs. Chapter two is about the initial fallout of Adams’ decision to disavow 
the treaty, as the Adams Administration maneuvered between the wronged Creek Indians and a 
Southern governor contemptuous of federal authority, and negotiated a new treaty with the Creek 
Nation. The last chapter discusses the climax of the crisis and the crisis’ resolution, while 
presenting the final evolution of John Quincy Adams’ views on the United States’ treatment of 
Native Americans. 
As with any piece of writing on John Quincy Adams, this thesis makes much use of 
Adams’ rather blunt private diary. While it is contextualized with and checked against other 
primary and secondary sources, including Adams’ public addresses and modern scholarship on 
Native Americans, Adams’ diary remains the backbone of this thesis. While not an entirely frank 
work, though often perhaps too frank, Adams’ diary is the purest and richest distillation of his 
thoughts that a historian can have. It is full of acute, farsighted observations not just on John 
Quincy Adams’ life but also on the movements and the temper of the nation he served, often 
rightly, sometimes wrongly, for over half a century. 

CHAPTER ONE It was one of the great oddities of John Quincy Adams’ career that he, long one of America’s most passionate expansionists, signed, as President, a treaty ceding much of an Indian nation’s land to the United States—then proceeded to disavow that very treaty just two months later, allegedly over its fraudulency, though Adams latter claimed that that had been a well known aspect of the treaty when he had signed it. The solution to this apparently bizarre set of actions lay in the war clouds that President Adams perceived to be gathering over Georgia in the aftermath of the treaty, as the Georgian frontiers braced for a conflict with the Creek Indian Nation.

The treaty that produced this grave situation had its origins in a land sale by then thirty years old, in which lay almost all future disputes between the federal government and the State of Georgia over Indian lands. The sale was made by the Georgia legislature and consisted of, roughly, present-day Mississippi and Alabama, which Georgia then claimed to possess, though its claim was disputed by the federal government, while Indian Nations actually occupied the land. 1 Nevertheless, in 1795, Georgia sold Mississippi and Alabama to four land companies. In this transaction, the people of Georgia soon discovered, all but one member of the Georgia legislature had had a financial interest. The people also soon discovered that the four land companies had apparently received a bargain deal. A popular outrage developed, causing a new legislature to be elected, which overturned the scandalous sales. But this action angered those Americans who had already bought land from the four companies. With disputes over whether

The Creek Nation, once a mighty confederacy, occupied a “narrow band of … low land” in central Georgia and Alabama, “among the richest cotton land in the world.” 8 White encroachment on Creek territory in the 18 th-century had caused the loose Creek Confederacy to centralize authority in a national council, as the Confederacy, once a military alliance for the land-grabbing Creeks, became a defensive alliance against the land-grabbing Americans. 9

Centralization became especially pronounced in the 1790s, when Benjamin Hawkins, the US agent to the Creeks, saw the great convenience a centralized Creek government would have for a US Indian agent hoping to impose his will on the Creek people. Hawkins’ base of power was in the Creek Nation’s Lower Towns, which were generally more Europeanized than the Upper Towns, practicing plow-agriculture, some of which was done by black slaves. 10

During the War of 1812, the divide between the Upper and Lower Towns was exacerbated, as many Upper Creeks sought to follow Tecumseh’s “campaign for a pan-Indian uprising” and oppose the Lower Creeks’ assimilationism. 11 Those “Red Stick” Creeks who followed Tecumseh got the entire Creek Nation into war with the United States. This proved disastrous for the Creeks. This Treaty of Indian Springs was actually the second Treaty of Indian Springs. The first 
had been ratified in 1821; in it, the Creeks had given up land east of Georgia’s Flint and 
Chattahoochee rivers, in return for which the US government paid the Indians $200,000. 13 The 
new Treaty of Indian Springs extended the Creeks’ land cessions, so that they no longer 
possessed any lands in Georgia and ceded two-thirds of their lands in Alabama. In exchange for 
this, the federal government promised to give the Creeks land west of the Mississippi, to which 
the Creeks could relocate, along with $400,000 for “losses and inconveniences” incurred in 
relocating to unfamiliar and undeveloped land. 14 That the US negotiators downplayed the 
severity of the Creeks moving away from the only land they had ever known as something that 
would merely entail “losses and inconveniences” indicates their limited concern for the Indians’ 
future. 

But this was not the problem that agitated John Crowell, an erstwhile congressman from Alabama serving as the US Indian agent to the Creeks, when, on March 10 th, 1825, he called upon freshly-inaugurated President John Quincy Adams and urged him not to sign the new Treaty of Indian Springs—though President Adams had already signed it, on March 7 th. Crowell’s agitation arose from the fact that the treaty was nakedly fraudulent. In retrospect, John Quincy Adams viewed the treaty’s fraudulency fatalistically, commenting that “nothing could have arrested the progress of this iniquity [the fraudulent treaty], after the Selection of two Georgians, as Commissioners for negotiating the Treaty.” 17 (Duncan Campell and James Meriwether were the iniquitous Georgians in question.) Yet, when President Adams heard Crowell’s claim that the Treaty was fraudulent, he immediately seems to have doubted Crowell, noting that, while Crowell “seemed disposed to urge that [the Treaty] should not be ratified,” “his name is affixed to it as a witness.” 18 Instead of believing Crowell’s concerns, John Quincy Adams here seems to have questioned Crowell’s truthfulness in claiming that the treaty he (President Adams) had just signed into law was a fraud. Thus, it is likely that President Adams knew that the Treaty of Indian Springs was a fraud when he signed it. Why, then, did he sign it? The delineation of Adams’ thinking on the treaty is complicated by the fact that, in December of 1825, in his First Annual Message to Congress, he wrote that he had signed the Treaty of Indian Springs “under the unsuspecting impression that it had been negotiated in good faith and in the confidence inspired by the recommendation of the Senate.” 28 Here, it is likely that President Adams was being disingenuous for purely political reasons. Having stirred up a controversy over the Treaty of Indian Springs by disavowing it, he was anxious to throw the original blame for the treaty’s approval onto the Senate. The evidence for Adams being aware of the treaty’s fraudulency in March 1825 indicates that Adams was, simply, lying in his Annual Message. This, of course, raises questions about the honesty of his other statements regarding the Treaty of Indian Springs. Clearly, when attempting to uncover Adams’ rationale for signing the treaty—and to better understand his actions in the crisis that resulted—it is necessary to go beyond analyzing Adams’ statements on the treaty, and to examine the general history of his thoughts on, and relations with, Native Americans.

John Quincy Adams did not view Native Americans as mere brutes of the forest. Thirty years before 1825, a 27-year-old John Quincy Adams attended a reception given by President Washington for a delegation of Chickasaw Indians. The reception began with a pipe-smoking ceremony, in which each person present had the opportunity to puff at a pipe “twelve or fifteen feet in length.” This ceremony was said to be a Chickasaw custom; yet John Quincy Adams, in

28 John Quincy Adams, “First Annual Message (December 6, 1825),” The American Presidency Project, https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/first-annual-message-2.

his diary, recorded his doubt of the ritual’s legitimacy. The Indians, he said, “appeared to be quite unused to [the pipe smoking], and from their manner of going through it looked as if they were submitting to a process in compliance with our custom.” Adams thought that some of the Indians grinned in a way that “denoted a sense of novelty and of frivolity, too; as if the ceremony struck them as … ridiculous.” 29 Such an observant and shrewd analysis was not the work of a man who had simplistic views of Indians; he implicitly recognized that Indians could be as calculating—as mentally astute—as white people. But this does not mean that Adams was sympathetic to Native Americans. Indeed, in perhaps the most noteworthy of his dealings with Native Americans in his pre-presidential career, John Quincy Adams seemed inclined to treat them with highhanded condescension. This was during Adams’ service on the US peace commission for the negotiations to end the War of 1812—the battles of which had done much to weaken the Eastern Indian nations. 30 During the negotiations, the British had proposed that Indians be given their own territory between the US and Canada, creating a buffer zone between American and British territory. Such a proposal required recognizing the Indian tribes as independent nations. This John Quincy Adams firmly refused, insisting that, during the negotiations, the Indians be treated as wards of the United States, who, during a period of invasion, had taken “part with the enemy.” The Indian tribes, Adams argued, were hardly similar to “civilized” nations; most had not adopted “permanent habitations and a state of property like our own,” and thus only possessed the right to hunt on their lands, not to own and treat with them. 31 (This was a somewhat odd statement to make at a

It is clear, then, that pre-presidential John Quincy Adams had no great regard for 
American Indians. Adams’ priorities lay with protecting the power of the United States and 
increasing the ability of its citizens to settle, and thus bring ‘civilization’ to, western lands. Both 
of these ends could be achieved by annexing more of the North American continent to the United 
States, leaving less space available for European colonizers and Indian nations. It must be 
remembered that John Quincy Adams was a child of the American Revolution, that his family 
had risked everything to make the United States a nation. America’s success in the world, its 
territorial strength, these were objects dear to John Quincy Adams’ heart. As president, he would 
aim to strengthen the Union by an ambitious program of internal improvements, the funding of 
which would come from sales of public lands. 35 Obviously, the receipt of more Indian lands by 
the US government was an excellent way of gaining more public lands to sell. For President 
Adams’ plans for the Union, Native Americans were helpful, but only if they gave up many of 
their extensive territories. 
It would thus seem likely that Adams was quite sympathetic to the aims of the Treaty of 
Indian Springs, which were, essentially, to claim more Indian lands for American civilization. 
Perhaps, even knowing of its fraudulency, Adams was instinctively inclined to sign and support 
the treaty.

As head of the War Department, Barbour, a Virginian aristocrat, was tasked with managing the nation’s Indian affairs. While the Creeks had promised to send a delegation to Washington in the winter and had told General Gaines that they were “anxious to preserve the peace,” Governor Troup was greatly attached to the large cessions of the Treaty of Indian Springs. 6 If he carried his support for the treaty and his disregard of President Adams any further than rhetoric, he would put Georgia in direct opposition to the federal government. John Quincy Adams, by halting the execution of the Treaty of Indian Springs in hopes of preventing an Indian war, had created a situation that brought him face to face with the prospect of a civil war. It was this alarming prospect, along with his sympathy for expanding American civilization, that determined many of John Quincy Adams’ actions in the coming months. The law was entirely on the side of President Adams and the national government. The Treaty, in sweeping language, guaranteed that the Creeks could no longer claim any land in Georgia. “The Chiefs and head men of the Creek Nation,” the Treaty said, “… agree to cede, and they do hereby cede to the United States, all the remaining lands now owned or claimed by the Creek Nation, not heretofore ceded, and which … may be found to lie within the chartered limits of the State of Georgia.” The Creeks were to receive $27,491, in addition to $8,000 for various presents and $7,000 for “the education and support of Creek children” at American-run schools in Kentucky and the Creek Nation. 29 Even after the Creeks’ removal from Georgia, the US government was determined that the Creeks be encouraged to take up American ways of life. The Treaty of Fort Mitchell, with its precipitate negotiations and ratification by the Creek National Council, effectively ended the Treaty of Indian Springs crisis of 1825-1828. end against” the weak Creeks. 32 Even John Quincy Adams, although constitutionally positioned 
against Governor Troup, had shared his ultimate goal of expanding US lands at the expense of 
those of the Indians. 
Indeed, the emphasis Adams placed on his resentment of Troup’s “bluster” against the 
federal government was telling. For with the Treaty of Fort Mitchell, President Adams achieved 
what had been his basic goal during all the Treaty of Indian Springs crisis: The Creek Indians’ 
removal from Georgia. President Adams’ disavowal of the fraudulent Treaty of Indian Springs, 
his agreement to the Treaty of Washington, his cautious response to Georgia’s invasion of Creek 
lands after the ratification of the Treaty of Washington—in all these actions, Adams had 
displayed only a prudent desire to preserve the tranquility of the Union and a lasting conviction 
that it was utterly illogical to allow peoples in a ‘savage’ state of society to waste bountiful 
swathes of the American wilderness which the white man would so eagerly cultivate. 
The critical view of American settlers, completely intolerant of Indian neighbors, that 
John Quincy Adams had developed in 1827 had not significantly challenged his assumptions 
about the superiority of American, over Native American, civilization. Adams may have taken a 
more balanced view of American expansion in 1827, but his views of Indian society had not 
changed. In his final Annual Message to Congress, in December 1828, President Adams 
expressed regret that the United States had been “far more successful in the acquisition of

[Indians’] lands than in imparting to them the principles or inspiring them with the spirit of civilization.” The most noteworthy failure of the government’s Indian policy was not its own immorality, but its inability to bring civilization and Christianity to the Indians, “those unfortunate children of nature.” Adams was perhaps being careful to not arouse the patriotic tempers of congressmen when he attributed the United States’ failure to civilize the Indians to the latter’s own refusal of American charity, as the Indians’ stubbornly formed “communities claiming to be independent of ours and rivals of sovereignty within the territories of members of our Union.” 33

This skeptical attitude towards Native American sovereignty—“communities claiming to 
be independent”—whether a sincere sentiment of the president’s or one expressed only to soothe 
Congress, had at least not made itself apparent in Adams’ dealings with the Creek Indians in 
1825-1828. Little respect as he might have had for Indian society, and much as he might have 
disliked the task, he had always publicly upheld the sovereignty of the Creek Nation—its ability 
to make treaties with the United States, and to do so under only diplomatic, never military, 
pressure. 
Still, to the end of his presidency, John Quincy Adams exhibited only a superficial and 
self-conscious regard for Native Americans. In November 1828, Adams’ reminder to a 
delegation of Winnebago Indians (after he had pardoned two of their chiefs for killing whites 
following lead miners’ encroachment on Winnebago lands) that he “had never pardoned a white 
man” “sentenced to Death for Murder,” came just after he had pressed the Winnebago to cede

CONCLUSION For the Creeks, their expulsion from Georgia was only the beginning of a decade of dispossession. Elected President over John Quincy Adams in 1828, and having received 97% of Georgia’s popular vote, Andrew Jackson, the vanquisher of the Creeks in the War of 1812, had almost immediately implemented a policy of Indian mass-removal. 1 Under the authority of the Indian Removal Act of 1830, the federal government forcibly rounded up almost all the Native Americans of nations east of the Mississippi and transported them to a new Indian Territory west of the Mississippi. In explaining his Indian removal policy to Congress, President Jackson had claimed that it would benefit Native Americans, removing “all possible danger of collision between the authorities of the General and State Governments on account of the Indians,” allowing the Indians to “pursue happiness in their own way and under their own rude institutions,” free from the intrusions of whites. 2

Given the notoriety and era-defining nature of Jackson’s Indian removal, it is worth considering how much of Jackson’s Indian policy had been foreshadowed by that of his predecessor. There was certainly a rhetorical similarity between the two presidents’ policies. John Quincy Adams, like Jackson, had recommended that Congress create an Indian territory to which Native Americans could safely remove from their harassed and ‘endangered’ existence in the East. Such thinking as President Adams and Secretary Barbour’s had reflected the ominous direction in which Indian affairs were moving in the 1820s, as federal officials searched for more

Yet, it cannot be said that much of the actual substance of President Adams’ Indian 
policy was translated into President Jackon’s policy. President Adams had advocated the 
cooperative, relatively voluntary removal of Native Americans west of the Mississippi; the 
highhandedness and severity of President Jackson’s removal policy were quite novel. Nor does it 
seem that President Jackson was ever all that concerned with the morality of US-Indian policy, 
as John Quincy Adams was. 
Adams’ moralistic reflections on US Indian affairs, were, indeed, one of the most 
interesting aspects of his response to the Treaty of Indian Springs crisis, even though they had 
apparently little influenced his course of action. From the course Adams took, it would have 
been easy to assume that he had had only a shallow cultural superiority, or a simple greed, when 
it came to Native Americans. But the reflections that Adams made in his diary in 1828, and his 
1827 study of US Indian affairs, reveal his Indian policy to have been the product of 
considerable thought and research. This does not justify Adams’ actions; but it is nevertheless 
interesting to see that policies which appear so simply condescending and immoral from a 
distance of 200 years were not so simply formed in the white heat of the historical moment. 
Whatever doubts posterity may have, Adams himself always defended his Indian policy, 
in 1841 claiming that “the Policy from Washington to myself … had been Justice and kindness 
to the Indian tribes, to civilize and preserve them.” This had changed when General Jackson 
marched into the White House and “by the simultaneous operation of fraudulent treaties and 
brutal force consummated the work” begun by Georgia in 1830, when it illegally extended its 
jurisdiction over the Cherokee lands within its borders, an “utter prostration of faith and 

justice.” 3 John Quincy Adams seems to have forgotten—doubtless intentionally—his limited response to Georgia’s invasion of Creek lands in 1826-1827 and his lack of response to Alabama’s extension of its sovereignty over Creek lands in 1827. Perhaps Adams’ weak response had encouraged Georgia to follow Alabama’s path. Though, as with many aspects of the Treaty of Indian Springs crisis’ long-term impact, its influence on Georgia’s extension of its jurisdictional is mostly conjectural. A strong federal response against Alabama may have only encouraged Georgia in its truculence. Likewise, a stronger response to Georgia during the Treaty of Indian Springs crisis may have sowed greater resentment against the federal government in the South, strengthening, not weakening, Southern states’ influence over federal Indian policy. Not conjectural were the results of the Treaty of Indian Springs crisis for the Creek Indians, as they removed from Georgia and crowded into Alabama. In the next decade, they faced still harder trials, as the Jackson Administration squeezed them out of Alabama, and as war began between the Creeks and white land speculators and squatters in 1836. Both Alabama and Georgia called out their militias, and President Jackson sent in the Army to finish the job.
