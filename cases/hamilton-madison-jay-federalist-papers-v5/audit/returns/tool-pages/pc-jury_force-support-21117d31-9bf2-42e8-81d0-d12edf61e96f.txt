## IN DEFENSE OF CIVIL JURIES

Peter W. Culley*

I. INTRODUCTION

Criticism of the civil jury system is nothing new. In fact, virtually since its inception the civil jury has been embroiled in controversy. 1 Typically critics argue that: the jury cannot handle the complex or technical case; there is great expense and delay attendant to trial by jury; jury adjudication is of low quality; verdicts rendered by differ ent juries in cases involving similar facts often appear inconsistent 2 Recently, Justice Donald Alexander of the Maine Superior Court stated that "[t]he civil jury system has outlived its usefulness." 3 In support of his basic premise that the civil jury system is merely an historical artifact, Justice Alexander reiterated some of the general criticisms that have been voiced by other commentators. He also raised some specific concerns based on his experience as a trial judge on the Maine Superior Court. Justice Alexander opined that under the present jury system the rights of potential jurors are disre garded, that the financial and temporal costs of the civil jury are too high, and that a jury trial in a complex matter has the potential of depriving the litigants of due process. 4 These problems, in his view, require massive reform of the civil jury system. This Article does not examine these criticisms exhaustively. Rather, it considers some of the concerns raised by Justice Alexan der in light of the Author's experience conducting civil litigation in Maine. On the basis of this experience, the Author believes strongly that the civil jury has continuing vitality and that many commonly held beliefs about shortcomings of the jury system are not justified. 5

To assess criticism of the civil jury and to evaluate whether the institution should be retained, it is important to examine briefly its origin. If the civil jury was an historical accident or today is simply a vestige of abandoned practices and values, then current criticism should be given great weight. If, however, the civil jury was adopted after opportunity for reasoned debate and with an appreciation of its shortcomings, then present criticism of the civil jury should be viewed in a different light. In the latter case, we should assume that those responsible for incorporating the right to a civil trial by jury into our system of justice did so after weighing its costs and benefits. Thus, we should be hesitant to give too much weight to contempo rary objections, which may overlook underlying values. The United States Constitution, as ratified by the several states, did not contain a guarantee of the right to trial by jury in civil cases. 6 As a result, the debate on the ratification of the Constitution contained repeated reference to the lack of guarantees of individual rights. In fact, at least one commentator has claimed that the entire debate over the absence of a bill of rights in the proposed constitu tion was precipitated at the Constitutional Convention of 1787 by objections to the lack of a specific guarantee of trial by jury in civil cases. 7 Anti-federalist opposition to ratification of the Constitution focused prominently on this omission of the guarantee of the right to a civil jury trial, as well as the absence of guarantees of other individual freedoms." The efforts to block ratification because of the absence of guarantees of individual rights is generally considered to have created the impetus for passage of the first ten amendments to the Constitution, 9 including the right to trial by jury. 10 The early state constitutions also contained provisions for trial by jury in civil cases. IV. BENEFITS OF THE CIVIM JURY

It is not sufficient to attempt to refute some of the commonly stated criticism of the jury system. Unless the civil jury has merit, it should not survive merely because it is a venerable and tradition bound institution. 0 The following are some of the benefits of the jury system. A. Equitable Resolution of Hard Cases Without Setting Legal Precedent All judicial decisions contain some degree of arbitrariness.51 The issues commonly presented to juries in tort cases, i.e., the determi nation of reasonable conduct under the circumstances and whether there was a breach of the standard of due care, invite imprecise de cision-making. It is difficult, if not impossible, to define exactly the fine line that separates negligent conduct from conduct evidencing ordinary care. Our law presumes, however, that such a fine line exists. Accord ingly, decision-making in most negligence actions must be based on an intuitive sense of the distinction between acceptable and unac ceptable conduct. While conduct such as the violation of a statutory duty may be evidence of negligence, the factfinder in civil negligence cases usually decides the negligence issue with little guidance from applicable legal principles. The jury is appropriately and necessarily given broad latitude in arriving at a verdict. It reaches a sometimes difficult decision under circumstances which not only encourage frank debate, but also af ford little likelihood of recrimination or scrutiny. By permitting the jury to deliberate secretly and to render a general verdict, by impos ing stringent rules limiting post-verdict interviewing of jurors and impeachment of their verdict, and by assuring relative anonymity of jurors through the selection process, a jury is able to resolve difficult factual disputes without setting legal precedent for future cases that must, even if ever so slightly, differ factually.

The Jury Injects Community Values into Judicial Decisions One of the frequently mentioned benefits of the jury is that it in jects community values into judicial decision-making. This is some times referred to as the policy-making function of the jury. Most civil negligence cases, for example, involve factual settings that in volve no "right or wrong" decision. The facts are often susceptible to a verdict favoring either party. The issue of liability thus depends on whether the parties were exercising reasonable care under the cir cumstances. Resolution of the issue requires an ad hoc determina tion with little reliance on legal guidelines, but rather reliance on common experience in the conduct of everyday affairs. Accordingly, in reaching a decision in such cases, the factfinder must consider experience, common sense, and, perhaps most importantly, a sense of society's tolerance for the conduct at issue. In other words, a de termination of what comports with reasonably prudent conduct under the circumstances requires an implicit definition of a standard of care acceptable to the community. This determination requires more than one factfinder. It requires trial by jury. 52 The jury system, then, engrafts community values onto our law. No single individual can represent the sense of the community. It is difficult to conceive of a better mechanism than the jury for mirror ing community values and establishing standards to which members of the community must conform. The emphasis on the role of the jury in negligence cases should not be taken to mean that the jury is less appropriate in resolving other types of civil disputes. C. The Civil Jury Involves Citizens in Government The jury exposes citizens to the judicial system and involves them in the functioning of their government. As such, the jury is an in strument of participatory democracy. To the extent that these citi zens come away from their exposure to our system of justice with a respect for it and interest in it, the system benefits. The Author's experience and the little data available support the view that jurors feel positively about their service.53 The educational process inher ent in jury service is valuable, although it is not the primary benefit of the involvement of citizens in the judicial decision-making process. More importantly, the citizen-juror fosters respect for the law. If citizens feel that the system of civil justice belongs to them and is adminitered by them, respect for the system will be enhanced. To the uninitiated litigant, the civil court system may appear to be a maze, with lawyers and judges speaking their own language. Because a jury verdict is the verdict of one's fellow citizens, litigants are far more willing to accept an adverse finding from a jury than from a judge. The phrase "the jury has spoken" has not only a definite air of finality, but also connotes a fair trial by one's peers. Confidence and faith in our system is thus bolstered by citizens playing an inte gral role in the judicial system. D. The Jury Protects the Litigant from Judicial Bias One of the more fundamental arguments for use of the jury is that the jury provides a check on the corrupt or biased judge. This is not to suggest that any member of the superior court in the State of Maine is, or ever has been, corrupt. On the contrary, the judiciary of this state has a long unblemished record of outstanding service. The jury is, nevertheless, capable of reaching a result different from that likely to be reached by a judge sitting without a jury. Thus, the jury is capable of checking what may be popularly regarded as legislative or judicial excesses." Bias exists in most people. Each of us is the product of a particu lar educational and social background, and these experiences are bound to influence our important decisions. Equally important is the jury's role in negating the power of a judge. A system in which the judge is the final arbiter of the law and the jury is the judge of the facts provides a system of checks on the power of each. The jury can obviate the effect of a biased judge by finding in favor of the party against whom the judge has shown bias in his or her rulings or demeanor during trial. Similarly, by granting a judgment notwithstanding the verdict, a judge can reverse a jury verdict he or she believes is based on bias or prejudice. E. The Effect on the Bench and Bar if Jury Trials Are Abolished Eliminating civil jury trials would have adverse effects on both the bench and the bar. A few of these are especially worthy of mention. First, one can only wonder at the additional time demands that would be placed on the superior court justices if they were required to sit as judges of facts as well as of the law. Second, the burden on the court to make factual findings would delay the entry of judg ment by necessitating preparation of written findings. Such delay would serve neither the court nor the litigants. Moreover, the quality of advocacy would suffer. Bench trials tend to encourage sloppy presentations, with counsel often imposing on the patience of the trial judge. Presenting a case to a jury, on the other hand, presents pitfalls to ill-prepared or antagonistic counsel. Before a jury, trial counsel engage in rambling inquiry, repeated and technical objections, and time-consuming presentation of evidence only at great risk to their client. Such risk encourages self-discipline on the part of advocates, a trait likely to disappear if bench trials were to become the rule.

Justice Alexander makes some excellent observations regarding improvements that could be made in the present function ing of the civil jury in Maine.
