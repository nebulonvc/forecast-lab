## Alternative Dispute Resolution

Sally Engle Merry

As the study of law moves from an exclusive preoccupation with social control administered by courts to an interest in social control provided by a range of alternative modes of dispute resolution, it is increasingly valuable for the teachers of law to understand the holistic, contextual mbdels of law and society which have been developed within anthropology. The central concern of legal anthropology is the relationship between law and social structure. The perspective is comparative and cross-cultural. Legal anthro pologists look at the relationship between forms of social organization and modes of creating law and handling conflict in various cultural settings. The approach is actor-centered, examining the ways individuals control and are controlled by rules. It is holistic, uncovering the multiplicity of legal systems and dispute resolution niechanisms that exist in any society.' The fundamental question legal anthropologists ask is rather different, from that typically raised in the study of law by lawyers. Anthropologists are interested in the mechanisms that exist for the maintenance of order in a society. They seek to determine the range of processes which produce order and induce individuals to conform to social rules. The courts are only one such process. Many small-scale societies lack state organization and any formal legal apparatus, yet maintain order through a wide range of informal mechanisms such as gossip, fear of ostracism, dependence on others for economic assistance and political support, and mediation by village leaders. These informal systems are pervasive and far older than the earliest states. They persist even within contemporary state societies. Consequently, the legal system itself should rightly be viewed as an alternative, rather than the other way around.

This article elaborates two of the central propositions anthropology has 
contributed to the study of law: 
(1) Any system of social control consists of a range of alternative mechanisms for achieving conformity to the rules of society. 
(2) Law is embedded in social structure and culture and cannot be understood in isolation. 

Alternatives in Non-Western Societies A few specific examples from small-scale societies will illustrate these two propositions. Hunters and gatherers, of whom only a few contemporary examples remain, represent the only form of subsistence thought possible during the first three million years of human evolution. Hunters and gatherers in the contemporary period typically live in small groups of ten to thirty individuals and move from one home to another as food resources are depleted or new opportunities appear elsewhere. One of the best documented groups of contemporary hunter/gatherers is the !Kung San, inhabitants of the Kalahari Desert in Botswana and Namibia in southern Africa. 2 The IKung subsist on gathered vegetable food and meat from hunting and move regularly from one waterhole to another as food resources become depleted. They do not own their land. Use rights are vested in kin groups centered around the longest residents of the area. Social control is maintained by gossip and barbed jokes, by the fear of violence, by "talks" or verbal confrontations in which both sides argue heatedly and attempt to enlist the support of the onlookers, by verbal abuse involving sex, and by "the feet." A disgruntled individual can simply move away, picking up his few belongings and settling at another waterhole where he or she has other kinsmen. The !Kung value peaceful settlement and accommodation highly but, as a last resort, will use the poisoned hunting arrow against an adversary. According to Lee, homicide is not a rare phenomenon: he counted 22 homicides among a population of 1,500 !Kung during the thirty-five years from 1920 to 1955. 3 With the introduction of an outsider who functioned as headman and settled disputes authoritatively, the rate dropped sharply: Lee recorded no intra group homicides from 1955 to 1969. In the pre-headman era, the homicide rate was three times that within the United States in 1972, but that homicide rate excludes the large number of individuals killed in war in the same year. 4 Lee argues that with increased sociocultural integration into larger and larger units, the rate of internal conflict declines while external conflict escalates. To a large extent, informal modes of conflict resolution are powerful because the members of the group depend heavily on one another. They need to cooperate in hunting, gathering, child care, and food sharing in order to survive the rigors of desert living. Getting along and compromising are the costs of continuing membership in the group. However, if they cannot reach a settlement to a conflict, they can still leave the group. Thus, they can avoid

The Nuer cattle herders of the Sudan provide a second example of nonstate social control. When Evans-Pritchard studied them in the 1930s, they were organized into patrilineal descent groups owning and herding cattle in common. 5 Although they were not organized into a state, they had developed a political organization that provided social order for 200,000 mobile cattle herders who needed to cross and recross one another's grazing lands and watering places. The Nuer developed a system of splitting and joining kin groups in situations of conflict which always guaranteed structurally equivalent units on either side. This produced a pattern that Evans Pritchard termed segmentary opposition, providing peace through the process of feud. 6 Grievances such as insult or murder were handled either by negotiating payments of cattle between closely related and interdependent groups or by feuds between more distant groups. The Nuer institutionalized the role of the mediator in the person of the leopard-skin chief, an individual with supernatural but not secular powers who provided sanctuary for the culprit and negotiated a settlement between the hostile lineages. More formal modes of dispute resolution of this type are typically additions to the kinds of mechanisms found among the !Kung. Informal social pressure and the threat of violence occur among the Nuer as well as the IKung. Avoidance-leaving the situation of conflict-is also possible but somehwat more difficult as rights to land and water become more closely tied to particular descent groups. Chinese peasant farmers represent a more technologically sophisticated society in which a state legal system has been superimposed onto existing informal systems. 7 Within stable villages, gossip, fear of scandal, and concern with "face" or family reputation serve as powerful brakes on rule breaking. More formal systems coexist as well. Clan elders mediate disputes within their kin groups and a group of these elders or even elders from another village may mediate disputes between kin groups. The formal legal system has been a remote and inaccessible institution in the past, 8 and the small number of lawyers now in China suggests that the pattern continues into the present. The vast majority of village conflicts were handled informally or through mediation in traditional China. The emergence of formal, state-sponsored systems of social control may coincide with a decrease in population mobility and, consequently, with the reduced use of avoidance as a mode of resolving disputes, both between equals and between the individual and the state. Peasants dependent on complex irrigation systems or with access to farmland in limited fertile areas surrounded by desert or other nonproductive lands are less able to escape the extension of state control. These were the settings in which the first states developed. With state formation came the first formal legal codes and institutions for adjudicating conflict. These examples illustrate possible connections between modes of dispute resolution and social structure. 9 For example, compromise may occur more frequently when parties are relatively equal and interdependent, but less frequently where power differences are great or parties are strangers to one another. Informal social pressures such as gossip, scandal, and the threat of ostracism are most effective in small, close-knit social groupings that are highly interpendant and share the same moral code. Such social systems occur in complex as well as small-scale societies, but are typically more common and pervasive in the latter.' 0 When exit from conflict situations is easy (a product of economic and kinship organization), the need for an authoritative and binding mode of settling conflicts may be less. When indiViduals are locked into strong and enduring relationships, their vulnerability to informal social pressure is greater, but so i5 the need for some authoritative mode of resolving differences that elude these pressures. Dispute-resolution systems are additive. More formal systems add a layer to but do not replace more informal ones. As societies become more complex, the number of coexisting social control systems grows." The role of the state in the maintenance of social control typically expands.' 2

The holistic perspective suggests that a wealth of "alternative" systems already exist outside and in addition to the courts. Most of the regulation of social life, even in the United States, does not take place in the courts or administrative agencies. In the various urban communities I have studied, informal social control systems persist, more or less, within families, friendship circles, and work places. Within city neighborhoods, however, their presence is far more variable and uncertain. To some extent, state institutions have eroded the reliance on neighbors and friends for assistance at times of sickness, unemployment, divorce, death, widowhood, and old age by providing reliable, if minimal, subsidies. Further, because economic life is generally organized on a far larger scale than the neighborhood, neighbors are rarely economically dependant on relationships to each other in the same way that pastoralists or peasant farmers are. These American urbanites typically do not work in the neighborhood where they live. If they do, their neighbors do not routinely control their access to job and income. They commonly deal with conflict by avoiding their neighbors or by breaking off relationships. 4 More affluent Americans invest in spacing their houses and building fences to deal with neighborhood conflict. The kinds of privacy this strategy offers makes avoidance possible without moving out. Divorce and separate housing for aged parents and in-laws represent similar strategies for managing conflict within families. These observations suggest that alternative modes of resolving disputes which attempt to build upon existing informal systems of social control within neighborhoods may find that these systems are weak. Consequently, alternatives may be pressed to assume a different, more legalistic or professional form to blend with the forms of social control already in place and, to some extent, accepted as authoritative and legitimate in American neighborhoods. Indeed, as Silbey and I argue, this appears to be taking place.' 5 Those involved in alternative dispute resolution are themselves actively seeking to redefine their roles and occupational status. There is a burgeoning interest in increasing the professional status of mediators by licensing them, upgrading their training, and converting them into full time employees rather than community volunteers. Mediation programs are more and more attached to existing court systems or social service agencies rather than community or neighborhood groups. How can we explain the popularity of alternative dispute resolution over 
the past decade, from the standpoint of American culture and social 
organization? Here again, the holistic analytical framework of anthropology 
can make a contribution. The interest in alternatives appears to be the 
response to a series of major social transformations in American society. 
First, there has been an upsurge in consciousness of legal rights and the 
ability to exercise these rights in formal legal processes, dating clearly from 
the 1960s, but probably beginning early in the twentieth century. My 
research on disputing in working-class and poor American communities 
revealed a pervasive notion of legal rights at all levels of society. Barely 
literate citizens arrive in the court complaining that their neighbors are 
insulting and harassing them, that their spouses are hitting them, or that 
their children are being beaten in school. This willingness to assert rights 
seems characteristic of long-established Americans, those with at least one or 
two generations' residence in the country, not recent immigrants. Even 
teenagers learn that they have certain rights with respect to their parents. 
They discover that the police and the courts uphold and protect these rights. 
A second major social change is the acceptance of pluralism as a 
characteristic of the moral life of urban communities. If American society is 
an amalgam of different peoples with their own cultures and religions, the 
issue of which moral code should apply to interpersonal relationships 
becomes very problematic. I suspect that the alternatives movement is in a 
sense an effort to deal with this question by sidestepping the problem of 
whose moral order should prevail. By creating a process for resolving 
conflict in which the only standard for the agreement is that both parties 
accept it, no third party must decide which moral code is superior. As Fuller 
observes: 
The essence of the judicial function lies not in the substance of the conclusion reached, 
but in the procedures by which that substance is guaranteed. One does not become a judge 
by acting intelligently and fairly, but by accepting procedural restraints designed to 
insure-so far as human nature permits-an impartial and informed outcome of the 
process of decision.'

Third, the increasing complexity, scale, and stratification of American 
society requires a change in conflict behavior. Alternatives shift modes of 
dealing with conflict. Essentially, they teach that conflicts should not be 
resolved by fighting, arguing, violence, or litigation but by calm and 
rational discussion, compromise, avoidance, and the creation of a contract. 
Mediators often give advice about the value of compromise, the advisability 
of simply staying out of conflict situations, and the importance of talking 
over differences before fighting or going to court. Agreements typically 
include provisions for calm discussion or avoidance to handle future 
problems. The mediation session itself provides an experience in this mode 
of handling conflicts. Data from follow-up interviews of families in the 
family mediation program reveal a significant shift of this kind in the way 
parents and children say they deal with family problems. 
If alternative modes of dispute resolution provide a training ground for 
nonconfrontative, nonlitigious modes of resolving differences, how can we 
explain their emergence at this point in the evolution of American society? It 
may reflect the increasing absorption of the individual into large, complex 
bureaucratic organizations in which the confrontative, violent, or litigious 
individual is increasingly disruptive and threatening to the social order.' 8 If 
the modern capitalist society indeed requires high levels of stability in its 
work force and community life to preserve its prosperity, as Hofrichter 
persuasively argues,' 9 at the same time as the social conditions for maintain 
ing that stability through informal pressures are disappearing, the 
introduction of mechanisms for reeducating and redirecting those who 
demand and assert rights into more conciliatory and less political modes is 
hardly surprising. 
Through the holistic, legal pluralist model of anthropology one can move 
beyond *the question of the effectiveness of alternative modes of dispute 
resolution to ask what they mean for our society and how they will both 
change and be changed by the social structure with which they mesh.
