The single transferable vote (STV) is commonly hailed by would-be reformers as an electoral mechanism that can better reflect the preferences of voters. Rather than casting a single vote for a lone representative in a single -member district, plurality election system or for one political party in a pure proportional representation system, a voter is expected to rank order preferences among alternatives— candidates or parties—presented on the ballot. A number of scholars (among them Lakeman 1970, Bogdanor 1984; Farrell 1997) have made positive evaluations of STV as an electoral system for multimember districts. Proponents have championed the STV as a means to improve representation and to decrease the propensity of wasted votes. However, many studies gloss over the mechanics of how STV operates. Indeed, there is no such thing as the single transferable vote, rather STV is a family of systems grouped by a shared emphasis on ordinal rankings of alternatives (Farrell and McAllister 2000; Bowler and Grofman 1997). In this paper, we consider a few of the implications regarding the application of STV in mass elections. We offer hypothetical examples as well as evidence from the 1997 general election in Ireland. We first review some history of the STV, particularly in Ireland. Next, we consider some formal properties of STV as an electoral system. In particular, we analyze the effects of how methods of counting transferable votes can produce different outcomes. The counting paradox we identify is a problem that is underappreciated in the discussions of and literature on the STV. Finally, we consider the issue on nontransferable votes and make an empirical test of their incidence. Chief among arguments in favor of STV as an electoral system is that voters can order preferences among many candidates and parties. Although the examination of party strategies and voter behavior has produced an important literature (see for the Irish example, Gallagher 1999; Marsh and Sinnott 1999; Marsh 2000), less attention has been given to whether, in fact, many votes can be transferred from one round or count to another. Finally, we discuss our general conclusions and implications for further research.

Adoption of the Single Transferable Vote in Mass Elections 
The single transferable vote emerged in the United Kingdom in the mid-1800s, in response to 
concerns that the single -member district is not proportional and can deny representation to political 
minorities. In 1859, British barrister Thomas Hare first presented the electoral scheme in a publication 
titled Treatise on Election of Representatives. Two years later, John Stuart Mill, in his work 
Considerations on Representative Government, commented on the “perfect feasibility of the scheme.” 
Mill stated “Mr. Hare’s plan [is] among the very greatest improvements yet made in the theory and 
practice of government,” adding that it was “approaching to ideal perfection” for remedying the problems 
that Mill saw with the electoral system used in Britain (Mill 1862). Mill’s concern was that political 
minorities should have a voice in politics, and that other proposed schemes for the U.K., such as multi 
member districts with cumulative voting, did not guarantee a sufficient minority voice. Hare’s scheme, on 
the other hand, had the advantage of giving voters in a broader geographic area “the power of combining 
with one another to return a representative” even if they found themselves in the minority in the local area 
(Mill 1958). 
Similar electoral systems were proposed in the 19 th Century. Hare’s contemporary, Carl Andrae 
of Denmark, proposed a similar system. E.J. Nanson, a mathematician at the University of Melbourne, 
argued for the alternative vote (AV), a variation of STV for the election of a single representative. His 
efforts along with those of Tasmanian attorney general Andrew Inglis Clark and other reformers led to the 
adoption of STV/AV in Australian elections. Today, the single transferable vote is used in many places 
around the world, primarily for local elections. Malta uses STV for national parliamentary elections, and 
the alternative vote is used for federal Senate elections is Australia. Estonia experimented with an STV 
system in 1993. Sri Lanka uses AV for presidential elections and Northern Ireland (UK) adopted STV for 
local and European Parliamentary elections. In the past, STV has also been used in state/provincial 
elections in Canada and Australia, for primary elections in Alabama, and in a number of American cities. 
A number of municipalities in Canada use STV, as does the city of Cambridge, MA, and New York City 

community school boards in the U.S. But it is most commonly associated with Ireland, which uses the 
single transferable vote for elections to both the upper and lower houses of its parliament. 
The use of the Hare system was proposed for Ireland in the late 1800s, when Irish members of 
the British parliament were pushing for Irish “Home Rule.” Many saw proportional representation as a 
way to assure the various factions in Ireland, including the Protestant minority, of a place in a new 
government. Indeed, Arthur Griffith, founder of the Sinn Fein (“ourselves alone”) movement (and later 
the president of the Free State Government during the Irish Civil War) was an early member of the 
Proportional Representation Society of Ireland. But it was not until 1919 that Ireland first used the single 
transferable vote, for local elections held in Sligo. This was followed a year later by its use in local 
elections throughout Ireland (Sinnott 1996). 
Ireland has been using proportional representation for its parliamentary elections since the creation 
of the constitution of the Irish Free State (Sáorstat Éirann) in 1922. The structure of that constitution was 
subject to the approval of the British government under the terms of the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921, and 
many provisions in the document created a decidedly British-style state (Gallagher 1996). But the 
requirement for proportional representation met with the approval of both the Irish, who had already begun 
using the single-transferable vote as a form of proportional representation, and the British, who sought a 
way to guarantee a voice for the Protestant minority under the rule of the Irish Free State (Sinnott 1996). 
In 1937, when the Irish national leader Eamon de Valera presented a new constitution to Ireland 
for a vote, it specified that the single transferable vote would be the form of proportional representation to 
be used in all elections for the Dáil (house) and Seanad (Senate) of the Oireachtas (parliament). But the 
system has not been without controversy, and has been reconsidered twice since then. In 1959, while 
preparing to step down as Taoiseach (prime minister), de Valera led his party to propose a change to the 
constitution to abolish the single transferable vote in favor of a plurality system, apparently out of the belief 
that it would be better for his Fianna Fáil political party. The amendment was defeated in a plebiscite by a 
margin of 52% to 48%. In 1968, the Fianna Fáil party once again presented the amendment to the 

electorate, but this time lost by a resounding 39% to 61% (Sinnott 1966). The system has continued to have its critics, however. Most recently in 1999, a member of Dáil from the Fianna Fáil party has proposed that Ireland switch to an electoral system like Germany’s, where voters in single member districts vote for both a party and a candidate (Coghlan 1999). Although a committee of the Oireachtas held closed meetings on the issue (FitzGerald 2000), several Fianna Fáil backbenchers have publicly indicated their opposition to a change (Donohue 2000).

The Mechanics of Counting Single Transferable Vote Ballots 
The single transferable vote in its ideal form asks much of voters, that they create a full, ordered 
preference profile of all alternatives or candidates listed on the ballot in an election. Voters are asked to 
rank order their preferences by writing “1” next to the their first choice, “2” next to their second choice, 
etc., for as many places down the ballot as desired by the voter. However, STV is even more 
complicated in the way that it actually identifies the candidates elected to office. 
In order to convert the votes to the selection of candidates, a quota must first be calculated. 
Thomas Hare’s original scheme involved simply dividing the number of voters by the number of seats in 
the national (or constituency-wide) election, but this method has been universally abandoned in favor of the 
Droop Quota, which is calculated by dividing the total number of voters by one more than the total number 
of seats to be elected, and adding one to the result. Thus, 
Droop Quota = [(Number of Voters/Number of Seats + 1)] + 1 
This figure is rounded down to the nearest whole number. The Droop Quota is generally favored because 
of its ability to determine the minimum number of votes that a candidate would need in a multi-member 
district to get elected (Gallagher 1992). 
One peculiarity of STV is the variety of ways that the ballots can be counted. All of the polities 
that use STV begin counting by first separating the ballots into piles (or “parcels”) according to the first 

place choices for each candidate. Any candidate who has reached the quota on the first ballot is then declared elected, and the surplus beyond the quota is determined. At this point, there is no universally agreed upon method for what to do next. In elections for the Dáil in Ireland, the counting proceeds as follows. All of the ballots for any leading candidate who has reached a surplus are examined and sorted into the piles (or “sub-parcels”) for the second choice preference or “next available vote.” If the second choice on any given ballot has already been elected, the ballot is examined for the third choice, and so on. The proportion of votes in each pile is then determined, 1 and that proportion is multiplied by the size of the surplus to determine the number of votes to allocate to each of the remaining candidates. For example, if a candidate’s pile has 35% of the next available votes, this candidate is entitled to receive a transfer equal to 35% of the surplus. The number of votes entitled to each candidate is counted off the top of the appropriate sub-parcels and these ballots are added to the top of the candidate’s original pile of first place votes. If the candidate now has reached the quota, he/she is declared the winner. If there is now a new surplus for another candidate, the ballots to be transferred from his/her parcel is determined by examining all of the ballots of the “last parcel received,” that is, the ballots from the sub-parcel that put the candidate over the quota. (This, of course, represents a potential distortion, since the distribution of votes in the “last parcel received” may not be the same as the distribution of all of the votes in the original pile for the newly elected candidate). Again, these are separated into sub-parcels according to the next available vote, the proportion of each sub-parcel is determined, that proportion is multiplied by the surplus and the quotient is the number of papers to be moved from the top of that sub-parcel. If, on the other hand, no candidates have reached the quota, the candidate with the lowest number of votes is eliminated, and his/her votes are distributed according to the subsequent votes indicated on the ballots. The above procedure is followed until all candidates have reached the quota, or the remaining number of candidates equals the number of candidates to be elected (Sinnott 1996).

A second method of counting has been developed in response to an obvious problem with the method used for elections to the Dáil. When transferring the votes from the original parcels, why should the top ballots be chosen? Are they representative of all of the votes in the pile? The assertion in Ireland is that the ballots are sufficiently handled as to constitute a random sample (Sinnott 1996). But others have argued that this presents a potential distortion into the system. A solution to this problem is called the “Gregory Method” of counting, after J.B. Gregory, who developed the system in 1880. Under the Gregory method, vote counters forego the transfer of selected ballots and opt instead to transfer all ballots at the fractional value of the proportion received. Suppose, for example, that Candidate Jones had a total of 500 votes and the quota was only 400. All 500 of his ballots would transfer to their next available vote at a rate of 0.2 each, since 100 divided by 500 is 0.2. If Candidate Smith were the next available vote on 91 of Jones’s ballots, she would receive all of the appropriate ballots at a total of 18.2 votes. The Gregory Method is also used for the transfer of the ballots of the “last parcel received” in subsequent surpluses (Gallagher and Unwin 1986). Fischer (1988) and Gallagher and Unwin (1986) each suggest the necessity of using the Gregory method to avoid potential sampling errors. The Gregory Method is used for the elected seats to the Irish Seanad (Senate) (Sinott 1996), and for elections to the Australian Senate (Fischer 1988). In Malta, the Gregory Method is used for the transfer of surplus votes from the original count, but not the transfer of values for “last parcel received” in subsequent surpluses (Hirczy de Miño and Lane 1996).

Yet another method of counting has been employed in some municipal elections in the United States. This method is called the “Cincinnati Method” in Cambridge, Massachusetts, where it is currently used. As for surpluses in Dáil elections in Ireland, this method entails the switching of selected ballots at their full value. But instead of creating parcels and calculating the proportions to transfer, the Cincinnati method entails numbering the ballots on the original count, and then continuously removing every n th ballot until the winning candidate’s total number of ballots has dropped is to the Droop Quota. To determine the value of n, the total number of votes for the candidate is divided by his/her surplus and the result is

rounded to the nearest whole number. For example, if a winning candidate received a total of 1000 votes, 
of which 230 are surplus, 1000 is divided by 230, and the resulting 4.35 is rounded to 4. Then, the stack of 
ballots is sampled in the order in which they were originally counted, removing every n th ballot. If ballots 
still need to be removed after going through the pile, the process begins again, removing ballots n+1, 2n + 
1, 3n + 1, etc. Candidates receiving these ballots are declared elected once they hit the quota, and 
subsequent ballots marked for them are distributed to the next available vote on the ballot. Thus, there can 
be no new surpluses beyond the first round of counting as in elections using the other two methods. After 
first round surpluses are distributed, subsequent rounds entail distributing the next available votes from 
candidates eliminated for receiving the fewest votes. Candidates are eliminated until the appropriate 
number of candidates has been elected. 2 
Methods similar to the Cincinnati Method have been used in other American cities. In the 1920s, 
for example, Cleveland used the single transferable vote. Although they did not calculate an n to 
determine which ballots to transfer, and merely transferred the quota by taking “as nearly an equal number 
of ballots as possible” from the top of the winning candidates pile from each precinct, they did transfer 
votes, one at a time, only until another quota was achieved (McBain 1922; Moley 1923). They thus 
avoided the problem of needing to transfer subsequent surpluses. 
The avoidance of secondary surpluses by the methods used in some American cities creates 
another potential bias. Since there are no additional surpluses to be transferred, the counting proceeds by 
going to the second choice of the ballots for the least popular candidates. Note that this suggests a higher 
importance on the second place choice of voters who cast ballots for unpopular candidates than it does on 
the next available choice in the surpluses of wining candidates. 

Debating the Virtues of the Single Transferable Vote The single transferable vote is often cited as a favorable alternative to the election of candidates through a plurality in single member districts. This system, employed for elections to the United States House of Representatives and the United Kingdom’s House of Commons, has long been criticized for

some well-known flaws. Most criticism of the single member district system stems from questions about 
its ability to provide proportional representation. The 2001 general election in the United Kingdom is 
instructive. Across the U.K., the Labour Party received 40.7% of all votes cast and received 62.5% of 
the seats in the parliament (412 of 659), the Conservative Party received 31.7% of all votes cast and 
received 25.2% of the seats in the parliament (166 of 659), and the Liberal-Democratic Party received 
18.3% of the vote but received only 7.9% of the seats in the parliament (52 of 659) (see Morgan 2001). It 
can be argued that the composition of the Parliament poorly reflects the will of the people; note that 
Labour has more than 50% more seats than its share of the vote, and the Liberal-Democrats have fewer 
than half the seats for its share of the vote. Similarly, the single member district system is said to provide 
poor representation for candidates who cast ballots for losing candidates. For example, if a voter in the 
United States votes for a Republican but the district is won by a Democrat, has the voter’s voice been 
negated? Critics of the system argue that it has. 
The typical response to these complaints is to call for some form of proportional representation to 
legislative bodies. Indeed, most European countries use some form of proportional representation, typically 
the list system whereby seats in the parliament are allocated to political parties in exact proportion to the 
percentage of the vote received (often with a minimum vote threshold). But these systems, which allow 
the parties to select the legislators, does not match the expectations of voters in countries like the U.S. and 
the U.K., who are accustomed to making the selection of the individuals who hold the seats. 
Thus, many reformers call for the single transferable vote. Supporters typically label the system 
as a proportional representation system, and the system tends to perform well on measures of 
proportionality. Since the number of representatives chosen from each constituency is low (three to five), 
many scholars point out that too few representatives are selected to guarantee proportional representation. 
Typically, the standard is that at least five representatives should be selected from each constituency to 
ensure some reliable level of representation (Taagepera and Shughart 1989; Farrell and McAllister 2000). 
Scholars such as Sinnott (1996) and Marsh (2000) also note that the Irish system centers on individuals 

and not parties. Thus, in Ireland, because of small district size, comparatively weak parties, and the use of 
transferred votes, STV’s proportionality can be approximate only. Still, the Irish system fares moderately 
well in comparative indices of proportionality (Loosemore and Hanby 1971; Lijphart 1990; Mackie and 
Rose 1991). Others approve of the system because of the high numbers of voters whose preferences will 
actually count as effective votes. STV can increase the number of voters whose ballots will effectively 
count towards the election of a candidate, something that Cohan, McKinley and Mughan (1975) refer to as 
the used vote. 
Numerous social scientists have also pointed out various flaws of the STV system. Doron 
(1979b) has observed that it fails the reduction principle. That is, the elimination of a losing candidate 
who is Pareto-dominated by a winning candidate can switch the winning candidate’s victory into a loss. 
Doron and Kronick (1977) label the single transferable vote “perverse” because it allows a candidate to 
lose by acquiring more votes; this is possible because acquiring more votes can change the order in which 
candidates are eliminated. STV violates monotonicity; that is, candidates can lose an election when voters 
place them higher on their order of preferences, or vice versa (Doron 1979a). 
A simple example showing how STV/AV may violate monotonicity can be given when only one 
candidate is selected from a field of three—A, B, and C—candidates. The preference orderings of 17 
voters are provided in Table 1. In the first count, A garners six votes, B six, and C five votes. Candidate 
C is eliminated because she has the fewest voters. The ballots of those who most preferred C are now 
transferred to their second choice, adding three votes for A and two for B. Thus, candidate A wins the 
second count with a 9-8 vote. However, consider the situation if two of the voters who most prefer B 
decide that they prefer the winner A more. This causes A to lose the election. In the first count, A 
receives eight votes, but less than the required majority of nine. Candidate C retains her five votes, but B 
has only four and is eliminated after the first round. Candidate B’s votes are transferred to C, however, 
making her total nine votes. So increasing the relative evaluation of candidate A, the previous winner, 
causes A to lose and C to get elected.

iv Comparing the methods 
The above example illustrates one major flaw with application of the single transferable vote; 
namely, the outcome of the election can depend on the counting method used. The main reason for the 
different outcomes stems from flaws due to the use of sampling in the Dáil and the Cincinnati methods. 
Clearly, an election system where sampling error can easily determine the outcome of an election is 
problematic. Gallagher and Unwin argue that sampling problems may result in election errors in as many 
as one in every twenty seats in Ireland (Gallagher and Unwin 1986). 
A defense of the single transferable vote can be made by arguing that the problems from sampling 
would be eliminated by using the Gregory method. Indeed, Gallagher and Unwin (1986) and Fischer 
(1988) suggest that the Gregory method should always be used rather than other sampling methods. Yet 
while use of the Gregory method does eliminate distortion due to sampling, it does not eliminate all 
problems associated with the single transferable vote. It does not eliminate the violations of monotonicity 
or the reduction problem discussed above. Moreover, it is not a pure counting method as it requires 
problems due to rounding of ratios. 4 
Furthermore, the example considered above illustrates an additional problem that is not solved by 
the Gregory method. Table 3 shows the total number of first and second choice votes for each candidate, 
and indicates the total. Note that the candidate with the greatest number of first and second choice ballots 

is candidate F, with 25 total votes. But F is the only candidate never elected under any of the three counting methods, including the Gregory method! More pointedly, the candidate with the fewest first and second place ballots is D, and D is only elected under the “superior” Gregory method. Finally, the Gregory method has been asserted to create a problem of complexity. Cooper argues that the Gregory method makes the counting process more complicated, since elections in which there is more than one surplus can result in “many different values of vote” (Cooper 1999).

Do STV Ballots Count? 
To estimate empirically the rate of incidence of the counting paradox, monotonicity and other 
potential problems with STV in mass elections, we would need actual ballot images. Unfortunately, ballot 
images are not available to us. The most detailed public information that is available after an election 
consists of the aggregate numbers of ballots for each candidate following each round in each multimember 
district election. The data discussed below come from the 1997 elections to the lower house in the 
Republic of Ireland. 
Using the 1997 Irish elections to the Dáil, we can establish some lower bound estimates of the 
number of ballots that are not marked well enough to count toward a winning candidate. Table 4 reports 
the aggregate number of invalid votes purged by Irish election officials. In the 1997 election, only 1% of 
Irish voters cast invalid ballots, which is not a particularly large rate, particularly when compared to other 
STV/AV voting systems. In part, this is because Irish election laws permit a wide range of ballot marks, 
including an ‘X’ for a single candidate. Other STV systems, particularly Australia’s (which currently 
averages an invalid ballot rate of 3-4% and was about 10% under more stringent rules), are notably less 
lenient. The number of invalid votes also compares favorably with other nations, including FPTP systems, 
as the proportion of invalid ballots in Irish national elections is substantially below that found in France, 
Brazil, and Canada, for instance. Table 5 follows with the number of identified nontransferable votes, 
which were determined as vote transfers were made to other candidates. 5 The ratio of these votes to all 
valid votes provides a (strongly probable) significant underestimate of voters who do not mark ballots in 
ways that can be transferred. A more accurate estimate of votes that cannot be transferred (though this 

is likely to be closer to an upper bound) is the ratio of ballots that can not be transferred to those selected 
for transfer.

There are two other ways to consider this data. First, as the number of alternatives, i.e. officeholders to

necessary to elect candidates increases, we can identify general patterns showing that the proportion of 
nontransferable votes increases. Table 6 shows the percentage of nontransferable votes for each 
sequential count (across legislative constituencies) along with raw numbers of transferred votes and those 
deemed nontransferable. In the 1997 Irish general election, all of the 41 districts took at least three counts 
(the initial preference and two rounds of vote transfers) to identify elected representatives. A majority of 
districts took eight rounds to complete the counting. 

Table 6 reports the numbers of transfer votes—surplus from winning candidates and ballots cast for a removed candidate—as well as the non-transferable ballots for each count or round. The first count, of course, reveals the most preferred preference for each voter, and it occurs before any ballots are transferred. The proportion of non-transferable votes in the second round provides an estimate of the number of voters who “plump” for a candidate—marking for one and only one candidate. Not all votes can be transferred; some do not identify other candidates with lower preference that still remain in the ‘electable’ pool. The empirical data shown in Table 6 give us no information regarding the strategies used by political parties or candidates in each district and how voters responded to them. Nevertheless, we find that a large share of Irish voters, approximately 12%, cast valid votes that cannot be transferred. 9 The emergent pattern matches expectations of a general rise in the proportion of nontransferable ballots. In addition to the values reported in Table 6, a graphical representation of the trend is provided in Figure 1. From the first count to the eleventh, the nontransferability rate follows a relatively continuous upward slope. After the 11 th Count the data come primarily from one constituency—Dublin South Central—and need not reflect general patterns. The proportion of nontransferable votes falls after the 11 th Count, but the sample of districts (1-2) is too small from which to draw conclusions. Based on selected votes for transfer, we would estimate that about 3% of Irish voters plunk for a single candidate (or at least rank only candidates elected during the first round). Typically, the rate of nontransferable votes increases through the first few rounds, then reaches a level for which about one in ten voters have ballots that are case aside in the midrange counts (4 th-7 th). Subsequent counts (9 th-11 th) reveal nontransferable votes about 20-30% of the time. Clearly the evidence suggests that many voters do not represent their full preference orderings, perhaps because they cannot do so, perhaps because they intuitively perceive STV as something more akin to a ranked approval voting system.

Discussion For nearly 150 years, many would-be election reformers have called for the use of the single transferable vote. Traditionally, supporters favor this system as a remedy to perceived flaws with the single-member district system of elections. Our research suggests, however, that such reform would only

replace one set of flaws for another. Even more troublesome, the flaws resulting from STV are more 
hidden and thus can go unnoticed by the electorate. While valid criticisms can be made of the single - 
member district plurality system, we do not believe that STV provides the solution that reformers seek. 
Part of the problem with the use of STV is that it is not really a voting system but rather a 
category of voting systems. Once the ballots are filled out, there is no agreed upon method for counting 
them, yet the counting system is crucial to the election outcome. Even the more favored Gregory Method 
does not guarantee the election of the most preferred candidates. 
Like the single-member district plurality system, STV also has flaws well known to political 
scientists, such as non-monotonicity and the failure of the reduction principle. Our research suggests other 
problems as well. An examination of the use of STV in Ireland, for example, finds a distressingly high 
number of ballots to be nontransferable due either to unwitting or deliberate actions on the part of voters. 
This should be seen as a significant problem, since the entire logic of STV is based on the transfer of 
surplus or unused votes based on a full (or nearly) ordinal ranking. Moreover, ballots may be counted, 
transferred or nontransferable (in whole or in part) under different counting schemes. 
We doubt that STV systems, as currently established, would satisfy reformers who were fully 
informed of its potential flaws.
