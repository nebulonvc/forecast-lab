Abstract This paper examines the question whether information is contained in forecasts from DSGE models beyond that contained in lagged values, which are extensively used in the models. Four sets of forecasts are examined. The results are encouraging for DSGE forecasts of real GDP. The results suggest that there is information in the DSGE forecasts not contained in forecasts based only on lagged values and that there is no information in the lagged-value forecasts not contained in the DSGE forecasts. The opposite is true for forecasts of the GDP deflator. 1 Introduction This paper examines the question whether information is contained in forecasts from DSGE models beyond that contained in lagged values. Lagged variables enter DSGE models through assumptions like habit formation, adjustment costs, variable capacity utilization, pricing behavior, and interest rate rules. Theoretical restrictions are imposed on these variables, and the question is whether predictive information is added by the restrictions? Consider an s s -period-ahead forecast of real GDP. Let Y t a Y^{a}_{t} denote the s s -period ahead forecast of log GDP for period t t from model a a , and let Y t b Y^{b}_{t} denote the same from model b b . The forecasts are assumed to be made at the end of period t − s t-s . The comparison method used in this paper is discussed in Fair and Shiller (FS) (1990). For the s s -period-ahead forecasts for periods 1 1 through T T , the following regression is run: Y t − Y t − s = α + β ( Y t a − Y t − s ) + γ ( Y t b − Y t − s ) + u t , t = 1 , … , T . Y_{t}-Y_{t-s}=\alpha+\beta(Y^{a}_{t}-Y_{t-s})+\gamma(Y^{b}_{t}-Y_{t-s})+u_{t},~~~t=1,...,T. (1) If neither model contains information useful for s s -period-ahead forecasting of Y t Y_{t} , then the estimates of β \beta and γ \gamma should both be zero. In this case the estimate of the constant term α \alpha would be the average s s -period-change in Y Y . If both models contain independent information for s s -period-ahead forecasting, then β \beta and γ \gamma should both be nonzero. If both models contain information, but the information in, say, model b b is completely contained in model a a and model a a contains further relevant information as well, then β \beta but not α \alpha should be nonzero. (If both models contain the same information, then the forecasts are perfectly correlated, and β \beta and α \alpha are not separately identified.) It may be that both coefficient estimates are significant, but one is negative. This means that the information contained in the forecast of the model with the negative coefficient estimate contributes negatively to the overall forecast conditional on the information in the other model’s forecast. One model’s forecasts may have a higher root mean squared error (RMSE) than another’s, but still contain useful independent information. Estimating equation (1) allows one to test for this, which the simple comparison of RMSEs cannot. Further discussion of this method is in Fair and Shiller (1990). The error term u t u_{t} is likely to be heteroskedastic and be a s − 1 s-1 moving average process. This can be corrected for when estimating the standard errors of the coefficient estimates. The procedure discussed in Hansen (1982), Cumby, and Obstfeld (1983), and White and Domowitz (1984) can be used to estimate the asymptotic covariance matrix of the coefficient estimates. When s s equals 1 the covariance matrix is simply White’s (1980) correction for heteroskedasticity. In the next section a set of comparison rules is suggested. Rolling estimation can be used up to the first period forecast, so no future information is in the coefficient estimates. In principle future information should not be used in calibrating parameters, although this may be hard to do.
