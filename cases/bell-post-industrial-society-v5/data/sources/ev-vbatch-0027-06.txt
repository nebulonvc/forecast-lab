Title: Global Financial Shocks and American Elites: Income and Wealth of the One Percent in the United States, 1989 to 2022

Abstract: Extreme levels of inequality have drawn increasing attention to those at the top of the income and wealth distributions, and the United States is home to some of the world’s most affluent and influential households. A robust literature on economic elites in the United States documented details about their incomes and wealth prior to 2019, but it is unclear how recent economic shocks affected their financial resources and the distribution of income and wealth among U.S. households more broadly. We contribute to the literatures on elite households and global political economies by exploring the association between the global economic shock that accompanied the coronavirus 2019 disease (COVID-19) pandemic and U.S. income and wealth. We use data from the 1989–2022 Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF) to provide details regarding changing inequality and the incomes and wealth of the top one percent of households. We explore changes in income sources (e.g., wage, business, capital) and wealth components (e.g., real estate, financial assets) to identify reasons for changing resource composition. We study how the demographics—age and education—of the one percent have changed, and we also explore whether the double-rich—those with both top incomes and top wealth—have changed. Our results are among the first to document the current concentration of economic resources in the United States and underscore the need to incorporate global processes in understanding inequalities in major economies.

By contrast to the growth in income received by those in the one percent, the percentage of total household net worth owned by those in the top one percent decreased during the COVID-19 pandemic. Figure 3 shows that the top one percent of American wealth owners held 37.2% of total household net worth in 2019 and only 35.1% in 2022. The wealth of the next 9% of households also declined modestly during that period, falling from 39.3% of the total to 38.3%. Although these are relatively small changes, they are notable given that they occurred over just three years and because they affected a small number of households. Of course, these changes are also notable because they underscore the extreme levels of wealth inequality that characterize the U.S. wealth distribution. Indeed, whereas the top 10% (the one percent plus the next 9%) together own more than 73% of total wealth, those in the bottom 80% own only 14.6%. It is also notable that the changes that occurred in the wealth distribution during the COVID-19 pandemic were quite different from the changes that occurred during the 2007–09 recession when the wealth owned by the top one percent increased from 33.6% (in 2007) to 34.1% (in 2010).

Income and net worth concentration also changed during the COVID-19 pandemic: the percentage of income received by elites increased, and the percentage of net worth owned by elites declined. As Figure 3 illustrates, between 2019 and 2022, the percentage of total household income received by those in the top one percent of income earners rose from 19.1% to 22.3%. Households in the next 9% also fared better in terms of income during the pandemic. During that time, the income received by the majority of households—the 80% of households that constitute the bottom portions of the income distribution—declined from 39.0% to 35.4%. This increase in income concentration followed a decline in income concentration that occurred between 2016 and 2019, returning income inequality to pre-pandemic levels. The Figure also shows that income concentration moved in the opposite direction during the 2007–09 recession when the percentage of total income received by the top one percent declined from 21.4% (in 2007) to 17.2% (in 2010) and income received by the 80 percent rose from 39.0% to 41.0%.

This trend—which began around 2016—was exacerbated by the pandemic and reflects two important patterns. First, this trend reflects the importance of business start-up and the ownership of business assets for membership in the top one percent by wealth (Keister, Li, and Lee 2021). Previous research using the same SCF data that we use shows that in 1989, approximately 40% of households in the one percent had sufficient business assets to be in the one percent based on those assets alone. By 2019, about 50% of households in the one percent had sufficient business assets to be in the one percent based on those assets alone. Second, this trend reflects the relatively young age of those who own business assets and start businesses (Aladangady et. al. 2023).

Figure 5 shows that there was a decline during the COVID-19 pandemic in the total stock-related assets owned by those in the top one percent: the top one percent of net wealth owners held 54.9% of total stock-related assets in 2019 and only 51.2% of the total in 2022. In contrast, the share of total stock-related assets held by the next 9% of households increased from 38.6% in 2019 to 40.3% in 2022, but the share for the remaining households increased marginally: the share held by the next 10% grew from 4.3% to 5.8%, and the share held by the remaining 80% of households grew slightly from 2.2% to 2.7%. These changes reflect wildly fluctuating values of financial instruments, particularly equities, which account for a large portion of the financial assets of those at the top of the wealth distribution. Although the changes move the U.S. wealth distribution toward more equality, the changes were modest particularly given the large portion of financial assets held by top wealth owners. Indeed, given that households below the top 10% of wealth owners hold relatively few financial assets, there is unlikely to be real change in the distribution of financial assets in the future unless the propensity for the average household to own equities changes.
