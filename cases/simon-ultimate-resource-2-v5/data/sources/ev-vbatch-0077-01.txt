Economics of Nuclear Power (Updated Wednesday, 18 November 2026). The OECD Nuclear Energy Agency's (NEA's) calculation of the overnight cost for a nuclear power plant built in the OECD rose from about $1900/kWe at the end of the 1990s to $3850/kWe in 2009. The French national audit body, the Cour des Comptes, said in 2012 that the overnight capital costs of building nuclear power plants increased over time from €1070/kWe (in 2010 prices) when the two Fessenheim PWRs were commissioned, to €2060/kWe in 2000 after Chooz B1&2 were built, and to a projected €3700/kWe for the Flamanville EPR. In the USA, Vogtle 3&4 (two AP1000s, 2234 MWe total) entered commercial operation in 2023 and 2024 respectively, at a total cost of about $35 billion. In the UK, EDF estimated in February 2026 that the two-unit Hinkley Point C plant (3.3 GWe) would cost £35 billion in 2015 values, excluding interim interest. In its Annual Energy Outlook 2025, the US EIA noted that no new nuclear capacity is projected to be built in its reference case, reflecting high capital costs estimated at $7821/kWe overnight (in 2024 values). Its 2026 edition assumed a total overnight cost of $8255/kWe for a new large light water reactor plant (in 2025 values). For large nuclear plants built in the near term, the median LCOE was $141/MWh. This compared with $130/MWh for coal and $98/MWh for gas (CCGT). The report also gives costs for 'Nth-of-a-kind' (NOAK) nuclear plants, built once construction learning effects have been fully realized. Their median LCOE was $81/MWh. In the 2025 edition, overnight capital costs for near-term large nuclear plants ranged from $8760/kWe in Canada to $14,940/kWe in Australia. For NOAK plants they ranged from $2487/kWe in Korea to $6791/kWe in Australia. Fuel costs have been one area of increasing efficiency and cost reduction. For instance, in the USA, fuel costs declined by 40% between 2012 and 2025 according to the Nuclear Energy Institute.
