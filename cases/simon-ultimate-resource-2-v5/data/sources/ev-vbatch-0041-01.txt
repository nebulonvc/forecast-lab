New IEA report shows Strait of Hormuz shock rippling through energy consumption patterns worldwide, with lasting efficiency gains needed to strengthen energy security and resilience
The global energy crisis is distorting energy consumption patterns around the world, with significant implications for efforts to improve energy efficiency in 2026 and beyond, according to a new IEA report .
The IEA's latest annual assessment of global energy efficiency trends finds that primary energy intensity – a measure of the energy needed to generate a unit of economic output – is expected to improve by nearly 3% this year, up from around 2% in 2025. But the forecast of a stronger annual advance in energy efficiency comes with caveats following an unusual year for the energy world. In 2026, global energy demand is projected to remain at broadly the same level as in 2025, held back in part by the impacts of the Strait of Hormuz crisis, which has disrupted energy supplies and pushed up prices. At the same time, the global economy has remained resilient and is expected to grow by around 3%.
The new IEA report finds that while the adoption of more efficient technologies and electrification continues to support underlying efficiency gains, the energy crisis has also driven reductions in energy consumption as households and businesses are forced to cut back or change their activities. This has also brought economic and social costs in some areas, particularly in regions that are most exposed to supply disruptions.
Governments have responded to the crisis with a wide range of measures to protect consumers and strengthen energy security. Around 120 countries have introduced measures to save energy, provide financial relief or improve longer-term resilience. While most have focused on immediate relief and energy conservation, more than 30 governments have also introduced longer-term measures to improve energy efficiency or accelerate electrification.
“Energy efficiency is one of the most powerful tools countries have to strengthen energy security, lower consumers’ bills and improve economic competitiveness,” said IEA Executive Director Fatih Birol . “This year’s energy crisis has shown once again how vulnerable economies and households around the world remain to disruptions in fuel markets. Governments have an opportunity to turn today's challenges into lasting gains by accelerating investment in efficient technologies and electrification, while strengthening the policies that make their energy systems more resilient."
Oil demand is expected to decline in most regions in 2026, while natural gas demand is also set to fall globally, reflecting supply disruptions and higher prices, according to the report. Energy-intensive industries have been particularly affected, with some businesses reducing production or shifting to less energy-intensive activities.
At the same time, global electricity demand is expected to grow by around 3.6% this year, supported by the continued electrification of transport and other end uses, as well as investment in artificial intelligence, data centres and advanced manufacturing.
Amid strained global oil supplies, efficient electrification is helping avoid additional demand for oil products. In China, electricity demand for electric vehicle charging increased by more than 50% so far this year, while in the European Union, sales of battery electric vehicles rose by 50% between January and September compared with the same period in 2025.
The report highlights the uneven impacts of the energy crisis across regions. Countries in Asia Pacific, particularly those heavily dependent on energy imports from the Middle East, have experienced some of the largest changes in energy intensity. In some cases, shortages have forced households to switch cooking fuels and businesses to curtail operations, underscoring the importance of distinguishing structural efficiency improvements from crisis-driven reductions in consumption.
The IEA analysis finds that nearly 80% of governments that have introduced longer-term policies included measures to support efficient electrification, particularly electric vehicles and heat pumps. Such measures can deliver lasting benefits for consumers, with the combination of heat pumps and improved building insulation offering the potential to reduce household heating bills by up to 90%.
At COP31 in Antalya this month, many countries supported a collective goal to improve the global energy intensity of buildings by at least 25% by 2035. IEA analysis shows that this goal could be achieved through wider deployment of technologies and policy measures that are already available today. This builds on the ambition established at COP28 in Dubai in 2023, when nearly 200 governments agreed to work together toward a goal of improving global energy intensity by 4% annually by 2030. Improvements have averaged around 1.5% annually so far this decade, highlighting the opportunity to accelerate progress.
The report emphasises that previous energy crises have demonstrated the potential for governments to translate short-term disruptions into lasting improvements in energy efficiency. For instance, within a decade of the oil shocks of the 1970s, new cars in the United States needed roughly half as much fuel to travel the same distance, while refrigerators in Japan used around a third less energy. Today, both are around three to four times more efficient than they were in the 1970s. In that period, global energy intensity has fallen by 50%, while GDP has increased fivefold. These improvements were driven by policies and standards that continued to deliver benefits well beyond the immediate crisis. The same opportunity exists today – through stronger efficiency standards, sustained investment and policies that accelerate the adoption of efficient technologies.
