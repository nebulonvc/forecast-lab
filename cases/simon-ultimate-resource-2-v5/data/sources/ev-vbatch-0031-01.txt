DESALINATION AND AGRICULTURE: BALANCING OPPORTUNITIES AND CHALLENGES IN THE CONTEXT OF CHANGING CLIMATE POLICY BRIEF DESALINATION FOR AGRICULTURAL DEVELOPMENT DECEMBER 2025 CONTEXTE Climate change and increasing water scarcity are posing significant challenges to global agricultural production, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions. Over 70 percent of global freshwater consumption is dedicated to agriculture, and yet, many regions are experiencing significant water scarcity, exacerbated by the changing climate. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), through initiatives like the Global Framework on Water Scarcity in Agriculture (WASAG) and the Regional Initiative on Water Scarcity for NENA region (WSI), is exploring non-conventional water sources to boost water availability for agriculture. Desalination, the process of removing salt and impurities from seawater or brackish water, has emerged as a potential solution to secure water supplies for agriculture. Currently, the worldwide installed desalination capacity is expanding at an exponential rate, generating more than 100 million cubic meters of fresh water daily from approximately 19,000 desalination facilities (figure 1). The rapid development of desalination technology has made it a critical component for ensuring the sustainability of global water resources. However, its integration into agricultural development requires balancing technological, economic, and environmental trade-offs. This policy brief explores the role of desalination in agricultural development, addressing its challenges and opportunities in the context of climate change and the global agricultural commodity market. It outlines key policy recommendations to maximize the benefits of desalination while addressing its challenges. FIGURE 1. Technological Advances • Reverse osmosis (RO) has become more energy-efficient, with modern plants consuming 3–4 kWh/m³ (figure 2). • Brackish water desalination offers up to 90 percent recovery rates with significantly lower energy use. • Innovations like brine mining (extracting minerals such as NaCl, Li, and Br) offer revenue opportunities and reduce environmental impacts. 2. Agronomic Opportunities and Concerns (Table 1) • High-value crops (e.g., fruits, vegetables) are the most viable for desalinated water use, while feasibility for strategic crops (e.g., cereals) requires further research. • Desalinated water can introduce phytotoxic elements or lack essential nutrients. When blended with other water sources or amended with nutrients, desalinated water can safely irrigate high-value crops (e.g., vegetables, fruits). • Combining desalinated water with reclaimed water can improve soil health and nutrient supply. 3. Economic Sustainability • Costs for large seawater desalination plants (> 400 000 m3/day) range from USD 800–1 300/m³/day, while smaller systems (< 20 000 m3/day) cost USD 2 000–4 000/m³/day. • Brackish water desalination is more affordable (USD 0.2– 0.4/m³) and suitable for small and medium farming systems. • Socioeconomic assessments, including non-monetary benefits (e.g., reduced groundwater overexploitation, protecting soil health or avoiding conflicts), are critical for project viability. 4. Environmental Sustainability • Desalination can support renewable energy integration (solar, wind). • Careful management of brine discharge is essential to avoid environmental degradation. • Desalination provides a reliable water source for agriculture in coastal regions 5. Social Acceptance • Farmer acceptance depends on water costs, crop value, and water quality. • Multi-stakeholder engagement, education, and economic incentives are essential for fostering acceptance. 6. Capacity Building • Training for farmers, operators, and decision makers is crucial for sustainable desalination adoption. • Platforms like the Observatory of non-conventional water resources and associated renewable energies facilitate knowledge sharing. 7. Policy and Governance • Integrated Water-Energy-Food-Ecosystems (WEFE) policies are essential for sustainable desalination. • Subsidies should focus on investments, while tariffs must cover operational costs to ensure financial sustainability (figure 3).
