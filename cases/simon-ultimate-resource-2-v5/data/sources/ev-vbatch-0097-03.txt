Medical care prices and overall health spending historically outpace growth in the rest of the economy. Health costs represent a growing share of gross domestic product, and many American families have seen the costs of health services and health insurance premiums grow faster than their wages.
There are several inflation indices that measure healthcare price growth from the consumer or producer perspectives, covering different goods and services and using different methodologies. This analysis primarily examines inflation in prices by urban consumers using Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U) data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). This brief also discusses inflation in prices received by healthcare providers and pharmaceutical manufacturers using another BLS index, the Producer Price Index (PPI). Other indices that can be used to track healthcare prices include the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index and the Personal Health Care price deflator, which are not included in this analysis.
This KFF analysis describes both short-term and long-term inflation trends to provide a more nuanced understanding of how healthcare price growth compares to price growth in the rest of the economy.  While overall consumer price inflation has exceeded medical inflation in recent months, medical care prices have risen substantially faster than overall consumer prices over the long term. There is also considerable variation in inflation within medical care, with price growth among hospitals outpacing price growth for other medical goods and services, as well as other sectors of the economy. This brief presents inflation data through October 2026, though annual percent changes for the CPI are shown for September 2026 due to missing data following the October 2025 government shutdown.
Key findings include:
Medical care prices cumulatively grew 33% more than overall consumer prices between January 2000 and October 2026.
Hospital prices have grown the most. Since 2000, prices for hospital services have quadrupled (314% growth), a much higher level of inflation than other medical goods and services and key non-medical expenditure categories in that period.
Inflation from September 2025 to September 2026 across all consumer goods and services (3.7%) exceeded medical inflation (1.5%).
Medical care prices have risen 33% higher than overall consumer prices since 2000
Between January 2000 and October 2026, prices for medical care—including insurance, drugs, medical equipment and general healthcare services—have increased by 133%. In contrast, prices for all consumer goods and services rose by 100% in the same period.

Hospital prices have more than quadrupled since 2000, far outpacing inflation in major expenditure categories
There is wide variation in inflation among the goods and services that comprise the medical care CPI-U, sometimes exceeding inflation in other sectors of the economy. Between January 2000 and October 2026, prices for hospital services have quadrupled (314% growth), the highest level of inflation among medical care indices available from 2000.
Hospital prices have also grown faster since 2000 than any of the eight major groups of goods and services in the CPI-U: apparel, education and communication, food and beverages, housing, medical care overall, recreation, transportation, and other goods and services. As with medical care, inflation varies widely among the components of each of these groups. For example, college tuition and fees have tripled (208% growth) since 2000, a much larger cumulative change than the broader education and communication group (47% growth).
In the same period, both nursing homes (179%) and dental services (163%) have experienced faster price growth than medical care overall (133%) and other major sectors of the economy. By contrast, prices for prescription drugs have nearly doubled (94%) since 2000, similar to inflation of all consumer prices (100%) but lower than non-medical sectors like housing (118%), and food and beverages (109%). Meanwhile, prices for physicians’ services have grown 84% since 2000, less than both overall medical care inflation and inflation across all consumer prices.
