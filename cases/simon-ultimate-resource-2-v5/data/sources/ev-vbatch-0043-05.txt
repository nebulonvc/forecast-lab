Nuclear power is cost-competitive with other forms of electricity generation, except where there is direct access to low-cost fossil fuels.

World Nuclear Association published Nuclear Power Economics and Project Structuring in early 2017. The report noted that the economics of new nuclear plants are heavily influenced by their capital cost, which accounts for at least 60% of their LCOE. Interest charges and the construction period are important variables for determining the overall cost of capital. Once a nuclear plant has been constructed, the production cost of electricity is low and predictably stable.

The OECD Nuclear Energy Agency’s (NEA's) calculation of the overnight cost for a nuclear power plant built in the OECD rose from about $1900/kWe at the end of the 1990s to $3850/kWe in 2009. The NEA's latest estimates are in The Costs of Generating Electricity 2025 , published in 2026 jointly with the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI). It is the tenth edition of the series previously published with the International Energy Agency (IEA) as Projected Costs of Generating Electricity. The median overnight cost for near-term large nuclear plants was $11,188/kWe. For 'Nth-of-a-kind' (NOAK) plants, built once construction learning has been realized, it was $5502/kWe. Country figures are given in the section below on Comparing the economics of different forms of electricity generation.

There are also significant variations in capital costs by country, particularly between the emerging industrial economies of East Asia and the mature markets of Europe and North America. Variations have a variety of explanations, including: differential labour costs; more experience in the recent building of reactors; economies of scale from building multiple units; and streamlined licensing and project management within large civil engineering projects.

In its Annual Energy Outlook 2025 , the US EIA noted that no new nuclear capacity is projected to be built in its reference case, reflecting high capital costs estimated at $7821/kWe overnight (in 2024 values). Its 2026 edition assumed a total overnight cost of $8255/kWe for a new large light water reactor plant (in 2025 values).

For large nuclear plants built in the near term, the median LCOE was $141/MWh. This compared with $130/MWh for coal and $98/MWh for gas (CCGT). The report also gives costs for 'Nth-of-a-kind' (NOAK) nuclear plants, built once construction learning effects have been fully realized. Their median LCOE was $81/MWh. For small modular reactors, the median LCOE was $146/MWh for near-term and $84/MWh for NOAK plants. Long-term operation of existing nuclear plants was the cheapest low-carbon option where available, with a median LCOE of $44/MWh. Median LCOEs for onshore wind and solar PV were $59/MWh and $67/MWh. These figures exclude system costs.

In the 2025 edition, overnight capital costs for near-term large nuclear plants ranged from $8760/kWe in Canada to $14,940/kWe in Australia. For NOAK plants they ranged from $2487/kWe in Korea to $6791/kWe in Australia.
