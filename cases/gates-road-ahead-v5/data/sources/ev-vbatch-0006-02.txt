Institute for Business in Global Society Harvard Research: Fewer than 1 in 700 get hired without a college degree Share via Facebook Share via LinkedIn Print Share via email BiGS Actionable Intelligence: Companies and public sector employers have been eliminating college degree requirements, but a new Harvard study shows a gap between intent and impact. To make a transition to skills-based hiring, managers must be trained to spot promising candidates, take risks when filling positions, and change corporate culture to give workers pathways to success. Read the job postings released by some of the biggest companies in the United States, and one might think a college degree is becoming less important in the hiring process. Many companies have eliminated the requirement.
However, a study by Harvard Business School in conjunction with the Burning Glass Institute shows that there is a difference between policy and practice. In short, a few companies that dropped degree requirements have hired more people without degrees.
The study examined more than 11,000 job postings at U.S. firms from 2014 to 2023 and found that hiring of non-degree job candidates increased by just 3.5 percentage points. When researchers took into account the fact that the 3.5% applies only to the small number of jobs where the degree requirement was eliminated, it falls to less than a percentage point. Overall, fewer than 1 in 700 new hires benefited from the no-degree reforms.
In a country in which almost two-thirds of Americans do not hold a four-year degree, college enrollment is declining , and companies are clamoring for skilled workers, the data shows how difficult it is for companies to change corporate culture even when it benefits both companies and workers alike.
At companies that are leading the way in skills-based hiring, non-degree hires have a retention rate that is 10 percentage points higher than their colleagues with degrees, according to the study. Non-degree workers who were hired into roles that previously required a degree saw an average salary increase of 25%.
“We found measurable but pretty modest changes, and that some companies have done better than others,” said Joseph Fuller, a professor at Harvard Business School who led the study. “But overall, removing the barrier that a post-secondary degree poses to hiring more broadly will take a lot of doing.”
Eliminating the traditional credential of a college degree is not a new idea, but the concept became more popular during the COVID-19 pandemic when companies were facing a tight labor market and America was undergoing widespread protests over racial injustice. The idea that employees can be hired for skills and experience, rather than based on a credential, caught on.
Scores of companies and more than 20 state governments loudly proclaimed their adherence to this new ethos, and many made solid progress. For example, Apple, Cigna, ExxonMobil, General Motors, Koch Industries , Target , Tyson Foods , Walmart , and Yelp , as well as government employers like the State of Minnesota and the City of Denver , have succeeded in their aim, on average hiring 18 percent more non-degreed workers, according to the study.
But many more did not. In fact, the study found that almost all of the changes in actual hiring took place in just 37 percent of the companies that were examined.
“When viewed from the vantage point of job postings, skills-based hiring seems to be a juggernaut,” the study said. “However, a deeper analysis of actual hiring patterns suggests a wide gap between intent and impact. Simply dropping stated requirements seldom opens jobs to those who don’t have a college degree.”
If companies want to truly put skills-based hiring into practice, they need to do far more than rewrite job postings, Fuller said. They need to change the way hiring managers evaluate applicants, shake up a corporate culture that has for generations created a “paper ceiling,” and implement methods to support applicants from nontraditional educational backgrounds after they are hired.
“Changing your hiring policy is, at best, the end of the beginning,” Fuller said. “Or maybe it's just a plain old beginning if you are aiming to accomplish, frankly, something more than virtue washing.”
