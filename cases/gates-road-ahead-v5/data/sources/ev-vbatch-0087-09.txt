AT&T's plan to retire the bulk of its copper network by 2030 was recently bolstered by FCC approval for the company to discontinue legacy copper services in 10% of its footprint. AT&T's Susan Johnson offers a progress update.
As announced late last year , AT&T is on a mission to shut down power-hungry and broadly inefficient copper-based services across the bulk of its US footprint by the end of 2029.
AT&T's current initiative spans copper footprint in 20 states. That will rise to 21 if and when California decides to join the copper phase-out party. Copper phase-outs in California remain on hold for AT&T, which is trying to work with policymakers and community members there on a path forward. However, there's no announced timeline for a potential resolution.
More broadly, a big pole in the tent for copper network shutdowns is ensuring that the few customers who still get services from AT&T over copper will get a like-for-like – and in some cases a better – replacement for their existing plain old telephone service, or POTS. Alongside local and long-distance phone calls, that means support for call forwarding, caller ID, E911, fax machines, alarm systems and medical monitoring devices. AT&T's answer to that is AT&T Phone-Advanced , an alternative that supports existing POTS devices via wireless and fiber networks and allows customers to keep their existing phone numbers.
AT&T is making progress. The FCC recently approved AT&T to discontinue legacy services in about 10% of its footprint. That approval, considered a significant milestone for AT&T, is set to take effect in June 2026.
"That's a pretty big, scaled filing across 500 of our wire centers," Johnson said.
The approval for this initial batch of wire centers – which serve as the central office/voice switch and contains elements such as the remote terminals and the copper lines themselves – also is a clear indicator the FCC is confident in the products AT&T has developed to enable that migration, Johnson said.
