The Promise of Election Markets
The Perils of Election Markets
In 2026, Americans will stake more than $1.4 billion on prediction markets tied to the outcome of U.S. midterm elections. Millions of voters, alongside major trading firms and private companies, will partake in the speculative frenzy. And election markets will permeate—not to mention price—the zeitgeist, as millions more monitor the odds on exchanges, television networks, and social media.
This trading boom has provoked vociferous opposition from Democratic Party leaders , state governments , and much of the electorate. At issue are two questions: one of legality and one of prudence. The first is whether election markets are legal to begin with—specifically, if they qualify as “swaps” under federal commodities law and can therefore be offered nationwide. The second is whether the utility of election markets outweighs the risks they pose to the United States’ moral fabric, democratic process, and financial markets.
The swaps question is being litigated fiercely, and may eventually be decided by the Supreme Court, while the question of prudence has sparked an equally intense debate. But until the judiciary renders a final verdict, election markets will operate at scale with the Trump administration’s support. In the interim, policymakers must develop a first-principles understanding of election markets, and, drawing on this assessment, update an outdated regulatory regime.

Election markets have the potential to serve the public interest as tools for political forecasting and hedging political risk, but existing regulations invite fraud and insider trading. Left unchecked, such abuse will harm market participants and, more important, undermine public confidence in the democratic process. To mitigate those risks, Congress and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), the independent agency charged with regulating these markets, must work together to define and enforce new legal standards for insider trading in election markets; require public disclosure of large positions to deter manipulation and expose conflicts of interest; pressure offshore exchanges to adopt U.S. standards; and study the financial impact of election markets on retail traders. Congress must also adequately fund the CFTC.
Long prohibited by law and CFTC rules, election markets now enjoy the robust backing of the Trump administration. In May, President Donald Trump declared that prediction markets—a new breed of derivatives exchanges which allow users to trade on elections, sports, and a wide array of real-world events—“ will thrive ” during his presidency. (His son Donald Trump Jr. is a major investor in one exchange, Polymarket, through 1789 Capital , and a paid advisor to another major exchange, Kalshi.) Trump’s handpicked CFTC Chairman, Michael Selig, is similarly supportive and has loosened the rules governing prediction markets to enable the listing of contracts tied to sporting events and elections. Under Selig’s leadership, the CFTC withdrew a proposed Biden-era ban on election markets, drafted rules to help election markets become a permanent fixture of U.S. derivatives trading, and joined the Department of Justice to sue nine states to stop them from enforcing their gambling laws against prediction markets, including vis-à-vis elections.
The Trump administration’s support is now the subject of a sprawling legal battle—comprising some 120 lawsuits across 28 states—among leading prediction markets, the states, and the CFTC. This litigation centers on whether the Commodity Exchange Act permits federally regulated exchanges to list event contracts on sports and elections, and, if so, whether it preempts state gaming laws. Federal courts are divided on this question, particularly regarding sports, and legal experts expect that ongoing litigation will take years to resolve.
All the while, election markets have become one of the fastest-growing segments in the U.S. derivatives industry. This year, U.S. trading volume on markets tied to the 2026 midterms has eclipsed $1.4 billion, and markets tied to the 2028 general election are forecast to surpass $10 billion. The mainstream adoption of election markets has likewise surged: a recent Paradigm/Echelon Insights poll found that more than a third of likely voters have used prediction markets in some form, whether to trade or simply check prices. Meanwhile, CBS, CNN, Fox News, the Wall Street Journal , and the social network X, among others, have struck agreements with the two leading prediction markets, Kalshi and Polymarket , to integrate election market odds directly into their 2026 midterm coverage.
Policymakers and voters, however, are still assessing the core costs, benefits, and mechanics of election markets. Are they accurate? Are they vulnerable to manipulation and insider trading? Are they of tangible economic benefit? Are they a risk to election security? And are they immoral?
A growing body of literature answers a number of these questions. Recent analysis has found that election markets are a uniquely accurate mechanism for forecasting political results and public opinion.
