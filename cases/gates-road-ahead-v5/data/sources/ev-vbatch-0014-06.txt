Optic fiber cables form the backbone of global communications. An often-cited statistic is that submarine cables carry approximately 99% of the world's Internet traffic . Unfortunately, the typical framing of that statistic contains a subtle error. It is true that approximately 99% of internet traffic is carried by undersea cables, but this refers to 99% of intercontinental traffic. In a well-functioning internet, the majority of traffic for a typical user is not likely to travel via undersea fibre optic cables but remain on national terrestrial networks. 
To know where these are, the World Bank Group has supported the development of the Open Fiber Data Standard (OFDS), a common language for describing and mapping terrestrial fibre optic networks. Since 2022, the work has been guided by a consortium of organizations, including the World Bank Group, the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), the Internet Society, Mozilla, Liquid Intelligent Technologies, CSquared, and Digital Council Africa. Since that time, the importance of global internet resiliency has risen dramatically in the wake of multiple disruptions to fibre optic cables, most recently affecting air traffic control in the US. OFDS is more important than ever as a result. 
When we connect to the internet, our interactions are often localized because we are communicating with people or services in our own countries or region. When we access international services and content, we are often served by local Content Distribution Networks (CDNs). The globally distributed nature of a CDN means reduced distance between users and website resources. Instead of connecting to a website’s origin server, a CDN lets users connect to a geographically closer data center. Less travel time means faster service. It is true that CDNs must be refreshed from their global sources, but their very existence suggests that local traffic predominates. 
Our growing social and economic dependence on internet infrastructure, combined with some serious cable breaks in the Red Sea, off the coast of Côte d’Ivoire , and elsewhere in recent years, has focused more attention on the need for a more resilient global internet infrastructure. Much-needed effort is now being placed on critical submarine cable issues such as route diversity and the availability of repair ships. However, if we accept that terrestrial fibre optic infrastructure plays an equally important role in internet infrastructure resilience as submarine cable infrastructure, we should ask why terrestrial fibre has not received the same level of attention. Telegeography, a telecommunications data and research firm, maintains an excellent resource in its submarine cable map, tracking the development and lifespan of more than 600 undersea cables. Resources like this provide wonderful visibility for submarine cable infrastructure. However, the absence of similar data on terrestrial fibre optic infrastructure perpetuates the idea that terrestrial fibre is of lesser importance. The very lack of visibility of terrestrial infrastructure in these maps diminishes its significance.
