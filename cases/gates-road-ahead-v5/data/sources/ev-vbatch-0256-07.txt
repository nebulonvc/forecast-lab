Kate Green Tripp Wudan Yan Photo: Yuliya Yesina Adolescents today are more digitally connected than ever before.

Most teenagers in the U.S. have a smartphone, and nearly half told the Pew Research Center in 2024 that they’re online "almost constantly," browsing social media platforms that are designed to capture and hold their attention.

But the impact of phones on kids is still debated. On one hand, this widespread connectivity has been linked to rising rates of depression and anxiety in youth; in schools, teachers have also reported that smartphones can be highly disruptive to learning. On the other hand, smartphones enable kids to do plenty of crucial things in their daily life: connect with friends or parents, access school materials, listen to music, hop on a bus, buy a snack, or look something up in the moment.

Stanford economist Matthew Gentzkow, the Landau Professor of Technology and the Economy in the School of Humanities and Sciences and Faculty Director of Stanford Impact Labs, has had a front seat to this debate, as part of his research on phones and kids.

“The question I wrestle with isn’t whether phones are bad or good,” he says. “The question I’m motivated to answer is: In a world where attention-grabbing cell phones are ever-present, and very capable of offering kids access to both good and bad influences, what measures work to help kids learn, grow, and thrive?” Gentzkow’s research stretches across contexts—looking at what works to support youth well-being in both home and school environments.

In recent years, one approach being aggressively tested is policies that either restrict phones during class or ban phone use outright at school. Nearly all school districts in the U.S. are now experimenting with new policies, and parents, educators, state lawmakers, and researchers alike are eager to see what results will emerge.

With support from Stanford Impact Labs, Gentzkow teamed up with Hunt Allcott, professor in the Stanford Doerr School of Sustainability; Jason Baron, assistant professor of economics at Duke University; Thomas Dee, the Barnett Family Professor at Stanford Graduate School of Education; Angela Duckworth, the Rosa Lee and Egbert Chang Professor at the University of Pennsylvania; and Brian Jacob, the Walter H. Annenberg Professor of Education Policy at the University of Michigan to generate evidence on how school phone policies affect student outcomes.

“A key thing that has been missing from the conversation about phones and kids is clear data on school cell phone policies. Through the amazing efforts of our interdisciplinary team, we were able to assemble the first comprehensive national data set on these policies and use them to provide credible estimates of the impacts of phone policies,” says Gentzkow.

Against a backdrop of varying restrictions and bans, Gentzkow, Duckworth, and team chose to collect phone use data from a variety of schools. They opted to focus their initial study specifically on schools where lockable pouches were deployed to separate students from their devices. By comparing schools with an unambiguous intervention to those without one, the research could take clear shape.

The schools in the study purchased and distributed magnetically sealed pouches made by Yondr, a San Francisco-based company, to their students. (As of early 2026, a reported three million students across U.S. schools were using Yondr pouches.) These schools also followed a similar daily protocol: When students arrived each morning, their phones would be placed in pouches, and they generally would not be able to access them until the end of the day.

Deciding what indicators to measure became a collaborative effort. Early conversations with educators gave the researchers a better grasp of what most worries teachers about phones at school, and those concerns shaped both the survey structure and the outcomes the team tracked: phone usage, test scores, attendance, student well-being, and even online bullying.
