Younger and LGBTQ Adults in the U.S. Stand Out for Finding Friendship Online
The Pew-Knight Initiative supports new research on how Americans absorb civic information, form beliefs and identities, and engage in their communities.

For roughly a quarter of Americans, the internet is where they found friends who are part of their life today . And for many of them, these friendships don’t just stay online – they’ve met in person, too.
Young adults are more likely than older Americans to find friendship online, according to a new Pew Research Center analysis from the Pew-Knight Initiative . Today, 43% of adults under age 30 say they first met at least one of their friends online.
Three-in-ten Americans in their 30s and 40s also say they have one or more friends they met online. The share drops further for older age groups. Still, about one-in-ten adults ages 65 and older say this is the case.
LGBTQ adults stand out for having friends they met online, too: 45% of adults who are lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender or queer say they met at least one of their friends this way. By comparison, 22% of those who are not LGBTQ say this. (This difference holds up even when accounting for factors like age.)
But not everyone is going online to make friends. Most Americans met all their friends without the internet’s help.
This analysis from the Pew-Knight Initiative looks at the role the internet plays in friendships. It explores how many Americans have friends they met online and whether those relationships have become in-person connections, too.
The Center conducts research to inform the public, journalists and decision-makers. We’ve long studied how technology shapes human connection and how this varies across groups . At a time when many say the internet both connects and divides , we wanted to understand who’s found friendship online.

We surveyed 9,750 U.S. adults from May 26 to June 1, 2026. Everyone who took part in this survey is a member of the Center’s American Trends Panel . The survey represents the views of the full U.S. adult population.

Meeting friends on the internet doesn’t always end there. Friendships that start online often lead to in-person meetups.
Among those who have friends they first met online, the vast majority (85%) say they’ve met at least one of them in person.
This is widely true across demographic groups. For example, eight-in-ten or more in each age group say they’ve gotten together with at least one of the friends they met online.
Though our broader survey found that about half of Americans doubt the value of relationships people form online , those who have found friendship on the internet feel differently.
Among those with at least one friend they first met online, 62% think these kinds of relationships can be just as meaningful as in-person connections. Even for those who haven’t met those friends in person, a majority say this.
For those who met all their friends off the internet, that share falls to 25%.
