BT has been talking about a retreat from the PSTN for 20 years, but exchanges it uses for the PSTN's copper lines will still be around in the 2030s.
The ancestors of BT, the UK's digitizing phone incumbent, first began to crisscross the country with a network of copper lines in the late nineteenth century. For more than 100 years, this public switched telephone network (PSTN), as it is called in the industry, has provided landline services for UK residents. But for the past 20, BT has been struggling to switch off its old analog systems and move to a digital, Internet Protocol (IP) successor. Realizing in May last year that it would miss the latest deadline of December 2025 for a full PSTN shutdown, it has given itself another 13 months to finish the job. Further delays would surprise no one.
Initial attempts to abandon PSTN technologies were made as long ago as 2005, shortly after BT had started to talk about something it called 21CN, the grand-sounding twenty-first century network project. These days it seems to be a footnote in BT's history. Executives would rather not be reminded that 21CN was linked at the time to a retreat from the PSTN. But the annual report BT published for its 2005 fiscal year is unambiguous.
"Technical trials began in the 2005 financial year," said BT in the section about 21CN. "For example, we launched a voice transformation trial, moving voice traffic from the traditional PSTN (public switched telecommunications network) onto an IP network." In its report the following year , BT said it expected the full national rollout of 21CN to be "substantially complete by 2010."
Fortunately, there was also a 55% drop in PSTN service hours lost last year, said Ofcom, thanks to speedy adoption of digital voice services by UK residents. The problem for Openreach, the part of BT that builds and looks after network infrastructure, is that some customers are not moving quickly enough. In extending its deadline for PSTN switch-off to early 2027, BT blamed the need to protect vulnerable people using telecare systems that are still based on the analog platform. Meanwhile, BT is repeatedly having to warn its customers that traditional phone services will "stop working" after January 31, 2027.
What's more, whether or not BT meets that target, it will certainly continue to have copper lines in service after January 2027. Its network goal, by that date, is to have reached only 25 million premises with its full-fiber network. As financial statements show, that figure was more than 6 million short of BT's total broadband network footprint in March. In many areas, BT provides a fiber-to-the-cabinet service it calls SOGEA (single order generic Ethernet access), which dispenses with the analog phone line. Elsewhere, it relies on older copper-based technologies. Almost 1.66 million of its broadband connections used ADSL in March.
That number is falling dramatically and was almost 1 million higher in March 2023. But it must obviously fall even more rapidly in the next 18 months if ADSL is to vanish. BT also still had almost 1.57 million non-broadband connections in March offered through its wholesale line rental (WLR) service, which it plans to withdraw in January 2027 with the PSTN shutdown. The number fell by only 870,000 in the preceding two years.
