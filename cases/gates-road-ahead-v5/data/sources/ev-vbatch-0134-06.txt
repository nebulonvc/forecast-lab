In the early- and mid-1990s, real-time 3D graphics became increasingly common in arcade, computer, and console games, which led to increasing public demand for hardware-accelerated 3D graphics. Early examples of mass-market 3D graphics hardware can be found in arcade system boards such as the Sega Model 1 , Namco System 22 , and Sega Model 2 , and the fifth-generation video game consoles such as the Saturn , PlayStation , and Nintendo 64 . Arcade systems such as the Sega Model 2 and SGI Onyx -based Namco Magic Edge Hornet Simulator in 1993 were capable of hardware T&L ( transform, clipping, and lighting ) years before appearing in consumer graphics cards. [ 59 ] [ 60 ] In 1994, Sony used the term GPU (with the meaning graphics processing unit) in reference to the PlayStation console's Toshiba -designed Sony GPU. [ 3 ] The term "GPU" was coined by Sony in reference to the 32-bit Sony GPU (designed by Toshiba) in the PlayStation video game console, released in 1994. [ 61 ] Another early example is the Super FX chip, a RISC -based on-cartridge graphics chip used in some SNES games, notably Star Fox (1993) and Doom (1995). Some systems used DSPs to accelerate transformations. Fujitsu , which worked on the Sega Model 2 arcade system, [ 62 ] began working on integrating T&L into a single LSI solution for use in home computers in 1995; [ 63 ] the Fujitsu Pinolite, the first 3D geometry processor for personal computers, announced in 1997. [ 64 ] The first hardware T&L GPU on home video game consoles was the Nintendo 64 's Reality Coprocessor , released in 1996. [ 65 ] In 1997, Mitsubishi released the 3Dpro/2MP , a GPU capable of transformation and lighting, for workstations and Windows NT desktops; [ 66 ] ATi used it for its FireGL 4000 graphics card , released in 1997.
