This report from the Pew-Knight Initiative focuses on online community and connection today. It looks at who’s part of online communities, where they form, what they’re built around, why people go to them – and why some people leave. It also examines what people think the internet is doing to connection overall.
The Center conducts research to inform the public, journalists and decision-makers. We’ve long studied what online connection looks like for Americans . At a time when fewer than half of Americans are highly satisfied with their social life – or with the quality of life in their local community – it’s important to understand the role online communities play, good and bad.
We surveyed 9,750 U.S. adults from May 26 to June 1, 2026. Everyone who took part in this survey is a member of the Center’s  American Trends Panel . The survey represents the views of the full U.S. adult population.

At a time when many are worrying about fraying social ties and rising loneliness , some Americans are finding community online.
A new Pew Research Center survey from the Pew-Knight Initiative finds that nearly four-in-ten U.S. adults are part of an online community – and that share rises to about half among young adults.
For some, these communities are deeply valuable parts of their lives. They widely offer information, advice or experiences people say they could only get online.
Still, these benefits sit alongside doubts about making genuine connections online. About half of Americans question whether online relationships can be as meaningful as those formed in person. Fewer – 34% – think they can be just as meaningful.
There’s an age divide on this topic as well. Adults under 30 are far more likely than those ages 65 and older to say online relationships can be just as meaningful as in-person ones (54% vs. 14%).

By this, we mean people connecting with others online, such as by joining or feeling part of a group or doing things together. This could happen in places like social media, online discussion forums, by emailing or messaging with a group, or in video games online.
Follow-up questions focused on the community people spend the most time in – we call this their most-visited online community.
More than two decades after the Center studied online communities in 2001, these spaces still vary in where they form, what they offer and who turns to them.
It’s most common to find online community on social media. About a quarter of Americans are part of an online community on social media sites or apps.
Discussion forums come next – 15% are part of an online community in places like Reddit, Nextdoor or Discord.
Fewer are part of an online community on video sites like YouTube and Twitch, in video games online, or on messaging apps.
And 5% are part of one on sites or apps for people who share a hobby – like Goodreads for reading, Strava for running, Ravelry for knitting or Letterboxd for movies.
When it comes to what these communities focus on: Hobbies and local area top the list. About one-in-five Americans say they are part of an online community related to each.
Roughly one-in-ten adults say they’re in one about topics like movies, books or TV shows; video games; or their work or profession. A similar share says they’re in fan communities of any kind.
And while politics can come up in a range of places, 8% of Americans are in an online community specifically related to politics or political issues.

Age, again, is an important factor: Young adults are more likely than all other groups to be part of several kinds of communities. For example, that’s true for online communities about video games; beauty or fashion; movies, books or TV shows; hobbies; and fan communities.
Gaming has one of the biggest gaps. About a quarter of adults under 30 (27%) are part of one about video games. This drops to 16% of those in their 30s and 40s and 4% of those 50 and older.

Online communities are widely seen as unique spaces and can add deeply to people’s lives.
About a quarter of those in online communities say these places are highly valuable to them. A larger share – about four-in-ten – finds some value there.
Together, about seven-in-ten say these spaces add something to their lives. Still, 29% of those who are part of online communities say they bring little to no value.
Both younger and older adults tend to find at least some value in these places. But adults under 50 in them are more likely to say they add deeply to their lives, compared with older adults (32% vs. 20%).
It’s common to be part of multiple online communities. In fact, 85% of those in online communities are part of more than one.
To better understand people’s experiences, we asked some questions about the one they visit most often.
First, we asked people to tell us in a few words what the focus of their most-visited community is.
These covered a vast array of topics, such as a specific hobby, neighborhood, musical group, medical condition or identity, for example. Other communities focused on connecting and talking with others.
Not only do some people find deep value in these spaces, but many also see them as offering something unique that they cannot get elsewhere.
About six-in-ten in online communities say their most-visited one is something they could only find online. A smaller share says it’s something they could find elsewhere.
People seek out online communities for a variety of reasons, but there are three near the top of our list: to stay informed, for fun or to get advice or recommendations.
About eight-in-ten or more say they go to their most-visited community for each of these. This includes more than half who cite information and fun as major reasons.
Smaller shares – though still majorities – say a major or minor reason they visit their go-to space is to share their thoughts or get support.
Just over half also say they go there because they feel accepted. And fewer say it’s to meet new people or for the drama.
Young adults are more likely than older age groups to turn to online communities because they feel accepted or to meet new people – but also for the drama.
For example, when asked about their most-visited online community, roughly six-in-ten under 30 say they go there to meet new people. Among those in their 30s and 40s, fewer (46%) say the same. And this share drops further for those 50 and older.
The pattern is similar for seeing if there’s drama or feeling accepted. But those in their 30s and 40s are just as likely as young adults to go to these places for support, advice or recommendations, or to share their thoughts.
And on some reasons – like staying informed – there is little difference by age. Most across groups say this is a motivator.
There are many ways online communities can bring value to people’s lives. All told, meeting people is fairly low on the list of motivators. We see that reflected in people’s feelings, too.
About one-in-five say they feel extremely or very close to others in these spaces, and an additional 37% feel somewhat close.
But nearly half don’t feel very close to others in their most-visited community. That includes 14% who don’t feel close at all.
People tend to start fresh in these spaces. Most (68%) knew very few or none of the people there before joining.
And it’s less common to feel close to others if you knew few people starting out. Of those who knew very few or no one, about one-in-ten say they feel extremely or very close to people there today.
Even so, these spaces start conversations that are often part of members’ daily lives. The vast majority of those in online communities – 74% – say they ever post or share in the one they visit most.
And nearly six-in-ten (56%) say they go to their most-visited community every day. That’s one-in-five U.S. adults overall who say they’re in an online community daily.
Not all online communities are limited to the internet. In some, the line between online and offline is blurred. When asked about their most-visited space, about a third say that it has in-person gatherings, too.
When we ask Americans for their take on online relationships more broadly – regardless of if they’re in an online community or not – we find many doubt the value of online connection.
About half of Americans overall say personal relationships formed online are not as meaningful as in-person ones. About one-third say they can be just as meaningful, while 20% aren’t sure.
