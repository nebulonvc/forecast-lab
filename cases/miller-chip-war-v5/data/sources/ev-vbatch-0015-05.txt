TSMC reportedly pauses shipments to Chinese designers after U.S. export rules flag decade-old 16nm and 14nm nodes — new BIS controls threaten smartphone, TV, and automotive chips. Washington’s previous export controls only focused on high-performance AI chips like Nvidia’s Blackwell and upcoming Vera Rubin chips. The new rules expand it to include all chips, with front-end fabricators (chip foundries) or OSATs (outsourced semiconductor assembly and test companies), also known as packaging and testing companies, required to assume that semiconductors with a 16nm or 14nm node are automatically classified as export-controlled advanced computing chips. The 16nm and 14nm nodes have been around for around 10 years now, and they could even be found inside consumer devices like smartphones and TVs, as well as in automotive applications. This new rule means that designers making chips using 16nm or newer nodes, even if they’re not specifically built for data centers, must secure documentation from an approved designer certifying the chip’s Total Processing Performance or performance density if they want to avoid additional scrutiny from the fab.
