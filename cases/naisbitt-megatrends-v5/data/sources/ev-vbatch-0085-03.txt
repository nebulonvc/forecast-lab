However, employee involvement did not gain wide acceptance by industry until the 1980s (Boxall & Macky, 2009; Cappelli & Neumark, 2001). By the middle of the 1990s there was significant growth in "high-involvement" work practices (also labeled "participative," "flexible," or "high-performance" practices) in the U.S. and Europe. Foreign competition and technological changes spurred widespread "reengineering" in large corporations (Hammer & Champy, 1988). Firms were under pressure to downsize and, in many cases, to cut large numbers of managers and eliminate hierarchy. This resulted in reduced direct supervision of front-line employees and the use of teams. It gave front-line employees more responsibility for quality and production decisions. By 2000, studies showed that some form of employee involvement practices were in place in a large proportion of workplaces in industrialized countries, including half of all U.S. firms and two-thirds of the Fortune 1000 (Cooke, 1994; Freeman, Kleiner, & Ostroff, 2000; Gittleman, Horrigan, & Joyce, 1998; Kling, 1995; Osterman, 1994; O'Toole, Lawler & Messinger, 2007). Over the last 10 years there have been no new notable large-scale surveys of employee involvement work practices in the U.S.. In the US the last available surveys indicate that the rates of adoption of employee involvement have leveled off or begun to decline.
