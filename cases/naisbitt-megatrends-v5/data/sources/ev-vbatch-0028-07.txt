As the United States grapples with aging building stock and the need for infrastructure modernization, a critical workforce challenge threatens the commercial real estate sector’s ability to maintain, modernize, and operate the built environment. JLL’s latest skilled trades talent research reveals that by 2030, an estimated 2.1 million skilled trades positions for electricians, HVAC technicians, plumbers, pipefitters, construction equipment operators, general maintenance workers, and more could go unfilled. With the U.S. Department of Education reporting potential economic losses could reach $1 trillion annually, this shortfall of skilled trades workers poses substantial risks to business continuity and economic resilience.
“Tradespeople are the custodians of the physical infrastructure upon which every organization depends,” said Dr. Paul Morgan, global COO of real estate management services at JLL. “We’re at a critical inflection point where demand for skilled trades is accelerating while the available workforce continues to shrink. This systemic crisis threatens how we power our data centers, cool our laboratories, secure our manufacturing facilities, and maintain the spaces where millions of Americans work every day. We risk operational disruptions that will ripple through the entire economy, affecting everything from construction timelines to energy costs to building safety. The window to act is now, before the gap becomes insurmountable.”
The Million-Job Gap Meets the Modern Building Imperative
With 53% of U.S. commercial building stock delivered before 1990, the need to modernize aging assets has never been greater. Meanwhile, projected employment growth for key trades significantly outpaces the national average: Electrician positions are expected to grow 9.5% through 2034—triple the 3.1% average for all occupations—while HVAC technicians face 8.1% growth over the same period.
The supply-demand imbalance has reached crisis proportions. Last year alone, nearly 600,000 jobs were posted for major skilled trades positions in the United States, while only about 150,000 new workers entered the labor pool through apprenticeship programs. According to U.S. Department of Education estimates, for every five workers who retire from construction, manufacturing, and other skilled trades sectors, only two replacements enter the workforce.
