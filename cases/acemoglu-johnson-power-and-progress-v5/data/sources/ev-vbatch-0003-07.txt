Since its launch, Tech Policy Press has covered the findings of the annual Freedom on the Net report from Freedom House, a nonprofit organization founded in 1941 to advance democracy and human rights. As we noted last year , it is typically grim reading, a chronicle of governments suppressing dissent, people arrested for nonviolent expression, tallies of those attacked or killed for their online activities, and other threats to internet freedom. 
It should come as no surprise to our readers that the topline results in this year’s report correspond to more of the same, as authoritarianism continues its advance across the planet. Freedom House finds that “Global internet freedom declined for the 16th consecutive year.” Among the most significant developments was Iran, which, amid demonstrations that rocked the country earlier this year and the subsequent United States and Israeli military strikes on the country, “imposed the longest nationwide internet shutdown Freedom on the Net has ever recorded.” In Russia, the Putin regime “consolidated control over internet service providers (ISPs),” further restricting internet access and introducing new censorship measures. 
But the problem goes far beyond these worst offenders: only 9.1% of the world’s internet users live in countries that Freedom on the Net considers “free.” The US remains in that increasingly lonely category, but it has seen its standings decline substantially over the years. Owing to mass surveillance of online activists by federal authorities, prosecution of journalists who reported on protests against immigration enforcement activity in Minnesota, and “steeper penalties on people for their online activities” in various parts of the country, the US scores 73 on the 100-point scale, just four points from the “partly free” category.

Age checks and AI raise new concerns 
One prominent trend driving concern over internet freedom, according to Freedom on the Net, is the global proliferation of measures to restrict children and teens from accessing certain online content. (Tech Policy Press is tracking this trend in its Global Social Media Age Restriction Tracker. ) These policies often come with requirements for age assurance, which the report says “has become a pressing rights and cybersecurity issue.” Pointing to Turkey and China, Freedom on the Net says these laws “open a new vector for abuse, with identification requirements enabling bad actors and repressive governments to create a footprint of people’s online activities.” 
The report warns that age checks relying on facial scans, government-issued identification or other sensitive personal data can threaten privacy and anonymity. It also points to evidence that children have continued to access social media despite new restrictions.
