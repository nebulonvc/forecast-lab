Making productivity growth work for people
In a statement to the Development Committee and International Monetary and Financial Committee at the 2026 Annual Meetings, ILO Director-General Gilbert F. Houngbo calls for policies that ensure productivity gains, including those generated by AI, are more widely shared, strengthening workers’ incomes, job quality and social protection while fostering inclusive and resilient economies.

Headline labour market indicators have remained broadly stable, but this stability masks underlying fragilities. Shocks such as the spillovers from the conflict in the Middle East are being absorbed not only through unemployment, but also through weaker hiring, lower real incomes, persistent informality and deteriorating job quality and working conditions.
Productivity gains are not reliably reaching workers. Globally, the gap between productivity and workers' incomes remains substantial, especially in low-income countries. While this gap narrowed for a time, further convergence has since been limited, and in high-income countries it has widened.
Productivity growth itself has been weakest where it is most needed. Low-income countries continue to face large and persistent productivity gaps, limiting the scope for the sustained improvements in living standards and jobs.
Market concentration may be one factor limiting the broad sharing of productivity gains. SMEs, where a large share of employment is concentrated, often face persistent productivity gaps and disadvantages in access to finance, technology and markets, while economic activity and capital are increasingly concentrated among a relatively small number of large firms, including in the technology sector. At the same time, limited worker bargaining power, weak collective bargaining coverage and labour market imbalances can constrain wage growth, particularly for women, young people and rural workers. Artificial intelligence could reinforce some of these dynamics, and in many developing countries the adjustment pressures associated with AI may emerge before its productivity benefits are fully realized.
These imbalances are macro-critical. The prevalence of low-pay, low-productivity jobs can constrain skills development, domestic demand and the tax base, while leaving households more vulnerable to shocks. Where the gains from growth are not broadly shared, trust in institutions and social cohesion can also come under pressure, making sustained reform more difficult. Strong labour market institutions are therefore part of the foundations of resilient and inclusive growth.
Policy can help ensure that the gains from economic transformation reach a wider share of society. Respect for rights at work, collective bargaining and adequate minimum wages, competition and labour market policies that widen workers' options, support for SMEs to access finance and technology, and sustainably financed social protection can all help translate productivity growth into higher and more widely distributed living standards, while preserving incentives for innovation and investment.
The international financial institutions have an important role to play. The distribution of productivity gains, evolving market structures and job quality merit greater attention in macroeconomic analysis, policy advice and, where relevant, programme design. Greater attention to rights at work and labour market institutions can also help ensure that reforms strengthen, rather than weaken, the foundations of inclusive growth. The World Bank Group's renewed focus on jobs provides a real opportunity to support jobs that raise productivity, income and skills, and continued ILO–World Bank Group collaboration on jobs measurement and on integrating job quality into development metrics is a valuable step in this direction.
Ultimately, economic stability and social justice are closely connected. Productivity growth that translates into better incomes, jobs that allow people to learn and advance, and institutions that give workers and enterprises a voice are important foundations for resilient economies and inclusive societies.

Productivity and its Distribution
Productivity growth is the foundation of rising living standards, but its benefits depend on where productivity gains occur and on how they are distributed between capital and labour, among enterprises, and among workers.
Global labour productivity has risen over the past two decades, but progress has been highly uneven. Productivity growth has been strongest in middle-income economies, driven to a significant extent by China and India, while many low-income countries continue to face large and persistent productivity gaps. Closing this gap requires sustained investment in infrastructure, skills and productive capacity, and the fiscal space and development finance to support it.
The stakes are high, because productivity growth remains a powerful driver of better livelihoods. But whether productivity gains translate into higher workers' incomes depends on how those gains are shared. Large inequalities in labour income persist in many economies, and the transmission of productivity gains into pay has weakened in some countries. Without appropriate policies, these patterns could become more entrenched as economies develop.
Market concentration, SMEs and artificial intelligence
The weak transmission of productivity gains to workers reflects in part the distribution of employment across firms. A growing body of evidence, from high-income countries and increasingly from developing economies in Latin America and Asia, shows that where workers have limited employment options, labour market concentration and weaker bargaining conditions can constrain wage growth. ILO analysis covering more than 40 countries
also points to a relationship between employment concentration and wage inequality, with particularly important implications for developing economies. Women, young workers and rural workers tend to be more exposed, so these imbalances can compound existing inequalities. Strong labour market institutions, including collective bargaining and adequately set minimum wages, can help mitigate these effects.
The structure of enterprises matters as well. MSMEs account for a large share of employment worldwide, but often face persistent gaps in productivity and access to finance and technology. At the same time, economic activity and capital are becoming more concentrated among a relatively small number of large firms, including in the technology sector. This can make it harder for smaller firms and workers to share fully in productivity gains.
Artificial intelligence could reinforce some of these dynamics. Around one quarter of global employment is in occupations exposed to generative AI, with higher exposure among women in high-income countries. The evidence so far suggests that AI is more likely to transform tasks and jobs than eliminate them on a large scale, but its benefits and adjustment costs may be unevenly distributed. This means that disruptive effects may emerge faster than productivity gains, potentially widening inequalities within and between countries. Smaller enterprises may face similar constraints where access to finance, digital infrastructure and skills is limited.
