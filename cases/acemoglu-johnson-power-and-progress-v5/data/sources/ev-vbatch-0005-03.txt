There’s no denying the widespread worry about the career prospects of new college graduates. Unemployment among recent grads was 5.6 percent in the second quarter of 2026 . That’s still lower than the 7.2 percent rate for workers ages 22 to 27 without college degrees, but the gap is close to its narrowest historical level. The rise of generative AI, along with the earlier shift to remote work, has prompted a rush of questions about the changing job market, what skills employers will value in the future, and how higher education can stay relevant. Critics of the push to expand college access (which they dubbed the “college for all” movement) say it was always guilty of utopian overreach and point to the underemployment data, and uneven graduate earnings, as vindication. They’re doubling down on calls for more college alternatives, from apprenticeships and trade schools to short-term credentials.
In fact, the magnitude and significance of underemployment are vastly overstated. The Strada Education Foundation/Burning Glass Institute study’s conclusion that 52 percent of new bachelor’s degree earners are underemployed—and 45 percent remain so a decade out—has a glaring, mostly overlooked gap. It limits its analysis to college graduates who stopped their education after earning an undergraduate credential; it doesn’t include any bachelor’s degree holders who went on to earn graduate or professional degrees.
As it turns out, that’s a huge number of individuals: 38 percent of all college grads. In other words, the dramatic 10-year underemployment figures leave out nearly four in 10 undergraduate degree earners, including the lawyers, doctors, and MBAs whose bachelor’s degrees opened the door to their careers. Part of the value of an undergraduate degree is that it serves as a building block, a prerequisite for advanced study that often leads to sought-after professions. The omission of this large group was disclosed but given little emphasis in the Strada/Burning Glass study. Its executive summary states, without caveats: “Even a decade after graduation, 45 percent of graduates are underemployed.”
