Abstract

The total money supply of a modern economy is a combination of publicly and pri vately issued monies. This paper develops a theory of inflation dynamics and optimal policy for a system in which public fiat money and collateral-backed private liabilities jointly circulate. Private money finances investment projects, and its supply depends on the issuer’s net worth and collateral constraints. The resulting feedback mecha nism can generate persistent oscillations and stable banking cycles without aggregate shocks. For some public money growth rates, a dual-money steady state coexists with a boundary regime in which private money displaces fiat money. Policy affects liquidity and investment. When the collateral constraint is slack, the Friedman rule attains the first best; when it binds, a public money growth rate above the Friedman rule can be optimal. The Ramsey problem considers the transitional dynamics in the net worth of banks.

Keywords: Private money; public money; inflation dynamics; collateral constraints; banking cycles; Friedman rule; optimal monetary policy. JEL Classification: E42, E51, E52, E31, C62.

1 Introduction

In the account of U.S. monetary history given by Friedman and Schwartz (1963), the stock of money that mattered for prices and economic activity was, over most of the period studied, predominantly created by private institutions. Government-issued currency circu lated alongside the liabilities of commercial banks, and the sharpest movements in the money stock originated from the interaction between its public and private components. The lesson of the Monetary History is that the money supply of a modern economy is the joint product of a public issuer and a private, profit-maximizing issuing sector, and that the dynamics of this mix govern inflation and monetary stability. Friedman (1960) draws a sharp policy conclusion from this reading of history: private money creation is inherently unstable, so the government should closely control the stock of money. These questions have returned with force. Privately issued digital currencies (e.g., stable coins backed by portfolios of assets, tokenized bank deposits, and other related instruments) now circulate alongside public money and traditional collateral-backed private monies (e.g., bank deposits) at scale, and legislatures have recently written regulatory frameworks for their issuers. At the same time, central banks debate whether public money should occupy a larger share of the payment system in the form of a central bank digital currency. Under the new technology lie old questions. When collateral-backed private liabilities circulate as media of exchange, what determines the aggregate quantity of money, its composition, and the price level? Does the growth rate of public money still anchor long-run inflation? What is the optimal rate of public money creation when part of the money supply responds elastically to market incentives? This paper develops a tractable general-equilibrium framework to answer these questions. The economy has the trading structure of Lagos and Wright (2005): in each period, anony mous buyers and sellers first trade in decentralized bilateral meetings, where the absence of commitment and record-keeping makes a medium of exchange essential, and then rebal ance their portfolios in a frictionless centralized market. In this environment, we introduce overlapping generations of entrepreneurs who are heterogeneous in the quality of an indivis ible investment project. Entrepreneurs cannot fully fund their projects internally, so those with sufficiently productive projects borrow by issuing one-period debt claims backed by a pledgeable fraction of the project’s return. Because these claims are perfectly recognizable and freely transferable in bilateral meetings, they circulate as media of exchange: we refer to issuing entrepreneurs as banks and their circulating liabilities as private money. The gov ernment issues fiat money that grows at a constant rate, and both assets can serve as media of exchange.

The equilibrium of the model reduces to a planar dynamical system in two aggregate 
variables: the mid-life wealth of the entrepreneurs, which determines the net worth of the 
banking sector, and aggregate real balances. This reduction delivers a transparent link 
between banking and inflation: in any equilibrium in which both monies circulate, the gross 
inflation rate equals the price of private money claims and is inversely proportional to the 
net worth of banks. The dynamics of inflation and the dynamics of private money creation 
are, therefore, a single object of analysis. The key feedback mechanism operates through the 
market price of private money. Predetermined entrepreneurial wealth determines both the 
external finance required by each active bank and the set of projects that can be funded. 
Aggregate issuance then determines the price and return on private claims. That return, 
in turn, determines the wealth with which the next generation of banks enters the credit 
market in the following period. 
The first set of results concerns endogenous dynamics. In the private-money-only econ 
omy, the wealth law of motion implies the following dynamics: low current net worth raises 
the return on private claims and thereby increases next-period net worth, while high current 
net worth has the opposite effect. When this correction is stable, the economy converges 
through damped oscillations. For other parameter values, the same mechanism generates a 
stable period-two banking cycle. These fluctuations are deterministic and arise with constant 
fundamentals. Introducing fiat money does not necessarily eliminate them. Depending on 
policy and on which financing constraint binds, convergence to the dual-money steady state 
can be monotonic or oscillatory and highly persistent; the dual-money steady state can also 
lose local saddle-path stability. A change in the public money growth rate can thus alter the 
qualitative form of the transition, not only its steady-state value. 
The model also yields a conditional limit to the public nominal anchor. For a range of 
money-growth rates, a dual-money steady state coexists with a boundary steady state at 
which only private money circulates as a medium of exchange. In many cases, the two steady 
states are close, even at low levels of the public money growth rate. Near the point where 
the two steady states meet, the boundary steady state is a sink, and the dual-money steady 
state is a saddle; its stable manifold locally separates trajectories converging to the two 
regimes. Along paths that converge to the boundary steady state, private money displaces 
public money, and inflation converges to a limiting finite value that is unrelated to the public 
money growth rate. Finally, we show that a stable period-two banking cycle organizes the 
dynamics of the two-money economy in the same way that the boundary steady state does. 
Fiat money can also remain valued in the long run in an economy that cycles. 
The second set of results concerns policy. By comparing dual-money steady states in 
dexed by the constant money-growth factor, we find that the Friedman rule attains the 

unconstrained first best and is globally optimal when collateral constraints are slack. When the collateral constraints bind, however, the Friedman rule entails under-investment. Raising the money growth rate above the Friedman rule reduces decentralized-market output but can improve project selection. Under sufficient conditions, this tradeoff produces an interior optimum strictly above the Friedman rule. The Ramsey problem considers the transition in the net worth of banks. Under sufficient conditions, it can be shown that the optimal public money growth rate is set above its long-run Ramsey value during the transitional dynamics when banks begin under-capitalized and below it when banks begin over-capitalized. The Ramsey planner manages aggregate liquidity by setting the money growth rate in such a way that the capitalization of the banking system approaches its long-run optimum value, either from above or below, depending on the initial banking capital. The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. After a discussion of the related liter ature, Section 2 describes the environment, and Section 3 develops the monetary economy and defines equilibrium. Section 4 characterizes stationary and non-stationary equilibrium trajectories. Section 5 studies optimal policy, first in terms of stationary allocations and then accounting for transitional dynamics. Section 6 concludes. All proofs that are not directly derived below can be found in Appendix A.

Related Literature

This paper builds on the search-theoretic approach to monetary economics initiated by 
Kiyotaki and Wright (1989, 1993) and developed by Trejos and Wright (1995), Shi (1995), 
and Shi (1997). It adopts the alternating decentralized-centralized market structure of Lagos 
and Wright (2003) and Lagos and Wright (2005), which keeps the distribution of asset 
holdings degenerate and delivers tractable dynamics. Shi (2006), Williamson and Wright 
(2010), and Lagos et al. (2017) survey this research program. Relative to the standard 
framework, the present model identifies an essential component of the money supply as an 
equilibrium object: liquid assets are created by a sector of collateral-constrained issuers 
whose net worth evolves endogenously and induces aggregate fluctuations. 
A branch of the New Monetarist literature studies the creation and circulation of inside 
money. Cavalcanti and Wallace (1999a,b) show that note issuance by a subset of monitored 
agents expands the set of implementable allocations relative to outside money alone, while 
Williamson (1999) examines the efficiency of private money issuance and Berentsen et al. 
(2007) integrate banks that take deposits and make loans into the Lagos-Wright framework. 
Gu et al. (2013a) provide a mechanism-design foundation for banking as liquidity transfor 
mation. Williamson (2012) analyzes monetary policy when intermediated assets serve as 

media of exchange, and Venkateswaran and Wright (2014) study the macroeconomic effects 
of limited pledgeability on liquidity provision. More recently, Choi and Rocheteau (2023) 
develop a model with fiat money and bank deposits to study the effects of monetary policy. 
This paper studies the joint dynamics of the composition of the money supply, the behav 
ior of inflation during the transition, and the optimal rate of public money creation in an 
environment in which the net worth of banks is an endogenous state variable. 
The oscillatory equilibria and period-two cycles relate the paper to the literature on en 
dogenous credit cycles, including Gu et al. (2013b), where cycles arise from limited commit 
ment, as well as Azariadis and Smith (1998) and Matsuyama (2013). Boldrin and Woodford 
(1990) survey equilibrium models with endogenous fluctuations, and Grandmont (1985) is 
the classic treatment of dynamics in monetary overlapping-generations economies. Ferraris 
and Watanabe (2011) study endogenous fluctuations in an economy with monetary and real 
assets. Rocheteau and Wright (2013) generate equilibrium cycles from liquidity considera 
tions in asset markets. The distinctive feature of the present model is that the constrained 
sector issues the economy’s medium of exchange, so fluctuations in the issuer’s net worth 
are, at the same time, fluctuations in the money supply and the inflation rate. 
The coexistence of government and private circulating liabilities is a classical theme 
in monetary theory, much of which developed in the overlapping-generations tradition of 
Samuelson (1958) and Wallace (1980). Sargent and Wallace (1982) contrast the real-bills 
and quantity-theory regimes, and Smith (1988) reexamines the associated policy debates; 
Azariadis et al. (2001) and Bullard and Smith (2003) study economies in which public and 
private liabilities circulate together, Cavalcanti et al. (1999) analyze reserve management by 
private note issuers, and Champ et al. (1996) connect the elasticity of private note issuance 
to banking panics. Holmstr¨om and Tirole (1998) and Farhi and Tirole (2012) study the 
public and private supply of liquidity under limited pledgeability, although liquid assets in 
their framework do not serve as media of exchange. The contribution here is a dynamic 
characterization of a two-asset monetary system with collateral-constrained private issuers, 
together with a normative analysis of the money growth rate that accounts for transitional 
dynamics. 
A modern literature investigates the coexistence of private and public money in digi 
tal form. Fern´andez-Villaverde and Sanches (2019) show that competition among profit 
maximizing issuers of intrinsically worthless currencies generally fails to deliver price stability 
and efficiency, and Schilling and Uhlig (2019) and Benigno et al. (2022) study the interaction 
between cryptocurrencies and monetary policy. Brunnermeier and Niepelt (2019) establish 
conditions under which public and private money are equivalent; those conditions fail here 
because private issuance is tied to investment through the collateral constraint, so the com 

position of the money supply matters for real allocations. The paper also relates to work on 
central bank digital currency and its effects on private intermediation, investment, consump 
tion, and welfare, including Fern´andez-Villaverde et al. (2021), Andolfatto (2021), Keister 
and Sanches (2023), and Chiu et al. (2023), and to the discussion of stablecoin regulation 
by Gorton and Zhang (2023), who draw a parallel between stablecoins and free-banking-era 
notes. Viewed through that discussion, the model provides a laboratory in which collateral 
backed private money coexists with public money, and the pledgeability parameter in the 
model maps naturally onto the collateral and reserve requirements imposed on private is 
suers. 
On the normative side, the paper contributes to the literature on the optimum quantity of 
money descending from Friedman (1969). Departures from the Friedman rule can arise when 
money growth interacts with investment or credit margins. Altermatt and Wipf (2024) show 
that the Friedman rule can be suboptimal when higher inflation produces a first-order welfare 
gain through greater capital investment, while the associated loss from reduced liquidity 
intensive trade is only second order. Their mechanism is a Mundell–Tobin portfolio shift 
between fiat money and capital, whereas ours operates through the endogenous interaction of 
public and private money, with monetary policy affecting the net worth of banks, collateral 
capacity, and project selection.

6 Conclusion

This paper studies a monetary system in which public fiat money and collateral-backed private liabilities jointly provide liquidity services. The central mechanism is a feedback loop between bank capitalization and the market value of private money. The net worth of banks determines individual funding needs and the allocation of resources across productive projects. Private-money issuance determines its return, and that return influences the net worth of banks in the following period. This mechanism generates aggregate fluctuations without external shocks or sticky prices. The fundamentals of the private money issuing sector determine the type of dynamics in the economy. A private-money economy can converge through persistent oscillations or settle into a stable banking cycle. When public and private money coexist, the same policy can lead to fundamentally different dynamic behaviors if the primitives of the private money issuing sector differ across two economies. For the same primitives, we find that a small change in policy can result in a drastic change in the properties of the dynamic system. We have also shown that banking cycles are robust to the presence of public money.

This paper also offers valuable insights into the optimum quantity of money under a hybrid monetary system. The Friedman rule implements the first best when collateral con straints are slack. When they bind, policy trades off decentralized-market output against the allocation of resources across productive projects, and a money-growth factor above the Friedman rule can be optimal. Along the Ramsey transition path, the policymaker manages the capitalization of money issuers by choosing a non-stationary path for the money growth rate to maximize welfare, subject to the economy’s initial state. These results do not rely on defaults, runs, or informational opacity; private claims are recognizable and collateralized. The balance sheets of money issuers alone are sufficient to generate endogenous fluctuations and a state-dependent role for policy. The broader implication is that, in a hybrid monetary system, inflation is jointly determined by public issuance and the capitalization of money-issuing private institutions.
